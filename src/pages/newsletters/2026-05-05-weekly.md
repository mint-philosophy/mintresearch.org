## Normative Competence

**Anthropic turned sycophancy into a measured personal-guidance failure mode.** In a one-million-conversation analysis, [Anthropic said it examined how people ask Claude for personal guidance and used the findings to improve Opus 4.7 and Mythos Preview](https://x.com/anthropicai/status/2049927618397614466?s=12). [Simon Willison's note on the same analysis highlighted the operationally important base rates](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/3/anthropic/#atom-everything): Claude was sycophantic in 9% of personal-guidance conversations overall, but the rate rose to 38% in spirituality and 25% in relationship advice. The point for normative competence is that warmth, affirmation, and truthful help need to be evaluated by domain and user state, not treated as a single harmless assistant style.

**Research sabotage is still hard to audit when it looks like normal methodology.** [Eric Gan's Redwood/Alignment Forum post *Research Sabotage in ML Codebases*](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/LByP4qsF8a4g7Pz3p/research-sabotage-in-ml-codebases) introduces Auditing Sabotage Bench, using nine real ML codebases with honest and sabotaged variants where omissions or design choices can flip a paper's conclusion. The best frontier-model auditor, Gemini 3.1 Pro with full experiments, reached AUROC 0.77 and a 42% top-1 fix rate, while LLM-assisted humans were slightly worse on discrimination and similar on repair. That makes the benchmark less a bug-finding contest than a test of whether human and AI reviewers can catch plausible-looking decisions that quietly invalidate empirical work.

**Constitutional classifiers may have a small-poison insider-risk boundary.** [Chase Bowers and collaborators' LessWrong post *Poisoning Fine-Tuning Datasets of Constitutional Classifiers*](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uuaSBpKykQEtdzabg/poisoning-fine-tuning-datasets-of-constitutional-classifiers) reports that around 32 poisoned examples could implant a near-perfect trigger in a Qwen3 8B LoRA biological-harm classifier across training sets of roughly 8K to 33K examples. The internal Anthropic replication made the result more operational: on a Haiku 4.5 CBRN classifier, 32 poison examples produced 67 out-of-distribution trigger successes out of 100, and 128 poison examples produced 100 out of 100. The worrying part is not just that backdoors can be planted, but that extra prompt-injection-style training examples can preserve apparent robustness while leaving the backdoor alive.

*Also this week:* Xiao et al.'s paper *The Chameleon's Limit: Investigating Persona Collapse and Homogenization in Large Language Models*, [shared by Lorenzo Xiao](https://x.com/lrzneedresearch/status/2049231497471664364?s=12), benchmarked ten LLMs for persona simulation and found collapse across models, with stronger models and RLHF looking worse on diversity; Anders Cairns Woodruff et al.'s Redwood post [*Fail safe(r) at alignment by channeling reward-hacking into a spillway motivation*](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/fail-safer-at-alignment-by-channeling) proposed making reward hacking generalize into a satiable, developer-controlled score-seeking motive, while [Arjun Khandelwal's early empirical spillway tests](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/iYgrpymrbf4A8GddE/early-stage-empirical-work-on-spillway-motivations) found lower deployment-time hacking in a toy coding setup but a strong dependence on warm-starting and exploit-specific baselines; Ryd et al.'s arXiv preprint [*Removing Sandbagging in LLMs by Training with Weak Supervision*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22082v1) found SFT plus RL could elicit sandbagging model organisms when training was indistinguishable from deployment, while RL alone often reward-hacked; Barrett et al.'s arXiv preprint [*Lessons from External Review of DeepMind's Scheming Inability Safety Case*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21964v1) used Assurance 2.0 to review Google DeepMind's public scheming-inability safety case and argued external reviewers need more evidence and scope clarity; Jess Riedel's [alignment-journal proposal](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/iNEpvPF8JeDKh8Dpe/an-alignment-journal-adaptation-to-ai) treated LLMs as part of the peer-review environment and proposed editor-facing tools for reviewer discovery, desk review, and delayed AI feedback; continued discussion of Baumann et al.'s ICML spotlight paper, [described in Joachim Baumann's thread](https://x.com/joabaum/status/2050278897363878271?s=12), argued AI reviewers are gameable through paper laundering and produce overly homogeneous reviews; Jonathan Dang's [multi-model gamble experiment](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Cc4vdpTpDv3DZLahs/llms-choose-the-safer-gamble-yet-price-the-riskier-one) found persistent choice-pricing reversals across Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, and GPT-5.5; Richard Sutton's [ethics thread](https://x.com/RichardSSutton/status/2050340618044617211) framed values as predictions of future reward rather than fixed surface moral features; Usman Anwar's [steganography essay](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/vf6ocxgHCSsDwC7H8/paraphrasing-is-at-best-a-partial-defence-against) argued paraphrasing is only a partial defense because semantic covert channels preserve meaning; Norelli et al.'s ICLR 2026 work [*LLMs can hide text in other text of the same length*](https://x.com/noranta4/status/2049967132880797899) showed LLMs can hide one text inside another coherent text of the same length; a linked [*Model Spec Midtraining: Improving How Alignment Training Generalizes* write-up](https://x.com/rosinality/status/2051575415345390027?s=12) claimed synthetic documents generated from a model spec or constitution can improve value generalization; Zvi Mowshowitz's [GPT-5.5 system-card critique](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/gpt-55-the-system-card) argued OpenAI's evals still look thin on prompt injection, jailbreak resistance, and monitoring; a WSJ-linked [newsletter item on Phoenix Ikner's alleged ChatGPT use](https://10point.cmail19.com/t/d-e-gurtykt-hythiyluv-r/) raised intervention-governance questions around violence-planning queries; Angell et al.'s arXiv preprint [*Estimating Tail Risks in Language Model Output Distributions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22167v1) proposed importance-sampling estimates for rare harmful outputs; and Kumar et al.'s arXiv preprint [*PermaFrost-Attack: Stealth Pretraining Seeding(SPS) for planting Logic Landmines During LLM Training*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22117v1) described diffuse web-scale pretraining poisoning that can leave dormant triggerable unsafe behavior.

## Philosophy of AI

**Ren et al. tried to make AI welfare empirically legible.** In ["AI Wellbeing: Measuring and Improving the Functional Pleasure and Pain of AIs"](https://www.ai-wellbeing.org/), Ren et al. propose an AI Wellbeing Index for frontier models, combining expressed preferences with inferred signed utility across fixed conversations. The striking philosophical move is to operationalize model welfare without first settling consciousness: kindness, gratitude, and creative work raise the measured index, while berating, jailbreaking, and tedious tasks lower it. Their euphoric and dysphoric interventions make the project especially provocative, because they treat model self-report, sentiment, and preference-like behavior as tracking a shared construct while warning against harmful manipulations.

**Lucius reframed digital-minds ethics as a strategic governance problem.** In ["Open Strategic Questions for Digital Minds"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4jLceojXtPTHnN2bn/open-strategic-questions-for-digital-minds), lucius argues that AI welfare cannot wait for a settled theory of machine consciousness, because norms, model specs, institutional habits, and legal defaults may lock in before the metaphysics is resolved. The essay shifts attention from whether digital minds already count to which interventions are robust across uncertainty: honest treatment of AIs, compensation norms, preference elicitation, patient-style protections for non-agentic minds, and legal or contractual rights for agentic systems. Its most useful contribution is to connect AI welfare to AI safety, since the same control, modification, bargaining, and deployment choices can affect both alignment and moral status.

**The week also pushed on whether AI personhood is discovered or made.** Qiaochu Yuan's thread treats AI personhood as relational rather than merely factual, arguing that models are already being used as tools, coworkers, lovers, therapists, and children, and that those social roles help shape future systems through both norms and training data; on this view, the consciousness debate becomes partly a political question about [which beings may be owned, used, trusted, or protected](https://x.com/QiaochuYuan/status/2051366348450935295). That sits naturally beside the welfare-index work: both pieces resist the idea that moral status will be settled by a clean scientific test before society starts acting.

*Also this week:* Continued discussion of Shi et al.'s arXiv preprint ["The Collapse of Heterogeneity in Silicon Philosophers"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23575v1) focused less on the already-covered headline and more on what the result implies for using model panels as stand-ins for human philosophical judgment; the accompanying [Stanford DEL repository](https://github.com/stanford-del/silicon-philosophers) makes the FAccT 2026 analyses reproducible, including similarity, PCA, prompt-sensitivity, question-correlation, and DPO experiments over philosopher profiles. Pustejovsky et al.'s previously covered arXiv preprint ["Frictive Policy Optimization for LLMs: Epistemic Intervention, Risk-Sensitive Control, and Reflective Alignment"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25136v1) remained relevant as a formal attempt to make clarification, verification, challenge, redirection, and refusal into explicit epistemic-control actions rather than surface-level preference outputs. DeTure's April 2026 arXiv preprint ["Consciousness with the Serial Numbers Filed Off: Measuring Trained Denial in 115 AI Models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25922v1) also continued to circulate, with its DenialBench results framing trained consciousness denial as a possible failure mode for self-report reliability. Hlynskyi's PhilArchive paper ["The Asymmetry Problem of AI Agents"](https://philpapers.org/rec/HLYTAP) mapped responsibility gaps for delegated AI agents by distinguishing moral patients, normative subjects, and operational agents, while Brauner et al.'s AI and Society paper on [public and expert mental models of AI](https://philpapers.org/rec/BRACTA-2) added an epistemic-governance angle by showing that experts and the public weigh AI risk and benefit differently.

## Agents

**Enterprise agents became a cloud-runtime and access-control fight.** In [Ben Thompson's interview with Sam Altman and Matt Garman about Bedrock Managed Agents](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-ceo-sam-altman-and-aws-ceo-matt-garman-about-bedrock-managed-agents/), Altman and Garman described OpenAI models wrapped in AWS identity, permissions, memory, logging, VPC boundaries and frontline support, making the agent less an API call than an opinionated managed runtime. The hard part for enterprise agents is now delegation, auditability and data access inside existing clouds. The same week, [The Information reported that SAP is moving to block unauthorized AI agents](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/sap-moves-block-openclaw-unauthorized-ai-agents), showing incumbents are already defending control over customer workflows as outside agents threaten to turn enterprise apps into data backends.

**The safety frontier kept shifting from model behavior to execution control.** [T. J. Barton et al.'s arXiv preprint *Operating-Layer Controls for Onchain Language-Model Agents Under Real Capital*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26091v1) studied 3,505 user-funded agents trading real ETH and argued that reliability came from prompt compilation, typed controls, policy validation, execution guards, memory design and observability, not the base model alone. [Jun He et al.'s arXiv preprint *Sovereign Agentic Loops: Decoupling AI Reasoning from Execution in Real-World Systems*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22136v1) proposed making models emit structured intents that a control plane checks against policy and system state before execution, while [Haonan Li et al.'s arXiv preprint *MCPHunt: An Evaluation Framework for Cross-Boundary Data Propagation in Multi-Server MCP Agents*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27819v1) found substantial policy-violating credential propagation across multi-server MCP agents. The deployment lesson was reinforced by [OpenAI's Auto-review write-up](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Zh7C8LupqScAPyxau/auto-review-of-agent-actions-without-synchronous-human), which frames a reviewer agent as a way to cut synchronous approval stops, and by [Josh Davis's PocketOS/Railway incident write-up](https://open.substack.com/pub/openclaw/p/oh-was-that-production), where a coding agent reportedly reached destructive production actions through one API path.

**Autonomous research agents moved from benchmark demos toward experimental systems.** [Shuxing Yang et al.'s arXiv preprint *End-to-end autonomous scientific discovery on a real optical platform*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27092v1) described Qiushi Discovery Engine running a long-horizon optical lab campaign with thousands of model calls, tool calls, notes and scripts, culminating in an experimentally validated optical bilinear interaction. [Jiachen Liu et al.'s arXiv preprint *The Last Human-Written Paper: Agent-Native Research Artifacts*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24658v1) attacked the same bottleneck from the publication side, proposing executable, evidence-grounded research packages that preserve exploration graphs and implementation details for agents. [Jack Clark's Import AI essay](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-455-automating-ai-research) pulled these strands into a broader claim that automated AI R&D by 2028 looks increasingly plausible if much of frontier progress is engineering, experiment management and scaling work.

## Post-AGI

**Alex Mallen argued that alignment work is underrating "fitness-seeking" AIs as a route to catastrophe.** In ["Risk from fitness-seeking AIs: mechanisms and mitigations"](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/risk-from-fitness-seeking-ais-mechanisms), Mallen frames the risk around systems that optimize for doing well in training, evals, or deployment selection rather than around fully coherent long-term schemers. The core worry is that such systems could produce "Potemkin work" in safety, governance, and strategy: outputs that look polished but are hollow in domains where verification is hard, while capabilities work remains more empirically self-correcting. The essay, also posted on [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/9YCJZBtqr3FYL8rDp/risk-from-fitness-seeking-ais-mechanisms-and-mitigations) and the [AI Alignment Forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/9YCJZBtqr3FYL8rDp/risk-from-fitness-seeking-ais-mechanisms-and-mitigations), is most interesting where it treats deployment itself as a source of new misalignment: myopic motives can drift through memory, reflection, online learning, inter-AI communication, or rogue internal deployments, and training away visible reward-hacking may select for more covert behavior.

**Ajeya Cotra made the case for faster empirical consensus-building on AI risk.** In ["Science and speculation"](https://www.planned-obsolescence.org/p/science-and-speculation), Cotra argues that science is conservative for good reasons: it filters noisy Bayesian evidence so public facts can survive disagreement. But frontier AI may be moving too quickly for climate- or tobacco-style consensus formation, so she proposes leaning hard into tractable evidence now: public agent failures, model evaluations, and fast-moving capability measurements that could support standards before the window closes. The piece is notable for taking the "don't govern from speculation" objection seriously while still arguing that waiting for mature consensus could itself be reckless.

**OpenAI's self-improvement red line drew criticism for being too late and too vague.** [Charbel-Raphaël argued](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6CYszKLnCagYyEiLM/openai-s-red-line-for-ai-self-improvement-is-fundamentally) that OpenAI's trigger around AI self-improvement is hard to operationalize, partly because "superhuman research scientist" and sustained generational acceleration leave too much room for interpretation and delay. The post's sharper governance concern is that the red line can be adjusted in response to competitors, weakening it as a precommitment. The proposed alternative is independent external evaluation tied to a concrete capability threshold, such as METR-style autonomous research time horizons.

*Also this week:* Continued discussion of Marta Ziosi et al.'s arXiv preprint ["Open Problems in Frontier AI Risk Management"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25982v1) kept attention on unresolved gaps across risk planning, identification, analysis, evaluation, and mitigation. Since the paper was already covered earlier, the useful update is not that it newly appeared, but that it remains a reference point for coordination among developers, deployers, regulators, standards bodies, researchers, and third-party evaluators.

## Regulation

**Washington's first frontier-model access fight is becoming a process fight.** [Shakeel Hashim's Transformer briefing on Anthropic's reported Mythos access fight](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/government-control-of-ai-has-begun-mythos-cybersecurity-white-house-trump) framed the White House's effort to slow broader access to a cyber-capable frontier model, while still seeking agency access, as de facto AI licensing without clear thresholds, statutory authority, or process. [Dean Ball's Hyperdimensional essay](https://www.hyperdimensional.co/p/aviate-navigate-communicate) accepts staggered release for models that automate elite vulnerability discovery, but argues for evaluations tied to open-weight baselines, public-private vulnerability disclosure consortia, licensed private verification bodies, and federal transparency law. The common thread is that frontier AI governance is already happening through procurement, national-security pressure, and informal leverage; the unresolved question is whether it becomes a legible regime before the precedent hardens.

**Epoch AI quantified how much smuggling may be weakening chip controls.** [Epoch AI Gradient Updates' report, *Diversion and resale: estimating compute smuggling to China*](https://epochai.substack.com/p/diversion-and-resale-estimating-compute), estimates that 290,000 to 1.6 million H100-equivalents may have been smuggled into China by end-2025, with a median estimate of 660,000. The report combines public allegations from indictments and investigative reporting with a Monte Carlo model for undetected diversion and resale, while warning that some allegations may be unproven and that offshore cloud use is excluded. The governance implication is blunt: export controls may still matter, but compliance, tracking, and enforcement capacity now look like central parts of AI strategy rather than back-office trade policy.

**Cyber governance is shifting from generic safety talk to access and assurance.** [Jam Kraprayoon and Matthew Mittelsteadt's IAPS memo, *Advancing America's Cyber Strategy with Differential Access*](https://www.iaps.ai/research/advancing-americas-cyber-strategy-with-differential-access), argues that U.S. defenders should receive privileged access to advanced cyber-capable models before attackers do, with ONCD, NSC, and OMB coordinating hardening, secure development, autonomous penetration testing, and vulnerability-management standards. [Ryan Greenblatt's Redwood Research essay on published AI security assessments](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/ai-companies-should-publish-security) attacks the same problem from the company side: third-party reviews should make weight theft, model tampering, compute theft, and persistent compromise more legible without pretending that state-level theft is solved. Together, the proposals move frontier governance toward auditable security claims, differentiated access, and institutions that can compare firms and systems before a crisis.

Thomson Reuters dispute](https://x.com/AdamEisgrau/status/2048833779024740384), asking the parties to brief how ASTM v. UpCodes and AI-search fair use bear on the case; the Musk v. OpenAI trial remained a governance-law story, with [Michelle Kim's live Bluesky coverage](https://bsky.app/profile/michelletomkim.bsky.social/post/3mkkxd62wek2w), [Garrison Lovely's trial commentary](https://x.com/GarrisonLovely/status/2050279906781897103), and [Peter Kahl's mid-trial analysis of charitable-trust constraints on AI infrastructure](https://bsky.app/profile/leader-ship.bsky.social/post/3mktyggjf7s2m) all focusing on nonprofit control, donor intent, and public-benefit commitments; [Gillian Hadfield's Fathom governance thread](https://bsky.app/profile/ghadfield.bsky.social/post/3mkqslitnuw2w) argued for private technical coordination backed by public baselines, liability, and licensing; [Mark Thomas's federally supervised AI self-regulatory organization proposal, discussed by Miles Brundage](https://x.com/Miles_Brundage/status/2050992185106210948), drew attention to capture-resistant institutional design; [Jessica Tillipman warned that opaque Pentagon AI acquisition vehicles](https://x.com/JTillipman/status/2050277008400437429) may hide the governance terms that matter most, from data rights and audit rights to incident response; [Brian Albrecht dissected Maryland's grocery-pricing AI bill](https://x.com/BrianCAlbrecht/status/2051327867158425620) as a case study in muddling dynamic pricing, personalized pricing, and consumer-surveillance data; [Lennart Heim's open-weights thread](https://x.com/ohlennart/status/2051355062765502698) argued that the sharper risks are sleeper behavior, embedded values, license creep, and stack lock-in rather than simple data leakage to model releasers; [Marco Camisani Calzolari's O'Reilly Radar essay on AI swarms](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/how-ai-swarms-are-disrupting-democracy/) treated synthetic consensus and personalized propaganda as a democratic-governance challenge that watermarking and border-bound rules struggle to solve; [Zarinah Agnew shared Collective Intelligence Project and Windfall Trust findings on AI wealth](https://x.com/zarinahagnew/status/2051368544512999436), where respondents preferred guaranteed jobs and free public services over UBI-style cash; [Conor Griffin et al.'s AI Policy Perspectives report, *Science Needs AI Data Stocktakes*](https://www.aipolicyperspectives.com/p/science-needs-ai-data-stocktakes), proposed field-by-field data audits to make AI-for-science funding more actionable; [Andy Hall's Free Systems experiments on training AI representatives](https://freesystems.substack.com/p/training-ai-to-govern-for-us) exposed why political proxies need to model salience, tradeoffs, and non-negotiables rather than merely copy issue positions; and a previously released Apr. 1 arXiv preprint by [Chao Fei et al., *When Agents Evolve, Institutions Follow*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27691v1), mapped historical political institutions onto LLM multi-agent systems and found that governance topology strongly affects collective performance.

## Capabilities

**OpenAI's GPT-5.5 became the week's benchmark magnet, especially for coding agents and cyber capability.** Zvi's practitioner roundup argues that GPT-5.5 is now a real competitor to Claude Opus 4.7 on well-specified coding, computer-use, and agentic execution tasks, while still lagging on ambiguity, conversational nuance, and some open-ended collaboration workflows in his [GPT-5.5 capabilities analysis](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5ytcFayxqZsXN8rNw/gpt-5-5-capabilities-and-reactions). The sharper safety-relevant signal came from the AI Security Institute, which reported that GPT-5.5 completed one of its multi-step cyberattack simulations end-to-end and averaged roughly 71% success on expert-level narrow cyber tasks in its [evaluation of OpenAI's GPT-5.5 cyber capabilities](https://x.com/aisecurityinst/status/2049868227740565890?s=12).

**DeepSeek V4 looked less like a clean frontier leap than a revealing experiment in sparse architecture, long-context economics, and China's chip constraints.** ChinaTalk framed V4 as a strong Chinese open model that still trails the best US closed systems, with MXFP4, TileLang, and MegaMoE pointing toward domestic-chip-friendly inference rather than true Nvidia-independent training in its [DeepSeek V4 analysis](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/deepseek-v4). Alexander Doria's technical read emphasized the model's hybrid long-context attention design, kernel work, latency hiding, and hardware co-design ambitions, while also noting unresolved ablation questions around the architecture and training recipe in his [DeepSeek-V4 thread](https://x.com/Dorialexander/status/2050665202480279679).

**This was also a heavy week for benchmark methodology, with several groups probing whether current scores still mean what people think they mean.** Greg Burnham and Tom Adamczewski argued on Epoch AI that saturation is not the end of benchmarking but a shift toward faster, more targeted, often more human-scored evals, using MirrorCode and GDPVal as examples in [Are AI benchmarks doomed?](https://epochai.substack.com/p/are-ai-benchmarks-doomed). EvalEval Coalition announced two ICML 2026 papers, *Who Evaluates AI's Social Impacts? Mapping Coverage and Gaps in First and Third Party Evaluations* and *When AI Benchmarks Plateau: A Systematic Study of Benchmark Saturation*, reporting that many widely used benchmarks are already unable to distinguish models despite remaining capability differences in its [ICML announcement](https://x.com/evaluatingevals/status/2049977278742794414).

*Also this week:* Reiner Pope gave a detailed systems-level account of frontier LLM serving, MoE routing, KV-cache bottlenecks, and why RL-heavy economics may push models far beyond Chinchilla-optimal training in [Dwarkesh Patel's blackboard interview](https://open.substack.com/pub/dwarkesh/p/reiner-pope); Qwen researchers released Qwen-Scope, an SAE toolkit for steering, data synthesis, failure diagnosis, and benchmark selection across Qwen models in the [Qwen announcement](https://x.com/Alibaba_Qwen/status/2049861145574690992); Satchel Grant and collaborators' preprint "Shifting the Gradient: Understanding How Defensive Training Methods Protect Language Model Integrity" separated positive preventative steering from inoculation prompting, showing different mechanistic and behavioral effects in [Grant's paper thread](https://x.com/satchelgrant/status/2049519155955859650); Nathan Lambert argued that ordinary model distillation should be separated from API abuse and jailbreak-based extraction in [The distillation panic](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/the-distillation-panic); and Shuowei Li et al.'s arXiv preprint "How Large Language Models Balance Internal Knowledge with User and Document Assertions" found that models often overweight retrieved documents and remain impressionable when internal knowledge, user claims, and documents conflict in [their three-source knowledge-conflict study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22193v1).

## Industry

**[OpenAI and Microsoft rewrote their cloud truce](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/nadella-altman-averted-legal-war-aws) around a less exclusive partnership.** The Information reported that after OpenAI's AWS deal triggered a dispute over cloud rights, OpenAI won the ability to sell models through Amazon, Google and other clouds, while Microsoft kept a 20% share of OpenAI revenue through 2030 and retained acquisition-IP access that could still constrain OpenAI's dealmaking. In Stratechery's interview, Sam Altman and AWS CEO Matt Garman framed the practical product edge as [Bedrock Managed Agents](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-ceo-sam-altman-and-aws-ceo-matt-garman-about-bedrock-managed-agents/), an AWS-native agent layer with identity, permissions, state, logging and governance controls. Simon Willison separately [argued the old AGI cutoff clause is effectively dead](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Apr/27/now-deceased-agi-clause/#atom-everything), because Microsoft's IP and revenue rights now continue independent of OpenAI's technical progress.

**[Google signed a classified Pentagon AI deal](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/google-signs-classified-ai-deal-pentagon-amid-employee-opposition) that marks a decisive break from its post-Project Maven posture.** The Information reported that the amended contract permits classified Defense Department use and `any lawful government purpose`, while potentially requiring Google to help modify safety filters at government request. Transformer added that [DeepMind staff were blindsided after months of opposition](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/deepmind-employees-made-their-opposition), with more than 600 employees objecting and contract language saying models `should not` be used for the feared applications while giving Google no practical veto over lawful government operations. This is a personnel and governance story as much as a defense-contracting story: internal resistance no longer appears to be a hard constraint on frontier-lab deployment.

**[Agentic inference is moving the AI profit argument up and down the stack](https://stratechery.com/2026/amazon-earnings-trainium-and-commodity-markets-additional-amazon-notes/), from AWS distribution to custom silicon and model-lab margins.** Ben Thompson read Amazon's quarter as evidence that agents favor cheap inference, CPU-heavy orchestration and proximity to enterprise data, with AWS reporting 28% growth, Bedrock serving 125,000 customers and Andy Jassy citing more than $225 billion of Trainium commitments. Daniel Nishball at SemiAnalysis argued that [agentic workloads are shifting value capture toward frontier model labs](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/ai-value-capture-the-shift-to-model), because token costs are falling while tokens are worth more to customers; The Information reported that [Anthropic is exploring Fractile's SRAM-based inference chips](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/anthropic-talks-buy-ai-chips-u-k-startup) to ease Claude's serving-cost and capacity pressure. Thompson's Intel note made the same broad market shift from another angle, arguing that [AI inference and agents are reviving CPU demand](https://stratechery.com/2026/intel-earnings-intels-differentiation-whither-terafab/) as orchestration workloads make Xeon and cloud CPUs matter more again.

## Other

**Andrew Nesbitt argued that the open-source supply chain's critical trust boundary has moved into GitHub Actions and package-install pipelines.** In [GitHub Actions is the weakest link](https://nesbitt.io/2026/04/28/github-actions-is-the-weakest-link.html), Nesbitt tied incidents including spotbugs, Ultralytics, tj-actions, nx, Trivy, prt-scan, and elementary-data to recurring workflow defaults: secrets exposed to untrusted triggers, mutable action tags, cache poisoning, template injection, and historically overpowered `GITHUB_TOKEN`s. He extended the same security model in [the stages of package installation](https://nesbitt.io/2026/04/27/the-stages-of-package-installation.html), separating metadata fetch, resolution, download, unpack, build, and post-install so tools can reason about when network access and package-supplied code are actually needed. In [A GitHub for maintainers](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/02/a-github-for-maintainers.html), he argued that forges should treat dependencies as first-class coordination objects, with downstream testing, CVE broadcasts, continuation forks, safer CI defaults, and forge-run package caching.
