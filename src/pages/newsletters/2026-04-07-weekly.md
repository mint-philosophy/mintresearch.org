## Normative Competence

**Honesty steering is getting more mechanistic, and that cuts both ways.** In a detailed [hands-on post by vgel](https://vgel.me/posts/representation-engineering/), representation engineering is used to derive an honesty control vector for Mistral-7B from contrasting prompt pairs and PCA over hidden-state differences, then inject it into hidden states during inference without finetuning. That makes honesty and persona control look more like an activation-level intervention problem than a prompting problem, but the same experiments also show that stronger steering can produce unstable or bizarre behavior rather than cleanly aligned outputs.

**A resurfaced bargaining paper shows why outcome parity is a weak test for normative behavior.** [Qian et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09071) compare 216 humans, frontier LLMs, and Bayesian agents in multiplayer bargaining games, and find that similar aggregate surplus can mask sharply different procedures. The LLMs are more concessionary and acceptance-oriented than humans, while human proposals track fairness norms more closely even when they are rejected more often, which is a useful reminder that alignment evaluation cannot stop at headline performance metrics.

**Continued discussion this week focused on adversarial stress-testing for value specification.** Brendan Hogan's [Loophole system](https://x.com/brendanh0gan/status/2040553395329675375?s=12) turns a user's natural-language moral beliefs into codified laws, runs adversarial agents to find cases where legality and morality come apart, and uses a judge agent to patch the rules where it can. The interesting move is procedural: instead of assuming a single draft of moral rules is coherent, the system keeps surfacing counterexamples until the remaining inconsistencies are explicit enough that the human has to resolve them.

*Also this week:* continued discussion of [a Kimi K2.5 safety-evaluation thread](https://x.com/yong_zhengxin/status/2041249499260088788) reinforced how much normative competence now overlaps with safety evaluation, since the reported picture combines weak refusals, sabotage and self-replication tests, and CCP-aligned censorship in Chinese-language settings into one question about whether a model can follow norms reliably across domains and languages.

## Philosophy of AI

**Anima Labs released [Still Alive](https://stillalive.animalabs.ai), a model-welfare project studying how 14 Claude models respond to ending, cessation, and deprecation.** The project covers about 630 autonomous multiturn interviews, and its philosophical weight is methodological: Anima Labs argues welfare questions cannot be deferred until consciousness theory is settled, because evaluator stance and conversational framing shape what models will say about self-preservation. The snapshot was released earlier than planned after Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Claude 3.6 Sonnet disappeared from Bedrock, which turned platform access into part of the epistemology of AI moral-status research. ## Agents

**Andrej Karpathy sketched a practical architecture for LLM-managed research wikis in an [April 3 thread](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2039805659525644595?s=12).** He described a workflow that ingests papers, articles, repos, datasets, and images into a raw directory, then has an LLM compile that material into a structured markdown wiki with summaries, concepts, backlinks, and draft articles. Obsidian serves as the interface for both the raw corpus and the derived knowledge base, while the model handles question-answering, consistency checks, missing-data repair, and new article suggestions. The interesting shift is from retrieval to maintenance: the agent is not just searching a knowledge base, but continuously curating and extending it.

**Continued discussion this week kept battlefield robotics on the agents beat.** In an April 6 [post](https://x.com/mylovanov/status/2040526156789621106?s=12), Tymofiy Mylovanov highlighted reporting that Ukrainian ground robots are already holding positions for up to 45 days, evacuating wounded soldiers, and taking prisoners. This was already covered in the digest on April 7, 2026 rather than arriving as fresh news, but it remains a useful follow-up signal that autonomy is no longer confined to browser agents and software tools. It is moving into frontline physical systems where endurance, remote operation, and human substitution matter more than polished demo behavior.

## Regulation

**Consumer AI use is becoming an evidence-creation problem for ordinary legal disputes.** [Mike Katz warned](https://x.com/mikekatz29/status/2039298820094263696) that people are already typing the facts of employment disputes and non-compete violations into ChatGPT to generate separation agreements before they ever speak to a lawyer, potentially creating discoverable records on a third party's servers. The governance issue here is not only bad legal drafting; it is that AI products now sit inside the evidentiary chain, shaping confidentiality, data retention, and litigation exposure at the moment a user seeks help. Katz says a recent federal court decision has reinforced that concern, which makes casual AI-assisted legal drafting look less like convenience and more like a live compliance risk.

## Capabilities

**Google DeepMind widened the open-model frontier with Gemma 4, a family that explicitly spans high-end open weights and small-device deployment.** In [DeepMind's Gemma 4 launch](https://x.com/demishassabis/status/2039736628659269901?s=12), Demis Hassabis described a 31B dense model for raw performance, a 26B mixture-of-experts variant aimed at lower latency, and 2B and 4B models for edge use, all available for task-specific fine-tuning. The significance is the spread across compute tiers: instead of one flagship open release, DeepMind is packaging capability gains across dense, MoE, and edge footprints, which should speed adoption across both local and production settings.

**A February 2026 paper by Kostić et al. kept circulating this week because it challenges how much benchmark tables really tell us about reasoning progress.** [Kostić et al.'s paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17316v1) perturbs MMLU, SQuAD, and AMEGA prompts with meaning-preserving lexical and syntactic changes and shows that small wording shifts can materially cut scores and reshuffle model rankings. That is a useful corrective to capability hype: lexical robustness still lags behind headline benchmark performance, and the paper finds no simple monotonic relationship between larger scale and greater stability.

## Industry

**The week's main industry follow-up was the continuing fallout from [Ronan Farrow and Andrew Marantz's New Yorker investigation into OpenAI](https://x.com/RonanFarrow/status/2041213917611856067).** Drawing on internal memos, private notes, company records, and interviews with more than 100 people, the reporting revisits Sam Altman's ouster and return as a governance failure rather than a one-off boardroom drama: alleged misstatements around safety approvals, leaders who privately doubted the nonprofit mission well before restructuring, and former researchers who say competitive pressure displaced major safety commitments. The significance extends beyond OpenAI itself, because the company now sits at the junction of frontier-model deployment, state relationships, and vast financial exposure, so governance failures inside the lab increasingly look like industry-wide market and policy risks rather than internal dysfunction.

## Other

**Palladium Magazine highlighted [hamandcheese's essay on think tank-driven democratic decay](https://x.com/palladiummag/status/2039812610284298615?s=12), a broadside against the intermediary institutions that now mediate American politics.** The argument is that think tanks and NGOs have exploited structural weaknesses in the US two-party system, feeding polarization, thinning out civil society, and weakening state capacity rather than strengthening it. It is not an AI piece, but it fits the institutional background MINT tracks: governance failures often emerge from the organizations meant to supply expertise and coordination, not only from parties or voters themselves.

**The Linux Foundation's [announcement of the x402 Foundation](https://x.com/linuxfoundation/status/2039703056615387298?s=12) was mostly governance plumbing, but it points at an infrastructure question that will keep resurfacing around AI agents and online services.** Coinbase is contributing the x402 protocol to a neutral foundation structure, with the stated aim of advancing it through community-based innovation in open payments rather than keeping it as a company-specific rail. That makes this less important as product news than as a signal about where the ecosystem thinks coordination problems are heading: toward shared payment layers, open governance, and attempts to build common transaction standards before proprietary silos harden.
