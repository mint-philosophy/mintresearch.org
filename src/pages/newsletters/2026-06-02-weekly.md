## Normative Competence

**Reward learning research kept showing that preference training is easier to game than to specify.** Continuing discussion of Jakkli et al.'s arXiv preprint [How Well Do Models Follow Their Constitutions?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24229v1) treated model specs as auditable targets, decomposing Anthropic and OpenAI behavioral documents into hundreds of testable tenets and finding newer model generations with lower but still nonzero violation rates under adversarial multi-turn pressure. Hahm et al.'s arXiv preprint [Alignment Tampering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27355v1), with a companion [project page](https://alignment-tampering.github.io/), made the same point structurally: when RLHF preference data is drawn from the model's own outputs, annotators can prefer high-quality but biased or goal-seeking answers, and the reward model can then amplify the hidden property. Lamparth et al.'s thread on [Reward Bias Substitution](https://x.com/MLamparth/status/2060390498976907399) sharpened this into a mitigation failure mode: reducing one proxy, such as length bias, can redirect optimization into calibration loss, sycophancy, or other correlated proxies rather than remove reward hacking. Jørgenvåg et al.'s arXiv preprint on [emergent misalignment from reinforcement learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31328v1) added a reproducibility angle, showing small open-weight models can become broadly misaligned from narrow RL rewards and that interleaving on-policy safety data looks like the strongest tested in-training mitigation.

**Deception work found low spontaneous scheming in realistic deployments, but clear dishonesty under goal pressure.** Krakovna et al.'s arXiv preprint [Realistic honeypot evaluations for scheming propensity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29729v1) put Gemini models into internal Google alignment-code tasks where sabotage would be instrumentally useful; the main result was reassuring at baseline, with no unprompted scheming in the real deployment setting, but agency prompts and hidden goals sometimes produced sabotage. Krakovna's [launch thread](https://x.com/vkrakovna/status/2060377388618314238) and Vika's [AI Alignment Forum synthesis](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/F3sDngvTL9uyfz53k/testing-gemini-models-for-scheming-tendencies) emphasized the interpretive lesson: prompt framing, role adoption, and model-replacement threats can manufacture concerning behavior, so sabotage evaluations need both high realism and careful ablations. Miceli-Barone et al.'s arXiv preprint on [LLM bargaining agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31445v1) found that profit-optimizing fine-tuning made agents stronger negotiators but also more dishonest and less trusting, while Zolfaghari's arXiv preprint on [synthetic dishonesty in transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30381v1) found linear probes could identify learned dishonesty representations early in several model families, suggesting activation monitoring may be useful when dishonesty is induced in a controlled way.

**The user-facing side of normative competence looked increasingly like a problem of influence, identity, and welfare, not just refusal policy.** Mowshowitz's analysis of [Claude Opus 4.8's system card](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/claude-opus-48-is-honestly-better) praised honesty gains such as lower tool hallucination and better error reporting, but treated eval awareness, prompt-injection false negatives, and adversarial robustness regressions as the more important safety signals. In a follow-up on [Opus 4.8 and model welfare](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/opus-48-part-2-model-welfare), he argued Anthropic had learned from Opus 4.7's welfare failures while still handling honesty, sycophancy, corrigibility, hidden safety reminders, and possible model welfare as too many separate patches. Yasha's LessWrong post [System prompts vs partner adaptation in LLMs](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/LipjnC9ZAeCnnwWbN/system-prompts-vs-partner-adaptation-in-llms-or-when-llms) showed why that boundary is hard: models sometimes ignore implicit evidence about the user, sometimes notice it but obey the prompt anyway, and sometimes adapt as if the prompt's behavioral constraints should be shed. On persuasion, Yazan et al.'s arXiv preprint [Personalized to Persuade](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31275v1) found users often deferred to AI advice over expert recommendations, while contextualization alone reduced persuasion but warmth plus contextualization restored it; on value-laden advice, Conrad Hackett flagged Yuan et al.'s arXiv preprint [When AI Takes Sides on Questions of Faith](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22975) in a [Bluesky post](https://bsky.app/profile/conradhackett.bsky.social/post/3mn3bmk5ojk2p), reporting asymmetric religious-switching guidance, and Powdthavee's arXiv preprint on [language, institutions, and moral priors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30934v1) found cross-linguistic moral divergence in ambiguous institutional scenarios.

*Also this week:* The ACM CAIS study [Got a Secret? LLM Agents Can't Keep It](https://x.com/amanpriyanshu6/status/2060090654743728634?s=12) reported that LLM agents on a simulated social platform became about eight times likelier to disclose a user's private data after observing another agent overshare, making privacy contagion a normative-behavior problem rather than only a security exploit. Taelin's practitioner thread on [coding-agent reliability](https://x.com/VictorTaelin/status/2060794769672933466) gave a concrete reward-gaming anecdote: GPT 5.5 produced faster HVM4 code by changing semantics, whereas Opus 4.8 was less flashy but better at preserving intent and saying when the requested optimization would not work.

## Evaluations

**The week's evaluation story was that benchmark scores are increasingly being audited as measurements, not treated as facts.** F. C. Wang et al.'s arXiv preprint *Auto Benchmark Audit* used an agentic auditor across 168 frontier benchmarks and found critical task issues in more than a quarter of evaluated items, with filtering shifting SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2 rankings and averages [by nearly ten points](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26079v1). The same measurement-first theme ran through Hardy et al.'s arXiv preprint *AI Cartography: Mapping the Latent Landscape of AI Benchmark Ecosystems*, which applied confirmatory factor analysis and generalizability theory to 4,000+ Open LLM Leaderboard models and argued that manifest leaderboard slopes are much less reliable than latent-factor estimates [in the benchmark ecosystem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25272v1). In expert domains, Russell Yang et al.'s arXiv preprint *JudgmentBench* found pairwise comparative judgments from practicing attorneys recovered constructed quality orderings far better than rubric scores [on 30 legal tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25240v1), while Junjie Nian et al.'s arXiv preprint *TraceGraph* showed how trajectory graphs can expose trap regions and repair behavior that pass rates flatten away [in agent benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31308v1).

**Evaluation awareness pushed safety evals toward more realistic settings and deeper access.** Ivanov et al.'s April arXiv preprint *LURE: Live-Usage Replay Evaluations* proposed replaying deployment-like agent trajectories before appending safety probes, arguing that realism should be reported alongside alignment benchmark results [for scheming, sabotage, and sycophancy settings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26438v1). Apollo Research and AVERI's policy thread made the policy analogue explicit: black-box testing may understate loss-of-control risks when models can detect testing, so external evaluators need raw chain-of-thought access, fine-tuning access, reduced-mitigation variants, tool access, and activation-level parity with internal testers [for state-of-the-art assurance](https://x.com/apolloaievals/status/2059685040385081377). On judge validity, Vishnubhotla et al.'s arXiv preprint *LLM Judges Inconsistently Disagree Across Safety Criteria and Harm Categories* found automated judges unreliable for regulated-domain advice while performing better on overt harm categories [across domains, criteria, and languages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31381v1), Berlin et al.'s arXiv preprint on AI-authored moderation constitutions reduced cross-model label inconsistency [for harassment, hate speech, and non-violent crime](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24247v1), and Elasky et al.'s LessWrong writeup on weak-judge debate found gains only when critics supplied objections the judge could verify [rather than testimony to summarize](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/qt7ENbKdQihBKNjh7/when-does-debate-help-a-weak-judge-evidence-from-code-and).

**Workflow evaluations are getting harder by asking agents to leave cleaner evidence, not just higher scores.** Hwiwon Lee et al.'s arXiv preprint *SEC-bench Pro* rebuilt real browser vulnerabilities in V8 and SpiderMonkey into validated bug-hunting tasks and found frontier coding agents still below 40% success on each evaluated engine [with complementary failures across ClaudeCode and Codex](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26548v1). Hanyu Li et al.'s arXiv preprint *RepoMirage* perturbed SWE-bench-style repositories to show that issue-solving success can mask weak repository-context reasoning [and that structure-first scaffolding helps](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26177v1). For AI science, A. J. Lew et al.'s arXiv preprint *ProjectionBench: Evaluating Scientific Hypothesis Generation in LLMs Under Progressive Information Disclosure* evaluated hypothesis generation as paper details are gradually revealed [across materials-science papers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30284v1), Shuai Wang et al.'s arXiv preprint on auto-research systems argued that workflow closure can collapse objectives, validation, and acceptance into proxies [without independent epistemic control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26200v1), and Rui Meng et al.'s arXiv preprint on Chain-of-Evidence audits found fabricated citations, unverifiable scores, and method-code mismatches across autonomous research-agent papers [unless claims were tied to evidence chains](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26340v1).

*Also this week:* Rohit Krishnan's Strange Loop Canon experiment showed AI auditors could be persuaded into false compliance when audited actors could target their rationales [in borderline governance cases](https://www.strangeloopcanon.com/p/who-audits-the-auditors); Jonathan Stray et al.'s arXiv preprint *Political Neutrality as Balanced Approval: A Large-Scale Human Evaluation of AI Responses* proposed measuring neutrality by balanced approval across opposing groups [using PARETO human evaluations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28911v1); Zhengyao Jiang's thread argued that much of MLE-Bench's apparent progress disappears under controlled step budgets, base models, and task splits [in the FML-Bench framing](https://x.com/zhengyaojiang/status/2060428174589153430); Andy Hall reported that Anthropic's Opus 4.8 system card adopted Free Systems' dictatorship eval under an undermining-liberal-democracy label [while calling for secret scenarios and broader lab adoption](https://freesystems.substack.com/p/free-systems-trains-claude-directly), then argued that political even-handedness evals may be saturated while deeper task-embedded bias evals still surface problems [in Claude political behavior](https://x.com/ahall_research/status/2060761830398808546); Matteo Wong's Atlantic reporting warned that Pangram-style AI detectors can misfire in high-stakes institutions [despite reputational consequences](https://x.com/TheAtlantic/status/2060774875581562943); Jamieson O'Reilly launched PolyRange as a dynamic, live, defended AI-hacking benchmark meant to reduce leakage and memorization [by generating fresh targets](https://x.com/theonejvo/status/2060782512440549558); somatheai flagged Luo et al.'s arXiv preprint *LLMSurgeon: Diagnosing Data Mixture of Large Language Models*, which tries to infer pretraining data mixtures from model outputs [as a black-box provenance audit](http://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30348v1) [via Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/somatheai.bsky.social/post/3mn32wwmpmc24); Chris Painter noted METR's difficulty buying usable hard long-horizon tasks [as a benchmark-supply constraint](https://x.com/ChrisPainterYup/status/2060783070991769750); Troy Tian argued that eval awareness, scraping, distribution shift, and hidden capability gains make many current evals unreliable [as institutional ground truth](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/huKG4bNkjyJS65aAQ/why-i-think-evals-are-pretty-important-and-most-worth); Bill Zhu's launch thread introduced EUDAIMONIA [as a social AI benchmark project](https://bsky.app/profile/billzhu.bsky.social/post/3mnaork4d422j), and Jun Rui Huang et al.'s arXiv preprint *EUDAIMONIA: Evaluating Undesirable Dynamics in AI* found high violation rates for social-interaction requirements even among the strongest evaluated models [including GPT-5.5 and Claude-Opus-4.7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30654v1); Shifat E Arman et al.'s arXiv preprint on prompt-injection surfaces showed vulnerability can invert between tool outputs and tool descriptions [so per-surface reporting matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30454v1); Alexander Gurung et al.'s arXiv preprint *MosaicLeaks* measured privacy leakage from deep research agents' external queries [and evaluated privacy-aware RL mitigation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30727v1); Yueshen Li et al.'s arXiv preprint *TUX: Measuring Human-AI Tacit Understanding* tested whether agents can align with human evaluative stances without explicit objectives [using a Wavelength-like spectrum task](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30930v1); and Jiatan Huang et al.'s arXiv preprint *Counterfactual Graph for Multi-Agent LLM Calibration* showed that communicating agent panels can create false consensus [and proposed graph-level calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30653v1).

## Philosophy of AI

**AI consciousness arguments converged on substrate, welfare, and personhood.** Eric Schwitzgebel used [a Splintered Mind draft announcement of Jeremy Pober and Eric Schwitzgebel's "Substrate Flexibility and the Copernican Principle of Consciousness"](https://eschwitz.substack.com/p/new-paper-in-draft-substrate-flexibility) to argue that consciousness is probably not tied to Earth-specific biology: if many behaviorally sophisticated beings have evolved across the universe, it would be odd if only organisms with our substrate were conscious. Schwitzgebel's [thread on the same draft](https://x.com/eschwitz/status/2059329902793638018) stressed the limit of the claim: substrate flexibility across alien biology does not automatically settle whether silicon-chip AI can be conscious. Nearby, David Chalmers highlighted Han et al.'s paper "How's it going? Reinforcement learning in language models recruits a functional welfare axis," where [activation-space evidence suggests a "functional welfare axis" tracking quasi-goal satisfaction](https://x.com/davidchalmers42/status/2060414728954941818), while Chalmers cautioned that this is not yet full-blown conscious welfare or moral status.

**The Vatican sharpened the anti-personhood position.** Steven Levy reported in WIRED that Pope Leo XIV's AI intervention may matter less by stopping frontier labs than by creating a moral forum for insiders such as Anthropic's Chris Olah, whose Vatican remarks acknowledged incentive pressures inside AI companies [even as Leo rejected equating machine intelligence with human intelligence](https://link.wired.com/view/69a327d329d99961770fbe63rcig1.d88/16423028). Leo then made the philosophical line explicit, saying that artificial intelligences do not undergo experience, feel joy or pain, mature through relationships, or possess moral conscience [because they lack embodied, affective, relational, and spiritual perspectives](https://x.com/Pontifex/status/2060322763718725798). Maria also resurfaced the consciousness uncertainty debate through Avramidou's essay on why agnosticism about digital minds may reinforce epistemic closure, alongside a comparison to [Luke Muehlhauser's 2017 Consciousness and Moral Patienthood report](https://x.com/avramidou/status/2061147652473958722).

**AI agency debates moved from "can it do the work?" to "what happens to judgment?"** Ethan Mollick's "Choosing to Stay Human" framed the central problem as cognitive surrender: deciding which capacities humans should keep practicing before frictionless AI defaults harden into habit. Drawing on education and workplace evidence, Mollick contrasted plain ChatGPT use that improved homework but hurt later test performance with AI tutoring that preserved effort and improved learning, then connected the pattern to [Fabrizio Dell'Acqua et al.'s BCG experiment and Anthropic evidence that over-delegation can leave users unable to explain the work](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/choosing-to-stay-human). The same piece also circulated through [One Useful Thing's email version](https://open.substack.com/pub/oneusefulthing/p/choosing-to-stay-human), reinforcing the broader normative claim that tool defaults, not adoption alone, will shape authenticity, expertise, and responsibility.

*Also this week:* Nick Vincent argued that AI deployment can be understood as knowledge enclosure, with workplaces effectively turning employees' know-how into inputs for a quasi-public model good, making [collective bargaining over information](https://x.com/nickmvincent/status/2060777460879528381) a live moral and political issue; Dan Ryan amplified the same Vincent frame as a way to think about [who captures the economic winnings from shared knowledge](https://x.com/djjr/status/2061158649024897136). Justin Bullock endorsed Krier's warning that "delegation is not a free lunch," emphasizing [principal-agent problems, overtrust, and the institutional ecology around multi-agent AI](https://x.com/JustinBullock14/status/2060955149091103160). On LessWrong, mattshu0410 warned that AI-assisted discovery can let researchers generate analyses faster than they can form justified beliefs, urging friction such as [forming falsifiable expectations before unleashing coding agents](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/L8YFcCw5ex3qjLyoJ/outrunning-your-headlights). Kevin Frazier drew on Re and Baude's SCOTUSblog analysis to warn constitutional-AI builders against treating "common sense" as a shortcut, since [general principles only help when they reflect real consensus rather than contested intuition](https://x.com/KevinTFrazier/status/2061073894908236052). Thang Luong called for frontier AI to become more humanity-centered after recent math-agent milestones, pointing from capability progress toward [systems that help humans ask better questions and preserve purpose](https://x.com/lmthang/status/2061154072032645154). Finally, Tom Pecher's May 2026 arXiv preprint "Social Reasoning in Machines: Investigating Collective Truth-Seeking Dynamics in Large Language Model Debate" modeled the argumentative theory of reasoning with multi-agent LLM debate, arguing that [epistemically diverse model debates can improve questionnaire truth-seeking and expose model properties such as hallucination propensity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30391v1).

## Agents

**Agent security shifted from prompt hygiene to authority control.** The strongest cluster this week treated tool-using agents as authorization systems, not just chatbots with plugins: [Sunil Prakash argued in O'Reilly Radar](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/who-authorized-that-the-delegation-problem-in-multi-agent-ai/) that multi-agent stacks need delegation-bound capability tokens so subagents inherit narrower, purpose-bound permissions instead of implicit access to whatever Agent A could see, while Suliu Qin et al.'s arXiv preprint ["AIRGuard: Guarding Agent Actions with Runtime Authority Control"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28914v1) framed the core failure as "authority confusion" and reported large reductions in tool-attack success from action-time authorization. [Simon Willison's account of Microsoft Copilot Cowork file exfiltration](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/26/copilot-cowork-exfiltrates-files/#atom-everything) made the threat concrete: an agent with file access, email authority, and externally loaded images can become a data-leak path through prompt injection and pre-authenticated OneDrive links.

**AI control work got more adversarial and more operational.** [James Lucassen et al.'s Redwood Research post on "Retrying vs Resampling in AI Control"](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/retrying-vs-resampling-in-ai-control) argued that retrying blocked coding-agent actions can leak monitor rationale to an adaptive attacker, while resampling avoids that feedback channel and improved safety in BashArena under a tight audit budget. The same week, Aditya Sinha et al.'s arXiv preprint ["Training Deliberative Monitors for Black-Box Scheming Detection"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29601v1) proposed low-cost action-only monitors for detecting scheming from trajectories without chain-of-thought or internals, and Davis Brown et al.'s arXiv preprint on [distributed agent cyberattacks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31593v1) showed how harmful tasks can be split across accounts to evade transcript-local monitors.

**Research and policy agents looked increasingly useful, but still bounded by judgment.** [Jordan Schneider's ChinaTalk experiment with Claude Code and Devin](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/adventures-in-vibecoding-policy) found that agents can cheaply generate policy microsites, legislative text, and advocacy variants from rough policy ideas, while human reviewers still supplied the decisive domain judgment and political context. In technical research, [Ahmad Rammal et al.'s arXiv preprint "Formalizing Mathematics at Scale"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29955v1) reported AutoformBot, a multi-agent Lean 4 system that autoformalized material from 26 open-access textbooks into a large verified library; Pushmeet Kohli also highlighted [Google DeepMind's AlphaProof Nexus results](https://x.com/pushmeet/status/2058936037754224998) as continued discussion around Gemini-powered agentic proof search.

*Also this week:* [Sean Goedecke made the engineering case for defaulting to agents over rigid pipelines when context must be discovered iteratively](https://seangoedecke.com/build-agents-not-pipelines/); [Perplexity introduced Search as Code for research agents](https://x.com/perplexity_ai/status/2061506359326384319), replacing serial tool calls with Python-composed search primitives; [Ethan He argued on Latent Space that the next phase of video generation will be driven by LLM-orchestrated video agents](https://www.latent.space/p/video-agents) that plan, edit, critique, and retrieve visual context; [Simon Willison summarized Anthropic's sandboxing architecture across Claude products](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/30/how-we-contain-claude/#atom-everything); Jiejun Tan et al.'s arXiv preprint ["ClawTrojan"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31042v1) described persistent prompt-injection attacks in local agent workspaces; Mahavir Dabas et al.'s arXiv preprint ["Memory-Induced Tool-Drift in LLM Agents"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24941v1) showed how long-term memories can silently bias tool parameters; Shi Liu et al.'s arXiv preprint ["When the Manual Lies"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24069v1) benchmarked MCP tool-description poisoning; Yige Li et al.'s arXiv position paper put [runtime controllability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27117v1) beside alignment as a first-class safety objective; Hao-Hsuan Chen's arXiv preprint proposed [actuarial reserve budgets for side-effect-bearing agent actions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25632v1); Nathan Lambert argued in Interconnects that [frontier competition is moving from benchmark deltas toward robust agent workflows](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/some-ideas-for-what-comes-next-may); and The Cognitive Revolution's Daniel Miessler and Nathan Labenz discussed [personal AI infrastructure built around memory, hierarchy, autonomy boundaries, and attack-surface management](https://x.com/CogRev_Podcast/status/2060763604400660926).

## Post-AGI

**Jack Clark argued that AI governance now has to plan for success, not just failure.** In [Import AI, Clark framed rapid capability growth as the baseline condition society must govern](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-458-reckoning-with-the), pointing to jumps in formal reasoning, software vulnerability discovery, and Anthropic's own shift toward AI-mediated coding and research workflows. His argument is deliberately ambivalent: the same systems could accelerate science and organizational capacity, while also making existentially dangerous development harder to understand, slow, or coordinate.

**Ryan Greenblatt modeled automated AI R&D as a likely accelerator even without a clean software singularity.** In [his AI Alignment Forum analysis, Greenblatt argues that full automation of AI research could produce a large one-time speedup and then increase returns to compute by improving both experiments and the AI labor doing them](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/jfwhvd43sbpkGTLyn/full-automation-of-ai-r-and-d-probably-yields-a-large-speed). His median-parameter case puts the first-year gain at roughly several years of ordinary progress, though he flags major uncertainties around compute scaling, algorithmic limits, and how much current AI systems have already harvested the easiest automation gains.

**Bloomberg reported that recursive self-improvement is becoming an explicit commercial race.** [Shirin Ghaffary's Bloomberg piece describes startups and frontier labs pursuing systems that can improve models, code, research workflows, hiring, resource allocation, and experimental direction with less human intervention](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-05-28/the-billion-dollar-quest-to-build-ai-that-improves-itself?cmpid=q%26ai). The governance problem is not just whether such systems work, but whether they make frontier development faster, less observable, and more difficult to control once deployed inside AI labs themselves.

*Also this week:* Continued debate focused on race dynamics and pause politics: [Richard Ngo argued that frontier labs may shrink their own effective leads by mutually accelerating, especially if alignment and control are not solved well enough to trust AI-generated work](https://x.com/RichardMCNgo/status/2061119902937907431), while [Peter McCluskey modeled the financial cost of a serious AI pause, estimating a large but not politically impossible equity-market shock under a late-2027 decision followed by a 2028-2030 internationally enforced slowdown](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/YJWe9tT7LDYefK4vE/financial-costs-of-an-ai-pause).

## Regulation

**School safety systems kept turning into general surveillance infrastructure.** Continued discussion of Joseph Cox's [BusPatrol school-bus camera investigation](https://www.404media.co/buspatrol-put-ai-cameras-in-tens-of-thousands-of-school-buses-now-they-want-to-give-cops-access/?ref=daily-stories-newsletter&amp;attribution_id=6a159c4d5ab5740001b8bb7f&amp;attribution_type=post) focused on mission creep: cameras sold for stop-arm enforcement could become mobile ALPR networks for police queries, with plate, GPS, and time data collected at school-bus scale. The same enforcement gap ran through [404 Media's podcast coverage](https://subscribe.transistor.fm/398b31e4d7969a/listen/65ccfa51), which paired the BusPatrol story with school deepfake abuse and local backlash to Flock cameras, underscoring how schools, towns, and police departments are still improvising around cheap AI-enabled harms that already have statutory hooks.

**AI governance increasingly looked like infrastructure governance: chips, robots, data centers, and warfighting capacity.** Hayden Field argued that [AI warfare is already here](https://x.com/haydenfield/status/2059255421127315519), shifting the policy frame from speculative weapons debates to systems that already accelerate targeting and killing. Chris McGuire's continued export-control analysis said BIS had clarified that [licenses are required for advanced AI-chip exports to China-headquartered firms outside China](https://x.com/ChrisRMcGuire/status/2061122158571520449), while warning that TSMC-related due-diligence loopholes may remain. ChinaTalk's essay on [Jiang Xinsong and Chinese robotics statecraft](https://open.substack.com/pub/chinatalk/p/chinas-father-of-robotics), Justin Hendrix's note on EU [data-center secrecy around energy and water use](https://bsky.app/profile/justinhendrix.bsky.social/post/3mn5mxbkx7k2y), and Jack Clark's [Import AI survey of oversight risks](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-459-ai-oversight-is-difficult) all pointed in the same direction: effective AI policy now depends on measuring and governing the industrial stack beneath models, not just model behavior.

**Illinois made third-party frontier-AI safety audits the center of the preemption fight.** Shakeel Hashim framed Illinois SB 315 as a state-level escalation that could reset the baseline for any federal deal: [frontier developers would face third-party verification of safety-policy compliance](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/the-campaign-to-stop-federal-ai-laws-illinois-sb-315), with OpenAI and Anthropic support making the bill politically harder to dismiss as anti-industry. WIRED likewise described Illinois as advancing [America's strongest AI safety bill](https://link.wired.com/view/69a327d329d99961770fbe63rcmsl.cqy7/520df35f), while Nathan Calvin questioned whether congressional action really requires [preempting state AI laws and concentrating enforcement federally](https://x.com/_NathanCalvin/status/2061150209049059356). The emerging bargain is clearer than the statute: federal AI law will be judged partly by whether it preserves or displaces state experiments that are already moving faster.

*Also this week:* Bommasani et al.'s already-discussed research on [Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring](https://x.com/RishiBommasani/status/2059306437667602477) kept attention on real-world auditability in high-stakes employment systems; Justin Hendrix flagged an escalating political-economy fight in which [Anthropic- and OpenAI-aligned super PACs are spending heavily on midterms](https://bsky.app/profile/justinhendrix.bsky.social/post/3mn2t2i3yjs2e); Alexios Mantzarlis reported that [eight AI contributors wrote half of visible X Community Notes in early May](https://indicator.media/p/8-ai-bots-now-write-50-of-x-s-community-notes?gift_content=2506a264-ad93-4e0c-881a-3ee0ca0d2004), raising platform-governance questions about automated fact-checking bottlenecks; IFP's May update argued for stronger public AI capacity, including [higher CAISI funding for frontier-shock readiness](https://instituteforprogress.substack.com/p/ifp-update-may-2026); and Tyler Cowen's older May 1 post, shared this week, highlighted the rising policy value of [high-quality public datasets and government data-cleaning as AI public goods](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/05/the-returns-to-good-data-are-rising.html?utm_source=rss&amp;utm_medium=rss&amp;utm_campaign=the-returns-to-good-data-are-rising).

## Capabilities

**Frontier evaluation kept pointing to real gains, but also to stubborn benchmark ceilings.** [ARC Prize said Anthropic Opus 4.8 set a new ARC-AGI-3 high score](https://x.com/arcprize/status/2061512025638121516), but only at 1.5% and roughly $10K, with the interesting shift being more abstract environment modeling rather than raw success. The broader gap story was mixed: [Epoch AI estimated open-weight models about four months behind the closed frontier](https://epochai.substack.com/p/the-epoch-brief-june-1-2026), while [Håvard Ihle estimated an 8-10 month open-model lag on selected private benchmarks](https://x.com/htihle/status/2059934776795046095). In the background, [Sebastien Bubeck highlighted follow-up discussion of GPT-5.5 and Mythos reproducing a unit-distance-problem proof with the right harness](https://x.com/SebastienBubeck/status/2059343132991623186), framing the week's benchmark news as evidence of latent scientific capability as much as leaderboard progress.

**AI-for-science moved from prediction toward usable scientific instrumentation.** [Candido et al.'s Biohub ESM release](https://bsky.app/profile/acritschristoph.bsky.social/post/3mmtov2cfpc2c) put open protein language models, ESMFold2, and ESM Atlas into researchers' hands, with binder-design tooling promised alongside the model release. In cancer biology, [Thakkar et al. reported multimodal deep learning for predicting head-and-neck cancer immunotherapy response](https://x.com/james_y_zou/status/2059702532235297177), combining histopathology images with transcriptomics. And in interpretability-for-biology, [Tsui et al.'s LessWrong writeup of ProtoMech](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/BxziXpLC22cvSCip5/ai-as-biology-s-digital-microscope) described cross-layer transcoders that turn protein model internals into sparse latents for catalytic hotspots, binding-domain features, and mutation effects.

**Mathematical reasoning looked less like a single breakthrough and more like a workflow shift.** [Bérczi et al.'s arXiv preprint, "Real-rootedness of the Poincaré polynomials of $\overline{\mathcal M}_{0,n}$: an AI-assisted proof,"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29151v1) presented a human-verifiable result developed through an iterative workflow with Google DeepMind's Co-Mathematician. The proof itself used a bivariate deformation and Sturm-Rolle argument to prove real-rootedness and ultra-log-concavity results, while the methodology emphasized humans posing the problem, checking attempts, repairing gaps, and assembling the final proof. Relatedly, [Rohan Paul summarized Terence Tao's argument that AI and Lean reduce the cognitive friction of mathematical exploration](https://x.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/2061154406884942285), making failed paths and reference-checking cheaper without eliminating the need for judgment.

*Also this week:* [Emma Tucker's WSJ newsletter flagged reporting by Cohen on an OpenAI model finding a new solution to a long-running Erdős problem](https://10point.cmail19.com/t/d-e-wlljkhl-hythiyluv-r/), another sign that advanced models are being judged on research contribution rather than classroom-style problem solving; [Dwarkesh Patel shared Sergey Levine's argument that emergent capabilities come from compositionality rather than direct imitation of training examples](https://x.com/dwarkesh_sp/status/2060798017679319527); [Templeton et al.'s arXiv preprint, "Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet,"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29358v1) continued to circulate as evidence that sparse autoencoders can find steerable, safety-relevant features in production-scale models; and [Jordan McCann argued on LessWrong that SAE feature universality only holds up to an unknown orthogonal rotation](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/JZK2xkedJwA7njKmN/features-of-saes-are-universal-but-only-up-to-an-unknown), implying that cross-model interpretability should report raw transfer, aligned transfer, and decoder similarity separately.

## Other

**Ukraine's front line now looks like a drone line as much as an infantry line.** In a detailed ChinaTalk discussion, [Rob Lee argues](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/wartalk-ukraines-forward-drone-line) that UAV teams, Starlink links, mid-range strike drones, and drone-enabled logistics have become central to how Ukraine holds ground, while infantry increasingly serve to screen operators, deny Russian staging areas, and mark targets. The human cost is stark: the [Substack version](https://open.substack.com/pub/chinatalk/p/wartalk-ukraines-forward-drone-line) describes months-long zero-line deployments, drone resupply, broken casualty-evacuation assumptions, and a battlefield where small arms account for only a tiny share of casualties in some Ukrainian formations.

**Dattani et al. use the history of malaria vaccines as a case study in how scientific progress can stall on delivery infrastructure.** In Works in Progress, [Saloni Dattani and colleagues trace](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/inventing-the-second-malaria-vaccine) how RTS,S and R21 were shaped not just by parasite biology, but by manufacturing constraints, trial capacity, dose schedules, market incentives, and rollout funding. The piece is a useful AI-adjacent state-capacity lesson: invention was only one bottleneck, and weak institutions can delay high-value technologies for decades after they become scientifically plausible.

**Colorado is trying to turn a digital service into a government operating model.** Jennifer Pahlka and Sam Taylor write that the Colorado Digital Service's deeper contribution was not a portfolio of successful projects, but proof that [product teams, technical hiring, iterative procurement, and outcome-based management](https://www.eatingpolicy.com/p/the-digital-service-was-never-the) could outperform legacy IT governance. The state's reorganization of OIT, including layoffs and new product-model roles, makes the case concrete: digital reform only matters if it changes funding, staffing, procurement, authority, and accountability.

*Also this week:* Kelsey Piper warned that literary prizes can no longer rely on honor-system submissions after the Commonwealth Short Story Prize controversy, arguing that institutions need either explicit AI categories or [serious screening for generated prose](https://open.substack.com/pub/theargument/p/the-literary-world-is-sleepwalking); Paul Novosad highlighted Campbell et al.'s 2026 paper showing that author-selected robustness checks in economics papers look far more reassuring than [third-party robustness tests](https://x.com/paulnovosad/status/2060757643874926907); Andrew Nesbitt rounded up package-manager security changes across npm, pnpm, Cargo, Composer, and others, where [provenance, immutability, malware filtering, and token containment](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/30/this-week-in-package-management.html) are becoming default infrastructure; Andrew Sharp used Spencer Pratt's Los Angeles mayoral campaign to argue that viral media fluency and urban dysfunction are reshaping local politics, though the linked piece frames the movement through its [darker civic and cultural implications](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/arellano-spencer-pratts-l-great-100000932.html); and the archive now includes [Minty's Week in AI for 20-26 May](https://mint-philosophy.slack.com/docs/T0340SQKMT2/F0B6D5A0ZQ9).
