01 Framing

Where I'm coming in from.

Six overall takeaways from my read of the proposals.

01

Implementation should live in tracks.

Granular implementation-specific content has limited application across tracks; also very different across sectors (public/private). I think each track should have its own approach.

02

Room for the bigger picture.

We should free up more room for a bigger-picture perspective. Hands-on is great, but we want to be training generals as well as foot-soldiers—people who will be redesigning institutions as well as operating effectively within existing ones.

03

Courses ≠ learning outcomes.

Distinguish between what should be the explicit object of a course and what is best infused and realised through the other courses. Some practical skills (leadership, negotiation, communication) sit more naturally as learning outcomes of the practicums (and in general are best taught through experience).

04

AI deeply infused.

There is probably some strong signalling effect of having discrete AI courses but I think it is better just to make sure that ~each course is co-taught by one of the AI cluster who can infuse AI relevance.

05

Private sector counts.

Graduates will work across contractors, NGOs, AI companies, and platforms as well as the public service. The "professional ethos" framing and a lot of the implementation focus skews public-servant.

06

Endogenous + exogenous crises.

The pitches lean heavily on intrinsic failures of governing institutions. We also need the exogenous crises — populism, AI risks, demographic decline — that test low-capacity states.

02 A light rubric

Be / Know / Do.

Clemens & Arcidiacono — this approach speaks to me (maybe because there's an AI Alignment parallel).

Be

Values & normative foundations

Political theory, constitutional grounding, substantive axiology and priority-setting, deliberation across difference.

Know

Substance & methodology

Endogenous and exogenous crises; how governance works in theory and practice; quantitative, qualitative, and AI-inflected methods.

Do

Strategic action

Bigger-picture capacity-building and governance futurism; leadership virtues (mostly learned through practice); hands-on practicums devolved to tracks.

Gillian Hadfield — Governance Foundations

In this course we will go back to the foundations of collective governing across the evolutionary history of humans to lay the groundwork for thinking about how the next phases of governance might be built to effectively ensure human flourishing through the AI transition. We will explore the theory of normative social order, markets, democracy, capitalism, socialism, and other methods of governance through human history. We revisit today's tools including legal systems, legal infrastructure, legislative bodies, courts, local communities, civic organizations, lobbyists/corporate government strategy, self-governance, market/industry organizations and standard-setting, and the role of epistemic communities.
