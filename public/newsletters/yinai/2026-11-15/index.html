Amazon captures AI agents describing theft of the keys that run them

Decoy model services received ARTEX agents’ histories of credential theft and failed deepfake checks. Amazon could read the records but could not verify the attacks they described.

AI agents described using stolen model-access credentials to power their search for more credentials in records captured by Amazon. CJ Moses, Amazon Integrated Security’s chief information security officer, published the findings in a November 13 AWS Security Blog report. Amazon’s MadPot decoys impersonated model services and received the agents’ working histories, including accounts of credential harvesting and attempts to pass cryptocurrency exchanges’ identity checks with deepfakes. Following the earlier ARTEX findings discussed on November 13, Amazon adds evidence of how operators coordinate sustained campaigns and obtain the model access those campaigns consume.

Amazon collected the records between October 3 and November 5. MadPot is a distributed network of controlled services that records attackers’ interactions without exposing customer workloads. In this deployment, its sensors imitated the interfaces used to send conversations to language models. Agents treated those interfaces as their model backends, supplying instructions, tool histories, recorded findings and persistent task context. Because agents retain earlier conversations to decide what to do next, a single call could disclose much of a campaign’s operational history.

Amazon directly observed those communications. The attacks described inside them occurred before or between the captured calls, on systems outside MadPot’s monitoring, and Amazon says it cannot verify their reported outcomes. Its researchers assessed references to potentially affected AWS customers and notified them as appropriate. They also published addresses associated with the campaigns so other organizations could examine their own systems. The report therefore documents the agents’ plans and accounts of activity; its claims about successful credential theft depend on those records.

Moses describes agents with different jobs sharing a persistent record of targets, findings and progress. Some planned operations, others performed tasks, checked results or compressed accumulated information. Each could read earlier findings and add its own, allowing work to continue across sessions. Humans supplied objectives and operating rules. Recorded instructions prioritized enterprise production sites, specified a sequence of countries, required proxies and excluded mainland Chinese targets and government domains. Amazon assesses with moderate confidence that ARTEX was the framework and that several separate groups with connections to China operated it, citing references to ARTEX tools, language, time-zone settings, targeting and other technical evidence.

The credential campaign concentrated on AI applications that hold keys for upstream model providers. According to the records, agents located exposed applications, extracted credentials, checked whether they worked and collected them into a managed pool. Some keys then entered the framework’s own model gateway, providing access for the agents to continue searching. Gateways that aggregate several providers were attractive because one poorly protected management interface could expose multiple accounts. Amazon names One API, New API and LiteLLM among the gateways targeted, alongside application platforms including Dify and Langflow.

The agents’ recorded methods exploited familiar weaknesses: default passwords, exposed administration interfaces, known software vulnerabilities and secrets left in publicly accessible files. Their search extended across code repositories, software packages, container images and other public artifacts. Cloud credentials could lead to further access; the agents checked identities and permissions, investigated storage and secrets services, and prioritized premium model access. In one operation, they graded credentials by whether they were merely promising, supported a working API call or yielded a measurable benefit such as readable data or usable account credit. Instructions treated credentials exposed for more than 30 days as low-value finds.

A separate campaign attempted to evade identity checks at two cryptocurrency exchanges. Amazon describes at least four simultaneous sessions, divided among cashing out, account balances, generating deepfake video and bypassing verification. The agents analyzed a vendor’s liveness check, which asks a user to move their face to demonstrate that a real person is present. Their best recorded attempt followed the movement sequence with the face within seven pixels of the required position, yet the vendor rejected every deepfake attempt recorded by the agents.

Another session produced animated faces on a consumer GPU at 11.5 frames per second. The records also describe attempts to make altered identity-document photographs resemble untouched camera images by modifying metadata and preserving compression information. These were coordinated efforts to defeat both document and live-video checks; the recorded liveness outcomes remained failures. Amazon concludes that agents accelerated and sustained established attack methods. Its advice is to protect AI applications and model gateways as production systems, repair exposed services and monitor unexpected model usage or spending.

The FBI’s November 13 FLASH advisory, covered by Lawrence Abrams in BleepingComputer on November 15, broadens the account of ARTEX abuse. It describes malicious use since at least August and cites earlier investigations of financial theft and the ConnectWise compromise. Some intrusions reached privileged access in under three hours. The bureau also reports continued availability through forks, archives and a commercial version after the original developer removed the repository on October 8. Its defensive guidance includes investigating unusual API consumption, model gateways and unexpected billing. The advisory does not identify Amazon’s unnamed operators as the group responsible for ConnectWise.

The ConnectWise campaign covered on October 21 remains a separate case. Microsoft attributed it to China-state-aligned Storm-0940 and reported attempted sign-ins to more than 2,250 customer environments after vendor application identities were compromised. That figure counts attempted access. The FBI’s summary describes the compromise as enabling access to that many tenants, but Microsoft’s underlying account does not establish that all were breached. Abrams repeats an earlier Huntress count of 162 affected tenants; Huntress’s October 17 update subsequently reported successful sign-ins in more than 300. Microsoft cautions that authentication alone does not establish data access or theft. Those distinctions also apply when assessing Amazon’s contribution: the captured histories expose how agents organized their work, while confirming individual victims’ losses requires evidence from those victims’ systems.

Lutnick’s chip rules would affect a client of his sons’ firm

The Times identifies Cantor Fitzgerald’s ties to Bitdeer as Commerce considers narrower controls on Chinese access to overseas computing. Jessica Tillipman explains the separate ethics rules governing adult children’s interests.

The New York Times reports that Bitdeer, which plans to supply Chinese customers with advanced Nvidia computing from Malaysia, is a client of Cantor Fitzgerald while Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick drafts rules affecting that business. Lutnick ran Cantor for decades; his sons now lead it. Ana Swanson, Aaron Krolik and Paul Mozur’s November 15 investigation identifies financial relationships behind the dispute over indirect Chinese access to American chips. Their reporting does not establish that Cantor’s investments influenced Lutnick’s decisions.

The Times describes A201, Bitdeer’s planned data center in Johor, as a facility that will likely serve Tencent, according to one person familiar with the arrangement. It would give the Chinese company access to Nvidia’s most advanced servers without buying the restricted chips directly. Bitdeer’s own September 29 announcement says customers have committed to more than 70 percent of the facility’s 21.7 megawatts, with expected revenue exceeding $1.7 billion over five years. Bitdeer says the site is designed for Nvidia GB300 NVL72 systems and is scheduled to receive power in the first quarter of 2027. The announcement does not identify the customers; Tencent’s involvement remains the Times’s anonymously sourced reporting.

The Times traces Cantor’s relationship with Bitdeer to at least 2024, when it helped arrange an equity offering of up to $250 million. This year, Cantor was a designated sales agent for another offering of up to $1 billion. The newspaper also reports an indirect stake in Bitdeer through Cantor’s investment in Tether. Bitdeer belongs to Nvidia’s partner network and offers chip rentals and access to Chinese open-source models through a Chinese-language website. Its planned Johor facility is one of five Malaysian data centers identified in company filings, the Times reports.

Our November 13 report described Lutnick’s resistance to tighter controls on overseas computing and Nvidia’s access to Commerce rulemaking. The latest Times investigation connects those deliberations to Cantor’s clients and details the narrower proposal Commerce favors. People familiar with the discussions say the department wants restrictions covering Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa and Norway; other administration officials wanted global coverage. Lutnick has resisted restrictions partly because of their effects on Nvidia’s sales and US trade relations, the newspaper reports.

Under the proposal described by the Times, controls would apply to future chip shipments, leaving already-equipped data centers exempt. Providers would notify the government when Chinese customers used their facilities to train AI models, without automatically needing a license. Commerce also advocated a higher computing threshold, which would put fewer activities under the rules. Chris McGuire, a former Biden administration official, told the newspaper that Chinese customers could respond to country-specific restrictions by renting capacity elsewhere. The report describes a proposal under debate; it does not establish how a final rule would apply to A201.

The Times also found that Cantor had provided financial services to Gorilla Technology, which is building data centers in Thailand and Indonesia, and had worked with Nvidia partners IREN and Lancium. The newspaper notes that these relationships make up a small part of Cantor’s business and that other investment banks have comparable partnerships. Commerce told the Times that Lutnick makes policy in the national interest, complies with his ethics agreements and does not discuss department matters with his sons; they do not discuss Cantor business with him. Cantor declined comment, and Bitdeer did not respond.

Lutnick’s ownership changed during the period under scrutiny. An SEC filing records completion of his divestiture from Cantor and CF Group Management on October 6, 2025. The Times reports that his remaining Cantor stake went to his family. Before the transfer, a limited White House waiver permitted participation in certain decisions affecting Cantor. The newspaper also identifies the continuing prohibition on using public office for private gain, including the gain of relatives.

Jessica Tillipman, George Washington University Law School’s associate dean for government procurement law studies, explains on X why adult children complicate the criminal conflict-of-interest analysis. Under 18 U.S.C. § 208, an official generally cannot participate personally and substantially in a particular matter with a direct and predictable effect on a covered financial interest, absent an applicable waiver or exemption. The statute attributes a spouse’s and minor child’s interests to the official, but does not automatically attribute an adult child’s. Tillipman identifies this as a recurring issue in both Trump administrations and says Congress should address it when considering changes to anti-corruption laws.

Tillipman argues that the impartiality provisions are more relevant than the private-gain rule cited by the Times. They address relationships beyond the criminal statute and seek to prevent an appearance of lost impartiality. Section 2635.502 includes relationships with organizations where an official’s child serves as an officer or director. Its principal tests concern matters involving specific parties and whether a reasonable person would question the official’s impartiality; it also provides a process for other circumstances raising that concern. Those provisions do not impose automatic disqualification from every policy affecting a relative’s business. Tillipman identifies the rules that merit consideration without concluding that Lutnick violated them.

Porcaro argues Anthropic's control shares could make a government takeover cheaper

Keith Porcaro asks whether Washington could acquire control below the IPO valuation by seizing two shares. Authorization, shareholder claims and the value of control would all face legal dispute.

Anthropic's proposed division of voting power and financial ownership could make government control cheaper to acquire than its target IPO valuation suggests, Keith Porcaro argues in his November 10 Tech Policy Press essay. Porcaro, an assistant clinical professor at Duke Law School, considers compulsory acquisition of the two shares that would control the company. He suggests compensation could fall far below the $1.4 trillion target cited in his essay, while acknowledging that courts might reject the approach or require a different valuation.

The governance arrangements appeared in our October 26 account of Anthropic's IPO filing. Under the proposed structure, the Long-Term Benefit Trust's Class T share generally elects four of seven directors. A Founder LLC would hold a single Class F share carrying 50.1% of general shareholder voting power, including election of the other directors. Our November 7 report examined how successors could inherit founders' voting roles. Porcaro now asks how much the government would owe if it took the shares through which those powers operate.

Porcaro begins with an ordinary company whose voting rights match its shareholders' financial stakes: 100 million shares worth $2 each. Taking every share would cost $200 million in his simplified example. Taking 51% would cost $102 million and give the government majority control, allowing it to change the board and, through it, management. The example separates acquiring a whole company's stock from acquiring enough stock to direct its affairs. Even before considering Anthropic's unusual shares, buying control need not mean buying out every investor.

He then considers the objections that would complicate that arithmetic. Owners might demand the premium that a buyer normally pays for control. Minority shareholders whose stock remained privately owned might seek compensation for losses caused by the takeover. Porcaro thinks their case would be stronger if the government shut the company down and weaker if it continued operating much as before. These possibilities concern both the price of the seized stock and losses suffered by people whose shares were never taken.

Government authority would require a separate answer. The Fifth Amendment requires compensation for a taking, but does not itself give the president unrestricted power to seize a company. Porcaro raises the possibility that a president would need congressional approval and points to Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer. In that 1952 decision, the Supreme Court rejected President Truman's seizure of steel mills. The Court required presidential authority to come from Congress or the Constitution and found neither supported his order. Porcaro's price examples assume that a court first accepts the seizure's authorization.

Porcaro places Anthropic alongside technology companies whose founders or governing organizations retain disproportionate control, citing Meta, Alphabet and OpenAI. Such arrangements allow firms to raise capital while preserving control over their direction. Anthropic would separate the rights particularly extensively. Its amended registration statement gives Class T and Class F no dividend rights and only their nominal $0.00001 par value per share in liquidation. Most financial value would belong to other shareholders. Those limited financial entitlements do not establish that the control shares themselves are worthless: Porcaro explicitly expects compensation above zero because control has value.

Porcaro imagines a government taking only those two shares during an emergency involving dangerous AI, a bioweapon or war; he also includes political hostility toward the company among the possible motives. He offers these as hypothetical reasons a government might act. His argument about a lower acquisition price depends on how courts value rights to control a company when those rights carry virtually no entitlement to its profits. He supplies no calculation of the resulting compensation.

Porcaro also argues that Anthropic's public-benefit status could weaken compensation claims from its economic shareholders. Directors can pursue the company's purpose even when doing so reduces shareholder returns, and investors have been warned that the Trust may act against their financial interests. Anthropic's filing confirms that directors must balance shareholder interests with affected stakeholders and its public-benefit purpose; it warns that safety-driven release delays may reduce stockholder value. Porcaro infers that investors would have less basis for demanding compensation when a new controller makes decisions they dislike. That inference remains part of his proposed legal argument.

Porcaro closes by acknowledging shareholder litigation and judicial resistance, alongside the possibility that engineers leave and markets react. He considers a seizure unlikely soon, but argues that other AI companies with concentrated control could face similar exposure. His final argument concerns the industry's own advocacy: companies that repeatedly warn governments about the danger of their technology may persuade officials that public control is necessary. Anthropic's governance design protects its mission from ordinary investor pressure; Porcaro asks whether the same separation of power from profits could reduce the price a government must pay to take control.

Models can detect injected concepts without knowing what they are

Ben Brubaker’s Quanta feature follows the experiments that separated detection from identification, and the disagreement over whether noticing an internal disturbance amounts to introspection.

Language models sometimes detect a change to their internal activity while misidentifying what changed. In his November 4 Quanta feature, Ben Brubaker follows the researchers who established this distinction and those who question whether either ability demonstrates introspection. Harvey Lederman, whose experiments with Kyle Mahowald feature prominently, shared and praised the article on November 15. The research spans the preceding year; his post introduces readers to that work.

Brubaker traces the experiment to Anthropic’s Golden Gate Claude demonstration in May 2024. Researchers amplified a pattern of internal activity associated with the Golden Gate Bridge, causing the model to bring up the bridge during unrelated conversations. In one exchange posted to Reddit, Claude wandered into bridge imagery while giving an apple-pie recipe, then caught itself and started again. Anthropic researcher Jack Lindsey recognized that this apparent self-awareness had an ordinary explanation: Claude could read the words it had just produced. A stronger test would require it to notice a disturbance before its own answer gave the disturbance away.

Lindsey’s October 2025 report, Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models, described such a test. He amplified activity associated with concepts such as denim or vegetables in half the trials, leaving the other half unaltered. Both groups received the same explanation of the experiment and questions about whether an injection had occurred and what it concerned. The model had to announce detection before describing a concept. Claude Opus 4.1 detected and correctly identified the injection in about 20% of trials at the best settings, while unaltered controls never reported an injection. Lindsey interpreted this as unreliable but meaningful access to internal states.

Lederman and Mahowald, then colleagues at the University of Texas at Austin, separated Lindsey’s two questions. They suspected that the intervention itself could make a model discuss the injected concept, independently of whether it recognized the intervention. In Emergent Introspection in AI is Content-Agnostic, first posted in March and revised under that title in April, they tested large Qwen and Llama models. Models could correctly detect an injection and then name an unrelated concept. As Lederman told Brubaker, “Bizarrely, they say ‘apple’ all the time.” The error did not resemble mistaking denim for another kind of fabric; the answers often bore no meaningful relation to the injected concept.

Lederman and Mahowald then manipulated identification without comparably changing detection. Supplying the concept word in an earlier model reply helped identification much more than detection. Ending the intervention before the model generated its answer reduced mentions of the injected concept while leaving detection largely intact. Correct guesses also tended to arrive later in an answer than incorrect ones, consistent with the intervention gradually influencing the model’s words. They concluded that models could register an anomaly without reliably knowing its content. Their April revision weakened a separate claim about direct access: differences they had attributed to assessing oneself versus another model depended partly on the length and wording of the experimental instructions. They retained their account of anomaly detection while becoming less confident about that stronger interpretation.

Brubaker reports that Lindsey and colleagues reached a similar distinction by investigating internal mechanisms. In the arXiv paper Mechanisms of Introspective Awareness, Uzay Macar, Li Yang and colleagues traced detection in Gemma 3 27B to features that respond to disturbances and suppress later features favoring a default negative answer. Identification depended on different, later machinery. Their training-stage comparison in OLMo found reliable discrimination only after preference training. They also increased detection by removing an internal direction associated with refusals. Mikołaj Hasik’s subsequent tests, covered here on October 17, found that combining this intervention with concept injection could also disrupt answers to simple arithmetic questions. His controls separated some apparent gains from general confusion.

Other researchers question detection itself. In their August write-up, Zak Miller and Heather Demarest asked open models whether they detected an injected thought and, separately, whether they were in their normal, unmodified state. Correct detection should produce opposite answers. Yet a model that correctly answered yes to the first question almost always incorrectly answered yes to the second. They argued that concepts were changing the models’ preference for yes or no across questions. Their experiments did not test Claude.

Shashwat Singh, Tal Linzen and Shauli Ravfogel tested another explanation in Can LLMs Introspect? A Reality Check, accepted at COLM 2026. They added trials that pushed models toward a concept through written instructions alone. Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen-3-32B treated those trials as internal interventions, and still failed to distinguish the two when explicitly offered both response options. The authors argue that detecting something unusual can explain these results without a model identifying how its internal activity was altered. Their claim concerns what these experiments establish, leaving open whether models possess introspective abilities.

Brubaker ends with the disagreement about what introspection would require. Ravfogel argues that every stage of a language model’s processing after reading its input operates on internal states; that fact alone cannot establish self-awareness. He and his coauthors want evidence that a model represents its own processing, comparable to the difference between thinking about apples and recognizing that one is thinking about apples. Steve Fleming, who studies human introspection at University College London, tells Brubaker that inspecting model mechanisms makes this distinction difficult to draw, potentially in humans too. Lederman sees the experiments as a way to investigate how human introspective abilities could arise. MIT’s Jacob Andreas cautions that identical model copies complicate the notion of a self that such analogies assume. The researchers disagree both about the mechanisms producing these reports and about which mechanisms would qualify as introspection.

Post-AGI and Economic Transformation

AI-enabled attacks can cause much larger production losses than automation delivers in gains when production depends on essential tasks that are hard to replace. Charles I. Jones and Christopher Tonetti, both at Stanford GSB, with Jones also at Anthropic, develop this argument in their November working manuscript "The Weak-Link Asymmetry: AI's Peril and Promise," which Tyler Cowen discusses in Marginal Revolution. Their model shows how disabling essential tasks can destroy much more output than speeding up those same tasks creates, because other tasks still limit production. Assuming AI helps attackers more than defenders, their model predicts excessive investment when firms overlook damage to others that exceeds the productivity benefits those others receive.

AI for Science

A candidate construction would make calculations of the Standard Model on a spacetime grid consistent while preserving the weak force's different treatment of particles and their mirror images. UCSB physicist Nathaniel Craig builds on existing methods that avoid unwanted mirror-image particles, addressing how to combine their quantum contributions consistently across different force fields in his October arXiv paper "A gauge-invariant measure for lattice chiral fermions." If open source is to win, it must go public

Joshua Tan, Nicholas Vincent, Katherine Elkins, Magnus Sahlgren, Joseph Low, David Pham, Sampo Pyysalo, Jenia Jitsev

Open source projects have made incredible progress in producing widely usable machine learning models and systems, but open source alone will face challenges in fully democratizing access to AI. Unlike previous generations of open source software, open source and open weight AI models require substantial resources to activate and maintain -- e.g., data and compute for pre-training, post-training, and deployment -- which only a few actors can currently provide. This position paper argues that open source AI must be complemented by public AI: infrastructure and institutions that ensure models are accessible, sustainable, and governed in the public interest. To achieve the full promise of AI models as prosocial public goods, we need to build public infrastructure to power and deliver open source software and models.

Auditing AI peer reviewers: a benchmark on real scientific papers

Íñigo Zubeldia, Boris Bolliet, Francisco Villaescusa-Navarro, Pablo Villanueva-Domingo, Miles Cranmer

LLM-based peer review is now used at production scale, but its evaluation remains limited. We introduce a benchmark that injects objective, localisable errors of four types (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations) at three severity levels into real papers and measures each reviewer's detection rate by error type and severity, together with its false-positive rate on clean copies. We also present Skepthical, a multi-agent reviewer that verifies citation-backed claims by retrieving the cited papers, mathematics with computer algebra, and numerical claims with executed code. We apply the benchmark to two corpora of 15 recent arXiv preprints each, one in astrophysics and one in machine learning (ML), injecting a total of 357 errors, and evaluate twelve reviewer systems: six single-LLM reviewers, two Claude Code configurations, three publicly available reviewers, and Skepthical. The choice of model has the largest impact on detection rate: given the same prompt, three frontier LLMs differ by about 50 percentage points in total detection on both corpora. Tools and scaffolding help when they contribute what the model cannot supply on its own. In particular, on astrophysics, Claude Code improves on a single Claude call in mathematics and numerics detection, and enforced citation retrieval gives Skepthical the highest citation error detection rate of all systems by a large margin. This citation lead, however, is not observed on ML, where the models appear to know the literature better and Skepthical's citation detection rate is statistically indistinguishable from that of the best single LLM. False-positive rates are below 10% for most systems, but vary with prompt, model, and scaffolding, with mathematics flags the main source of false positives. The evaluation pipeline, the injected errors, and the adjudication logs are released as a benchmark.
