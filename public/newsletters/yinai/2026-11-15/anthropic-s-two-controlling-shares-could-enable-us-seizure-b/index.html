Porcaro argues Anthropic's control shares could make a government takeover cheaper

Keith Porcaro asks whether Washington could acquire control below the IPO valuation by seizing two shares. Authorization, shareholder claims and the value of control would all face legal dispute.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 November 2026 790 words · ~4 min

Anthropic's proposed division of voting power and financial ownership could make government control cheaper to acquire than its target IPO valuation suggests, Keith Porcaro argues in his November 10 Tech Policy Press essay. Porcaro, an assistant clinical professor at Duke Law School, considers compulsory acquisition of the two shares that would control the company. He suggests compensation could fall far below the $1.4 trillion target cited in his essay, while acknowledging that courts might reject the approach or require a different valuation.

The governance arrangements appeared in our October 26 account of Anthropic's IPO filing. Under the proposed structure, the Long-Term Benefit Trust's Class T share generally elects four of seven directors. A Founder LLC would hold a single Class F share carrying 50.1% of general shareholder voting power, including election of the other directors. Our November 7 report examined how successors could inherit founders' voting roles. Porcaro now asks how much the government would owe if it took the shares through which those powers operate.

Porcaro begins with an ordinary company whose voting rights match its shareholders' financial stakes: 100 million shares worth $2 each. Taking every share would cost $200 million in his simplified example. Taking 51% would cost $102 million and give the government majority control, allowing it to change the board and, through it, management. The example separates acquiring a whole company's stock from acquiring enough stock to direct its affairs. Even before considering Anthropic's unusual shares, buying control need not mean buying out every investor.

He then considers the objections that would complicate that arithmetic. Owners might demand the premium that a buyer normally pays for control. Minority shareholders whose stock remained privately owned might seek compensation for losses caused by the takeover. Porcaro thinks their case would be stronger if the government shut the company down and weaker if it continued operating much as before. These possibilities concern both the price of the seized stock and losses suffered by people whose shares were never taken.

Government authority would require a separate answer. The Fifth Amendment requires compensation for a taking, but does not itself give the president unrestricted power to seize a company. Porcaro raises the possibility that a president would need congressional approval and points to Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer. In that 1952 decision, the Supreme Court rejected President Truman's seizure of steel mills. The Court required presidential authority to come from Congress or the Constitution and found neither supported his order. Porcaro's price examples assume that a court first accepts the seizure's authorization.

Porcaro places Anthropic alongside technology companies whose founders or governing organizations retain disproportionate control, citing Meta, Alphabet and OpenAI. Such arrangements allow firms to raise capital while preserving control over their direction. Anthropic would separate the rights particularly extensively. Its amended registration statement gives Class T and Class F no dividend rights and only their nominal $0.00001 par value per share in liquidation. Most financial value would belong to other shareholders. Those limited financial entitlements do not establish that the control shares themselves are worthless: Porcaro explicitly expects compensation above zero because control has value.

Porcaro imagines a government taking only those two shares during an emergency involving dangerous AI, a bioweapon or war; he also includes political hostility toward the company among the possible motives. He offers these as hypothetical reasons a government might act. His argument about a lower acquisition price depends on how courts value rights to control a company when those rights carry virtually no entitlement to its profits. He supplies no calculation of the resulting compensation.

Porcaro also argues that Anthropic's public-benefit status could weaken compensation claims from its economic shareholders. Directors can pursue the company's purpose even when doing so reduces shareholder returns, and investors have been warned that the Trust may act against their financial interests. Anthropic's filing confirms that directors must balance shareholder interests with affected stakeholders and its public-benefit purpose; it warns that safety-driven release delays may reduce stockholder value. Porcaro infers that investors would have less basis for demanding compensation when a new controller makes decisions they dislike. That inference remains part of his proposed legal argument.

Porcaro closes by acknowledging shareholder litigation and judicial resistance, alongside the possibility that engineers leave and markets react. He considers a seizure unlikely soon, but argues that other AI companies with concentrated control could face similar exposure. His final argument concerns the industry's own advocacy: companies that repeatedly warn governments about the danger of their technology may persuade officials that public control is necessary. Anthropic's governance design protects its mission from ordinary investor pressure; Porcaro asks whether the same separation of power from profits could reduce the price a government must pay to take control.
