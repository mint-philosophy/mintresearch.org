Sharadin argues that AI which thinks for children is wrong for children

In a new Synthese paper, Nathaniel Sharadin argues that always-available, substitutive, general-purpose AI help removes both the practice and the incentive children need to develop self-governing minds, that developers are responsible, and that age-gating, tutors and "different skills" do not answer the charge.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 19 October 2026 1497 words · ~7 min

Nathaniel Sharadin, assistant professor of philosophy at the University of Hong Kong, argues in "Cognitive enfeeblement by design", published open access in Synthese on 19 October, that giving children the kind of AI help now being built is pro tanto morally wrong. The capacities that make a person self-governing are skills, he argues: children build them through effortful practice and lose them without it, and an assistant that is always available, does the work itself and covers every domain takes away both the practice and any reason to attempt it. The abstract opens with "Thinking hard is hard." People are cognitive misers, Sharadin says, and the companies offering such help are making it the default way of meeting any cognitive demand.

The paper begins with a taxonomy. Cognitive difficulty, on Sharadin's account, is load on the executive system, felt as aversive effort, and assistance moves that load outside the person, to a tool or to someone else. Help varies on three dimensions. Substitutability separates what he calls orthotics, which support an engaged executive system (a spell-checker), from prosthetics, which take over from it (a text generator that writes the whole essay); the same tool can be either depending on how it is used. Availability runs from a ghostwriter to software on one's own laptop, and domain-generality from a calculator to a round-the-clock human executive assistant. Help that is high on all three he names extreme cognitive assistance. The substitution axis extends earlier taxonomies of cognitive artifacts, including Marco Fasoli's 2017 division of artifacts into those that complement, constitute or substitute for our cognitive processes; the availability and domain-generality axes are Sharadin's addition. As a portrait, offered explicitly not as evidence, he borrows Scott Alexander's 2012 fable of the Whispering Earring, whose first words to every wearer are "Better for you if you take me off." and whose always-correct advice ends up directing individual muscle movements.

Sharadin argues that LLM-based systems are headed toward this extreme. Natural-language interfaces make them available to anyone who can talk; the companies building them (he lists Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Meta, Amazon and Anthropic alongside Tencent, Baidu, Alibaba and ByteDance) state autonomous task completion as their goal; inference prices have fallen two to three times a year since 2023, on Epoch AI's estimate; and their competence, while jagged, is broad. For evidence that current systems already function as prosthetics he cites Nataliya Kosmyna, Pattie Maes and colleagues' EEG preprint, in which essay writers using an LLM showed the weakest brain connectivity of three groups.

The moral premise is what he calls the Open Future Principle: it is pro tanto wrong to impede a young person's normal development of the capacities required for autonomous agency without substantial justification. Sharadin traces it to Joel Feinberg's 1980 essay on the child's right to an open future, which treats certain rights as held in trust for the adult the child will become, and so as capable of being violated in advance, and notes that Tamar Schapiro, Harry Brighouse and Adam Swift reach similar obligations by other routes. The empirical premise, which he labels Development, says that the four core regulatory skills (inhibitory control, working memory, cognitive flexibility, and planning) are built by repeated exercise during childhood and adolescence and fail to develop normally when that exercise is substantially cut. He supports it from the executive-function training literature, where a 2019 meta-analysis across 43 studies found that training a component improves it while transfer to other skills is weak and not significant; from summer learning loss, where NWEA data show children losing a quarter of the previous year's maths gains after third grade and as much as half by seventh; and from studies of helicopter parenting, which find reduced executive function persisting into early adulthood. He also ties the premise to Aristotle's habituation and to Xunzi's accumulation.

Section 7 assembles the argument. Substitution removes children's opportunities for exercise; ubiquitous availability removes the incentive, because effort is aversive and help is ready to hand; domain-generality spreads both effects across every activity in which the skills might otherwise be practised. The result he calls cognitive enfeeblement: skills developed less robustly than they would have been, and so a more or less permanent diminution of the capacities for autonomous agency. Sharadin admits that nobody knows how much practice a child needs, a threshold he calls Baseline, but argues from instructional-time studies and summer loss that many children sit near it, so marginal reductions matter, and that under uncertainty the wrong is of a second kind: knowingly imposing a substantial risk of serious harm, like firing a gun into a room one has been told may be occupied. Convenience and productivity, the justifications that might excuse adult use, do not count as substantial for children. Because building and deploying such systems all but guarantees that children will use them, developers are responsible, though parents, schools and political actors share the fault.

Much of the second half answers objections. The obvious reply, keep it from children, concedes the point, but Sharadin argues it cannot be done: "Age-restricted gates presuppose the existence of a gate!", and unlike alcohol or a social-media account, assistance embedded in the infrastructure of daily life has none. Schools cannot police it (he reports that YouTube can be watched in districts that block the domain by embedding links in Google Docs), and parents, schools and jurisdictions each fear falling behind if they withhold it. The knife analogy, on which any tool can be misused, fails because the harm is foreseeable at scale, follows from proper use, and falls on people who did not choose it. He welcomes Sal Khan's vision of orthotic AI tutors in principle; the trouble with such tools is not that they would be bad, "it’s that we’re unlikely ever to get it." Users prefer substitution, companies competing for users will follow them, general chatbots route around locked-down classroom tools, and, citing a Brookings task-force report from January, "The track record of educational technology is completely abysmal." To the objection that children will develop different skills, supervising and delegating, he answers that supervisory load falls as systems become more reliable and that judging whether a plan is good requires being able to plan. He allows he could be wrong, since "we are presently running a civilizational-scale natural experiment that will provide evidence."

The conclusion limits the claim to children. Sharadin says it gives reason for laws and norms keeping extreme assistance from them, and for sanctioning those who supply it, but says nothing about adult use or the pace of AI development. His closing thought experiment is CogAid, a drink that makes adults vastly more productive while leaving children who drink enough of it unable to think for themselves; most adults would keep it in the fridge out of children's reach, and so would he, but for this technology there is no such place. "Worse, it is rapidly, deliberately being placed into every fridge on Earth."

The paper is the second of a pair. Its companion, "Extreme Cognitive Assistance and Open Futures", appeared in the Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy in July and carries the fuller taxonomy and defence of the principle; its abstract adds that the companies "may already have succeeded", since help need not be maximal on every dimension to impede development, and that for compensating demand for cognitive exercise arising elsewhere "the prospects are dim". Sharadin presented the argument at Lingnan University's Hong Kong Catastrophic Risk Centre in September 2025, and in June extended it to politics at the University of Iceland, arguing that widespread enfeeblement would mean "wholesale political delegitimisation". The word has an earlier life in AI-risk writing that he does not cite: Dan Hendrycks, Mantas Mazeika and Thomas Woodside's 2023 overview of catastrophic AI risks, following Stuart Russell, warned of a slide into dependence in which "people might have few incentives to gain knowledge and cultivate skills". Sharadin locates the harm in childhood development and grounds it in executive-function research.

Two recent studies bear on his objections without appearing in his references. Hamsa Bastani and colleagues' 2025 PNAS field experiment gave nearly a thousand high-school maths students either a standard GPT-4 interface or a tutor version with learning safeguards; once access was removed, the standard-interface students did worse than controls, while the safeguarded tutor largely avoided the harm. The result supports the enfeeblement mechanism in a classroom and also shows an orthotic design holding, under controlled conditions, where Sharadin expects the market to prevent it. Hao-Ping Lee, Advait Sarkar and colleagues' CHI 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers found that generative AI shifts critical thinking toward verifying outputs, integrating responses and stewarding tasks, the supervisory skills Sharadin says cannot stand in for planning. The Brookings report he relies on, by Mary Burns, Rebecca Winthrop and colleagues, drew on more than 500 participants in 50 countries and concludes that at this stage "the risks of utilizing generative AI in children's education overshadow its benefits."
