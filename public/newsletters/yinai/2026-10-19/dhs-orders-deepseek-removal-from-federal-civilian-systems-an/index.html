Inside the DHS order removing DeepSeek from civilian agencies

The July 24 order, public since October 19, bans DeepSeek and anything built on it from civilian procurement and IT, rests its case on data flows to China and a flawed iOS app, and gave agencies an exception window that closed before anyone outside government saw it.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 19 October 2026 1040 words · ~5 min

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin signed Exclusion and Removal Order #2026-01 with a release date of July 24, 2026, and the public learned of it on October 19, when DHS filed a notice for the Federal Register and The Wall Street Journal reported it. The order tells federal civilian executive branch agencies to stop buying products and services from Hangzhou DeepSeek and its affiliates, "effective immediately after issuance of this Order", and to strike them from approved-product lists, service-provider equipment lists and e-commerce markets. It also tells them to remove DeepSeek products, and "third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations", from their IT systems at once or apply for an exception. Mullin acted under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018 on a recommendation from the Federal Acquisition Security Council, which that statute created. National security systems and sensitive compartmented information systems are outside the order, because the statute assigns those to the Secretary of Defense and the Director of National Intelligence. The notice appeared in the Federal Register on October 20 at 91 FR 66346.

The Journal's Connor Hart wrote that "The notice didn't specify the security concerns underlying the decision" or set a removal deadline. That is true of the two-page notice; the four-page signed order appended to it does both. Its risk summary states that "DeepSeek's models present multiple threat vectors." DeepSeek's servers are in China, where the legal system gives state cyber and intelligence agencies wide access to private company infrastructure; DeepSeek-hosted instances collect personal information at sign-up and may fingerprint users; and government employees or vendors who type prompts into them may send sensitive data to China, which "could produce actionable intelligence on U.S. persons and/or government programs". The order adds that "The models themselves may contain malware or other security vulnerabilities." For evidence it cites the House Select Committee on the CCP's April 2025 report "DeepSeek Unmasked" for the data-collection and fingerprinting claims, and NowSecure's February 2025 analysis of the DeepSeek iOS app, which found that the app disabled App Transport Security, encrypted data with Triple DES and hardcoded keys, and sent telemetry to ByteDance's Volcengine. That evidence concerns the hosted service and the phone app, while the removal mandate reaches self-hosted and third-party deployments, and the order does not define "implementations". CyberScoop's Derek B. Johnson notes that the prompt-and-response exposure the order describes applies to every major chatbot, and that DeepSeek is the only Chinese developer named even though Moonshot's Kimi and Alibaba's Qwen draw similar concerns.

Agencies that cannot comply may ask CISA for an exception within 60 days of issuance, in writing, with a compelling justification such as harm to mission-critical functions and a list of alternative mitigations; CISA approves or denies in writing and may provide technical help. Until removal, agencies are encouraged to watch traffic in and out of their networks, tighten logging and data-exfiltration controls, and flag anomalous activity. The Secretary must review the order within a year and annually after that. Sixty days from July 24 is September 22, four weeks before the order became public, as TechTimes pointed out; whether agencies received the order privately in July is not documented, though Reuters described it as a July memo made public on Monday.

The FASCSA order power had been used once before. In September 2025 the Office of the Director of National Intelligence excluded Acronis AG from intelligence community and SCI systems, an order that gave no rationale, as Covington lawyers noted; judicial review of such orders lies only in the D.C. Circuit. DHS's closest earlier action was its 2017 removal of Kaspersky products through Binding Operational Directive 17-01 under FISMA, which gave agencies 30 days to identify the software, 60 to plan and 90 to begin removal, and left Kaspersky code embedded in other companies' products alone. The DeepSeek order requires immediate removal and reaches embedded and derivative products by design. Congress had already acted on the intelligence side: Section 6604 of the FY2026 Intelligence Authorization Act, enacted December 18, 2025 as part of P.L. 119-60, told the DNI to remove "the DeepSeek application or any successor application or service" from IC systems, a narrower target than the implementations clause. Commerce and the Navy had banned DeepSeek on their devices, as had New York, Tennessee and Virginia, per Reuters. Elsewhere in the executive branch, OMB Director Russell Vought, who is copied on the DeepSeek order, rescinded the 2023 TikTok device ban on August 10 after an Office of Legal Counsel opinion, stating that "TikTok may be used on government devices." And on September 25 the D.C. Circuit upheld the Department of War's exclusion of Anthropic under a separate FASCSA provision, holding that supply chain risk "does not require hostile or malicious intent by a supplier". NIST's CAISI evaluation of September 2025, which found agents built on DeepSeek R1-0528 on average 12 times more likely than US frontier models to follow malicious instructions, goes uncited in the order.

China's foreign ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said on October 20 that "China consistently opposes the over-generalization of national security" and that Beijing would protect its companies, CNBC reported via Reuters. The commerce ministry voiced "strong dissatisfaction and firm opposition", said the order disregards the consensus reached by Xi and Trump, urged Washington to use the two countries' AI dialogue mechanism and promised to "take all necessary measures", according to Reuters and the Associated Press; AP adds that Trump and Xi are expected to meet in mid-November at APEC in Shenzhen with AI on the agenda. DeepSeek has not commented. The order became public two days after DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash-1017 under an MIT license, and VentureBeat's Carl Franzen reads the "developed using" clause as aimed at vendors who fine-tune Chinese base models and sell the result under their own names. Gabrielle Hempel of Exabeam told Security magazine that "country of origin isn't necessarily a proxy for security risk" and that a locally deployed model under agency controls differs from a foreign-hosted service. On Hacker News, where the Reuters story drew 382 comments, the most-replied thread asked what the security risk is when open weights run on an agency's own servers, and several commenters asked why DeepSeek alone among Chinese open-weight developers was named.
