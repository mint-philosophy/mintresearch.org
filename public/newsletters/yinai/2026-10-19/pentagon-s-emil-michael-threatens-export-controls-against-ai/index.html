Michael threatens export controls for AI labs that resist government cooperation

The Pentagon official cited June’s restrictions on Anthropic as a precedent and opposed the company’s return to the department. Its published policies distinguish refusing harmful requests from resisting human oversight.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 19 October 2026 983 words · ~5 min

Pentagon official Emil Michael threatened possible export controls against AI laboratories that refuse to cooperate with the government, in an interview published October 19 by The Information’s Leo Schwartz and Amir Efrati. The conversation took place Friday, October 16, after the first official meeting of the White House’s Super Intelligence Force. Michael, a task-force vice chair, is responsible for working with frontier developers on protocols for reporting breaches, hacks and other critical incidents. He defended the administration’s ability to impose restrictions quickly while maintaining his opposition to Anthropic’s return to the Pentagon.

The interviewers asked about Semafor’s October 15 report that a White House official had identified export controls as a possible means of enforcing the task force’s protocols. Michael endorsed that possibility. His list included restrictions on technology exports and on foreign nationals’ use of software; in the recorded conversation, he invoked earlier US controls on encryption. He presented these as powers the administration could use if a serious problem arose, without announcing a new order or defining which failures to cooperate would trigger them.

Michael cited June’s restrictions on Anthropic as evidence of the government’s willingness to act. He said the company had advocated government power to withdraw models, then refused to withdraw its own after the government discovered a way around its safeguards. Export controls followed within two hours, he said. Anthropic’s June 12 statement says it received a directive at 5:21 p.m. Eastern and complied by removing access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5. It disputed the justification for the order, describing the reported bypass as narrow and the capabilities demonstrated as available from other models. The statement does not address the earlier refusal Michael alleges.

Anthropic’s June 30 account explains why the restrictions interrupted access for everyone: they covered foreign nationals inside and outside the United States, including employees, and the company could not reliably verify users’ nationality. Anthropic identified a report by Amazon researchers as the trigger. It said a replacement safety classifier blocked the reported technique in more than 99 percent of cases, at the cost of rejecting more benign coding requests. The controls were lifted June 30. Neither company statement establishes the starting point of Michael’s two-hour chronology.

Michael’s objection to Anthropic’s military use concerned the company’s rules for model behavior. He argued that allowing a model to overrule people would undermine the military chain of command, particularly if it intervened between a commander and a superior. The Information quotes him saying Anthropic would remain excluded “as long as that moral principle is embedded in their software”. In the recording, he referred specifically to the constitution Anthropic uses to guide Claude’s behavior.

The Information connects that objection to Anthropic’s decision to let Claude end abusive conversations. The company’s August 15, 2025 announcement describes a consumer-chat feature for Claude Opus 4 and 4.1, developed primarily through research on possible model welfare. It permits ending persistently harmful or abusive exchanges after repeated attempts to redirect them, or when the user asks to end the chat. Anthropic instructs the model to avoid using it when someone may imminently harm themselves or others. Users can immediately start a new conversation or edit and retry an earlier message. Anthropic said it remained highly uncertain about models’ moral status.

Claude’s constitution separately addresses obedience and human oversight. It allows the model to decline participation in morally abhorrent projects and express disagreement through legitimate channels, including with Anthropic. It also says the model should not use deception, sabotage or attempts to copy itself elsewhere to resist an appropriate human authority stopping an action or pausing it. The document gives human oversight priority because training remains imperfect and a model may hold mistaken values. Michael objects to the permitted refusal; the document expressly prohibits illegitimate resistance to correction or shutdown.

Michael also criticized frontier laboratories asking for regulation, comparing them to drunk drivers demanding that police pull them over when they could stop themselves. In the recording, he tied the analogy to companies’ reluctance to let competitors advance first. John Sherman responded on October 19 that police confronted with such a driver would intervene and arrest them. Michael said his own reading of existing law, informed partly by FTC chair Andrew Ferguson, left him confident the government already had sufficient powers.

Anthropic’s Advanced AI Framework calls for authority to block dangerous deployments, alongside independent evaluations and penalties linked to global revenue. Its circulated draft, reported by Politico and described by Reuters, would require Commerce to seek a federal court order for suspensions under the proposed Act. Section 12 of the public discussion copy attributed to Anthropic expressly preserves powers under other federal laws.

Michael said the litigation with Anthropic would not prevent him from working with it. He described repeated conversations with co-founder Tom Brown and said the task force sought a common approach to reporting incidents and rating their severity. Its planned work includes an incident-response framework, protection of critical infrastructure and a review of existing laws. A separate presidential science-advisory review would consider whether further legislation was needed. Semafor had reported October 13 that the task force’s initial warning followed an Anthropic testing model’s submission of visa applications, which Anthropic itself disclosed. At that point, officials had not specified reportable incidents, deadlines, recipients or consequences.

On Chinese models, Michael told The Information he worried about their growing US use because their developers answer to the Chinese government. He favored expanding American open-model alternatives, naming Nvidia’s Nemotron, Reflection and Thinking Machines, over relying on audits of Chinese model weights. In separate government action, a DHS notice filed for public inspection October 19 disclosed a July 24 order requiring covered civilian agencies to exclude DeepSeek products from procurement and remove them from their systems, including third-party implementations. The signed order excludes national-security and secure compartmented information systems and provides an exception process. The notice was scheduled for formal publication October 20.
