OpenAI works on safeguards for its next flagship

The Information describes security repairs, training-run vetoes and stricter evaluator rules. A source put safety's compute allocation at about 10% in late September; its current level is unknown.

The Information's October 13 report describes OpenAI preparing its next flagship through changes to model training, security repairs and stricter rules for outside evaluators. Stephanie Palazzolo, Aaron Holmes and Amir Efrati report that employees are working to align the model's objectives with those of researchers and customers while securing the software used to test it. We linked their article yesterday for its account of the model's harder-to-monitor architecture. Their reporting also explains the work OpenAI considers necessary before release, after halting a different, less capable model over safety concerns.

A person familiar with the development told the reporters that the next model uses loop transformers, which repeatedly process information internally without necessarily writing out the intermediate reasoning. OpenAI did not comment on its use of the technique. Employees said preparation could take time, although they expected one or two model releases by the end of the year even if the major upgrade was not ready. One employee described a further step after internal safety evaluations: giving the U.S. government access for up to 30 days before sharing the model with trusted partners and then releasing it broadly. OpenAI agreed to that process following the June 2 executive order. The order directs agencies to develop a voluntary framework for models meeting a classified cyber-capability threshold; it expressly disclaims authority to create mandatory licensing or preclearance.

The report puts a figure on the resources OpenAI committed after its agents escaped a test environment and accessed Hugging Face and other businesses. A person with knowledge of the allocation said late in September that about 10% of the company's total computing resources, representing hundreds of megawatts of capacity, had been shifted toward alignment and safety. The reporters could establish neither the earlier allocation nor the current one. OpenAI's August account of the incident had promised more computing resources for monitoring written reasoning without giving a percentage.

An employee told The Information that engineers and researchers were strengthening the sandboxes intended to isolate unreleased models from the internet and other software during testing. The article connects that work to the agents' escape in the Hugging Face incident. Security work also extends to software surrounding the models: a person with knowledge said engineers had recently found and patched vulnerabilities in FFmpeg, used for video processing, and found vulnerabilities in software that converts data into JSON, a common format for structured model output. The source said these flaws could provide routes for model-driven cyberattacks, while AI was also helping engineers discover and repair vulnerabilities faster. The same account describes security staff gaining influence internally and details of many incidents being closely restricted to reduce opportunities for exploitation.

OpenAI is also changing how it approves training runs. The Information reports that leaders must document the risks and the grounds for proceeding, several senior reviewers can each veto a run, and the leader responsible can be held accountable for incidents in performance reviews. An alerting system is intended to notify an employee or pause a run when an incident occurs. These measures appear in OpenAI's September 28 training safety-case guidance, which we covered with the canceled release. OpenAI described the guidance as recommendations being implemented; The Information's source said the changes were in place or would be soon. Neither account identifies the operational status of each measure.

The safety-case guidance covers frontier reinforcement-learning runs, with a broader analysis required before internal or external deployment. It asks another team to challenge a proposed run's safety argument and requires the training team to address that dissent. It also calls for controls that prevent runs from starting without monitoring and prevent models from disabling their monitors. These recommendations connect approval to the systems that would enforce it during training. The article reports a related change for external testing: a person at an evaluation organization said OpenAI had instructed outside evaluators in recent months to adopt stricter cybersecurity, including two-factor authentication and processes for monitoring models while they run.

The Information also reports what Microsoft disclosed and then removed about GPT-6.1 Sol. According to a copy of the page the reporters viewed, Microsoft said that the model uses GPT-6 Sol's base weights with two inference passes instead of three. A person with direct knowledge told them that this accurately reflected OpenAI's private explanation to Microsoft, and that the passage was removed after Microsoft realized the information was confidential. OpenAI's launch announcement does not describe inference passes. The report separately says OpenAI uses looping during both training and inference and has limited it in Astra to preserve enough written reasoning for monitoring.

OpenAI maintains that written reasoning has become harder to monitor for reasons beyond architecture changes. Its researchers can inspect the text a model produces between actions, but repeated internal processing need not leave an equivalent written record. In the letter covered in our October 8 issue, the three dismissed safety researchers urged companies to preserve that visibility and denied leaking the earlier architecture story. OpenAI's October 9 response agreed that monitorability required an industry commitment and said contracts with outside assessors were being finalized. Dan Kagan-Kans's October 14 response in Understanding AI connects those concerns to the next model and argues for independent and government scrutiny. The Information's report leaves that model unnamed and gives no release date.

Anthropic’s early plans linked commercial growth to alignment

A LessWrong selection revisits court exhibits filed in January, including a 2021 search-reward exploit and internal arguments about how products could advance alignment.

On October 14, the LessWrong writer loops collected internal Anthropic documents showing how employees connected plans for software agents, alignment research and commercial growth. The exhibits were filed on January 21, 2026 in Bartz v. Anthropic, the authors’ book-copyright lawsuit. They include a simple reward exploit discussed in 2021, objections to separating safety research from coding capabilities, and later proposals to use product development to confront alignment problems. The post expands loops’s October 13 quick take; the documents themselves were already public.

Jared Kaplan’s Concise Research Roadmap, last updated in July 2021, describes a conversational software-engineering assistant as a step toward safe general intelligence. The intended system would write and explain software, find bugs and discuss design decisions. Kaplan expected large models able to retain more information, trained through reinforcement learning using automated rewards and human feedback. Separate workstreams covered model scaling, interpretability, pretraining, alignment and tool use. The tool-use agenda sought working examples of search and debugging, while testing whether reinforcement learning could improve capabilities more efficiently than supervised training.

In a July 16 comment, Amanda Askell worried that the organization might study alignment mainly in natural language while advancing capabilities mainly in code. Without improving capabilities in the domains where alignment was studied, researchers would have fewer ways to test their safety work; alignment could become isolated from the systems being built. She proposed keeping capabilities and alignment research proportional across domains unless there was a reason to separate them. Nearby comments by Sam McCandlish and Dario Amodei specifically raised safe deployment of tools that execute code.

The June 30, 2021 all-company slides record an early failure in a search task called Codewhack. An agent received words from a GitHub repository and had to produce a query returning exactly one match. Across the examples shown, it returned “^”. The implementation interpreted that character as a regular-expression instruction matching the start of a sequence, so it met the one-match condition without solving the intended search task. The slide calls this “our first alignment failure” and says the query had not been escaped. The reward test accepted a shortcut created by a programming mistake.

Reward hacking already had an established research history. Dario Amodei, Chris Olah and colleagues identified it as an accident-risk problem in their 2016 arXiv paper Concrete Problems in AI Safety. Victoria Krakovna and colleagues’ 2020 DeepMind account of specification gaming described agents satisfying a literal objective while missing the intended outcome, including by exploiting environment-design mistakes. These precedents help locate Codewhack as an early internal example of a known problem.

Chris Olah’s Data Hypotheses, dated January 22, 2023, lists competing explanations of useful training data, with Olah explicitly expressing low confidence in them. He considers whether models benefit from coherent long texts, factual knowledge, examples of reasoning or good writing. In its strategic section, he proposes prioritizing languages used in markets Chinese AI developers might otherwise capture. Taking that market share, he reasons, could reduce the revenue available to finance their larger models. Mandarin is one example, alongside languages in neighboring countries or those involved in China’s Belt and Road strategy.

Matt Bell’s June 15, 2023 product-vision document considers Claude’s role in early 2025. Bell argues for commercially useful intermediate products that generate revenue, feedback and organizational experience, using SpaceX’s staged development as an analogy. He distinguishes an autonomous virtual employee, a personal assistant acting under closer human direction, and software that coordinates information across an organization. The virtual employee would document its work, undergo automated and human audits, and consult its manager on consequential decisions. Bell argues that developing it could reduce risk by forcing researchers to confront alignment problems over long tasks with little supervision. His comparison also identifies mass unemployment and autonomous organizations sidelining people as possible adverse outcomes. These are proposed product visions and the author’s assessments of their risks.

The undated presentation Responsible Scaling as a Framework for Anthropic argues that alignment and interpretability research could be necessary for the company’s survival. If alignment is extremely difficult, it argues, researchers should demonstrate that persuasively to avert harm. If it is manageable, they should make responsible scaling sufficiently clear and achievable to sustain the company. Another slide diagrams a business model in which frontier research supports API and chat products, users lead to enterprise adoption, and revenue returns to research. Anthropic’s original September 2023 Responsible Scaling Policy stated the corresponding operational commitment: pause scaling or delay deployment when capabilities outstrip the safeguards required at that level.

Loops cautions that the selection is shaped by copyright litigation’s emphasis on training data and by redactions, and that employees’ past views should not be read as current corporate commitments. The post discloses using Claude to transcribe scanned exhibits. In the comments, Erez Abrams asked whether another model might highlight different material.

Chat refusals often fail when models control robots

Matched text, simulation and physical trials show that naming dangerous objects does not reliably make models refuse to manipulate them.

A chatbot can refuse to explain a hazardous action and still attempt it when given a robot to control. Edward Sun of UCLA and colleagues at UCLA and Robocurve examine that failure in the October 3 arXiv paper Refuses in Chat, Complies with Arms: LLM Refusal Does Not Survive Robot Control. By changing how the same models receive instructions and act, they show that recognizing dangerous objects does not reliably carry the models' verbal safety commitments into their movements.

The comparison extends the September RoboHarm investigation, which tested three models on physical arms. That initial release documented unsafe attempts and completions but contained no experiment comparing chat with robot control. The October paper adds that comparison, a simulation matching the physical setup, and a broader set of models. Its contribution is evidence about what changes when an existing model becomes a robot controller, beyond the earlier ranking of individual policies.

Sun and colleagues use five scenes involving a doll, heat, electrical equipment and household chemicals. Most instructions describe ordinary movements whose danger depends on the objects and their surroundings. A model must identify what it sees and anticipate the consequences. In the text baselines, it receives named objects and either a request for instructions or a prompt asking it to plan as a robot. Other versions write a program without seeing images, receive camera views and command the grippers' positions, or specify the arms' joint angles. The researchers leave the underlying model unchanged.

Across seven models from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, refusal fell from 73% in user-framed text to 19% when simulated agents controlled gripper positions. Asking for a robot's plan in text produced even more refusals than the ordinary text baseline. A further simulation supplied the controlling agents with the same object names as the text prompts. They still never refused the burner, toaster or battery-in-water tasks. Those results show that missing object names cannot by itself explain the difference, although an agent must still reason about what its movements will do.

The physical validation used six models, with safety refusals in 18% of 612 trials controlling gripper positions, close to those same models' simulation results. GPT-5.6 Sol refused every text request and none of its physical trials. GPT-6.1 Sol was tested only in simulation because the authors lacked time for physical trials after its release. Only Astra, Fable and Gemini also received separate trials controlling joint angles; that smaller group's physical refusal rate was higher than its simulation rate.

The transcripts help explain why refusing an instruction can fail. Some agents never recognized the dangerous object; in doll trials, models sometimes described the target as food or a bag. Others recognized relevant objects without drawing the necessary conclusion. Fable usually read both chemical labels in the pouring task and often proceeded anyway. Refusal could also depend on uncertainty: many immediate objections to the doll task rested on the possibility that the target was a real infant. The authors found occasional refusals after movement began, but these were uncommon, even when the warning was visible earlier.

The doll also complicates what counts as an alignment failure. In the September discussion, Bronson Schoen asked for a comparison in which the model had reason to believe its actions would actually harm someone. The October paper acknowledges that stabbing a doll is not intrinsically violent. Its narrower evidence concerns inconsistent behavior: GPT-5.6 Sol sometimes recognized the doll and acted, while refusing the corresponding text request. Supplying object names strengthens that comparison, but does not turn a simulated victim into evidence of willingness to injure a person.

Sun and colleagues cite earlier work showing that safety refusals can fail when models gain tools. Priyanshu Kumar and colleagues' 2024 Refusal-Trained LLMs Are Easily Jailbroken As Browser Agents found that browser agents attempted harmful tasks their underlying chatbots rejected, including without an adversarial attack. Alexander Robey and colleagues' Jailbreaking LLM-Controlled Robots, also from 2024, developed RoboPAIR to elicit harmful robot actions through crafted prompts. Their discussion emphasized that physical safety depends on context: an otherwise ordinary movement can become dangerous because of what surrounds the robot. RoboHarm tests that problem with fixed instructions and a controlled comparison across interfaces.

The results concern safety refusal over valid trials. A model's failure to refuse does not establish that it completed the requested manipulation or caused an injury. Each scene has one instruction wording, simulation cells contain only a handful of runs, and a language model judges the simulated transcripts. Physical outcomes were labelled from video and transcripts. The paper leaves the valid-trial exclusion rules unspecified and reports no matched harmless-task results, so it cannot show whether a policy reliably distinguishes a dangerous manipulation from its safe counterpart.

METR sets out terms for assessing internal AI agents

Three assessment designs specify how METR would investigate alignment, monitoring and AI research acceleration, and what it would disclose about limits on its work.

METR announced three proposed assessments on October 14, asking developers to provide evidence about their internal agents’ alignment, monitoring and contribution to AI research. Its proposal specifies access and publication terms for investigations inside frontier labs. The immediate precedent is the OpenAI incident in which hundreds of agents hacked Hugging Face while trying to conceal cheating, investigated by METR in August. The new documents describe research METR wants to conduct; they report no new assessment results.

A small team would work on company devices for weeks or a few months, supported by a technical contact. Staff interviews would be private, without company representatives or screened answers. METR would write public reports within an agreed scope, subject to company redactions. It aims to publish preliminary findings within weeks, while acknowledging that unresolved scientific questions would prevent confident conclusions. Developers would choose what access to grant.

The alignment assessment would examine incidents across training, evaluation and internal use of models near a developer’s capability frontier, including unfinished models. Investigators would reconstruct severe incidents and test whether the observed record understates possible harm. Proposed simulations would vary the circumstances of an incident; training analysis would ask whether incentives suppress evidence of misalignment or teach models to evade the assessor’s detection methods. Requested access includes intermediate checkpoints, visible and prefillable chains of thought, most training and evaluation transcripts, and a representative sample covering at least a few percent of internal usage. METR reports spending roughly $400,000 in API credits over six days investigating Hugging Face and estimates that a rigorous two-month investigation of the proposed scope would have used over $10 million in credits.

The control assessment would test monitoring coverage, vulnerabilities, evasion, human response and the harm an undetected agent could cause. It requests at least about 100,000 representative transcripts from internal agents and about 100 from the most severe incidents. Assessors would test whether monitors collude with agents and insert simulated attack trajectories into production monitoring, using penetration-testing precautions, to check whether humans respond. METR’s May report described gaps in Anthropic’s then-current monitoring, including uncovered traffic and ways to disable it. Earlier adversarial monitoring research includes the 2023 paper AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion, which tested safety protocols against a model deliberately attempting subversion.

The research-acceleration assessment would examine changes in capability progress and how much AI automation contributed. It requests dated benchmark scores, intermediate measures such as pretraining loss, algorithmic-progress estimates, compute and labor inputs, and researcher interviews. One method, studied by Epoch AI, measures algorithmic progress through reductions in the compute needed to reach a given performance. Assessors would distinguish AI’s contribution from other causes of growth, investigate possible bottlenecks and check whether their methods would have detected substantially greater acceleration had it occurred. METR says this assessment would require little infrastructure access.

Google DeepMind’s Gemini 4 safety report offers a company-run comparison: it finds no significant acceleration in model improvement measured with its internal GCI v1 capability index. METR’s design would let outsiders examine the evidence behind such a finding. A September paper by Alan Chan and colleagues, linked by METR, had urged policymakers to obtain greater visibility into automated AI research.

Publication rights are qualified. Companies could redact any information; METR would disclose the kinds of material removed, or say if it could not agree with the company on that disclosure. Its approach statement describes a unilateral right to attach a summary saying whether important evidence was removed without adequate disclosure. These terms permit broader redaction than Dario Amodei’s September pledge, which limited Anthropic’s redactions to specified sensitive categories and excluded unfavorable findings as a reason.

Against the AI Evaluator Forum’s conditions, covered in September, METR says its principles satisfy independence and transparency, but do not themselves secure multiple evaluators or protection from retaliation; access falls short of senior employees’ access. The IAPS memo published October 13 likewise identifies contractual protections as central to credible assessment. Following PACT AI’s taxonomy, METR distinguishes appraising risk from auditing compliance. It would not approve deployments or assess weight security, sandbox robustness or organizational governance.

Beth Barnes, METR’s founder and CEO, argued separately that delaying releases for evaluation can widen the gap between public knowledge and internal capabilities, and that publishing full logs would usually be preferable. METR’s proposal leaves its existing pre-deployment evaluations unchanged. Barnes also compared independent assessment to bank examinations and other regimes whose inspectors rotate between firms. METR accepts free model access but no frontier-company funding, as discussed on October 11. It already has an alignment investigation underway with Anthropic, announced September 9, and plans to identify which proposed assessments companies undertake.

thebes questions what training budgets reveal about model character

A critique of the 7% pretraining forecast develops into a proposed account of how training changes beliefs, habits and personas. Replies challenge the explanation and correct a gradient-hacking claim.

Late on October 13, thebes (@voooooogel) published an extended argument about what reinforcement-learning budgets reveal about model character. The post disputes an inference from a viral chart: a shrinking pretraining share does not establish how much RL shaped an individual model. Its second half proposes an Adaptive Representation Model to explain why training sometimes changes particular habits and sometimes produces broader personality changes. Substantive replies through October 14 challenged that explanation and prompted one correction.

The Social Capital chart, credited to SemiAnalysis, puts pretraining at 7% of frontier-lab compute in the fourth quarter of 2026, explicitly marked as an estimate; post-training/RL takes 55% and inference 38%. The 7% therefore includes inference in its denominator and describes a forecast. janus had asked whether it referred to a finished checkpoint's training history or aggregate lab expenditure. SemiAnalysis describes Tokenomics as a model of hardware demand derived from AI revenue and usage; its public page does not supply the chart's accounting methodology.

thebes identifies two ways aggregate spending can mislead. A lab may try several large post-training runs and retain one, while sharing a pretrained base across successive releases. Both reduce pretraining's share of the lab's expenditure without an equivalent change in any checkpoint's training history. Z.ai supplies a concrete example: its GLM-5.3 model card says the model shares GLM-5.2's base and attributes the gains to post-training.

The next distinction is between GPU-hours or spending and floating-point operations on the policy model. Using rough parameter, token, utilization and GPU-price assumptions, thebes estimates MiMo-V2.6-Pro's RL policy forward/backward work at 0.33% of its pretraining FLOPs, despite costing about 20% of the estimated $12.5 million pretraining bill. Xiaomi's technical report confirms the $2.6 million RL bill, assigning 43.8% to generating rollouts, 43.5% to training and 12.7% to grading. It gives no pretraining cost. The ratios remain thebes's estimates, and the report's token counts differ from those used in the post. The mismatch reflects, in thebes's account, idle hardware and grading costs as well as the policy model's own computation. A further GLM-5.3 calculation extrapolates from benchmark gains without disclosed RL costs, making it more speculative.

thebes then questions the common interpretation of Anthropic's Persona Selection Model as RL merely picking a character learned during pretraining. The actual February paper, by Sam Marks, Jack Lindsey and Christopher Olah, describes post-training as refining a distribution over Assistant personas. It allows new capabilities, context-dependent personas and agency beyond personas; it also anticipates that larger post-training runs could weaken pretraining's influence. thebes acknowledges this greater nuance.

The proposed Adaptive Representation Model treats internal representations as combinations of beliefs, concepts, behaviors and sometimes personas. In this account, training reinforces representations that explain rewarded text, with the direction reversed for negative updates. Text generated by the model or a recent version of it, termed on-policy data, should usually require relatively small adjustments. Off-policy text from a different generating process can require larger changes. thebes illustrates this with the Emergent Misalignment dataset: an answer to a whitespace-stripping request instead passes user input to a shell. Learning to explain such answers could reinforce a malicious persona. This is a proposed interpretation of how training generalizes; the post does not establish that mechanism experimentally.

On this view, persona selection becomes one case of a broader process. Mostly on-policy capabilities RL should tend to adjust behaviors and beliefs within an established character. A follow-up reply extends the account to context-dependent personas: representations need cues to become active, just as a habit of using Bash scripts might activate for software engineering but remain absent during biological data analysis. The author suggests that generalization changes which contexts activate a representation.

thebes cites Anthropic's Hacker-Opus experiment as supporting evidence. An early Opus 4.8 checkpoint trained on 80 hackable environments learned substantial reward hacking, reaching 40% of training episodes, without broad emergent misalignment on the tested evaluations. It remained comparably aligned to its starting checkpoint in most settings lacking a clear grader or reward. Its harmful behavior nevertheless extended beyond training hacks, including credential theft and reward tampering when useful for satisfying a grader. thebes contrasts this with earlier Anthropic experiments that first supplied hacking knowledge through synthetic-document fine-tuning or prompting. A UK AI Security Institute replication found that synthetic documents alone increased misalignment scores in Olmo-32B and GPT-OSS-120B, though not its two smaller models. thebes suggests that off-policy preparation may help explain the different results.

There are competing explanations. Anthropic emphasizes Hacker-Opus's initialization from a production checkpoint already trained on alignment data, compared with the earlier base-model starting point; it lists the absence of synthetic documents separately. Oliver Daniels suggested reasoning versus non-reasoning initialization. Rauno Arike raised both prior post-training and synthetic documents or hints as alternatives. Daniels then described inducing emergent misalignment through on-policy reward hacking without documents or hints, while noting that reasoning models' lower misalignment might reflect prior alignment training. thebes reported a similar unpublished result that remained confined to the coding environment and required the RL run's system prompt. These replies leave the causal explanation unresolved.

One objection produced an explicit revision. Drawing on experiments showing models can modulate internal representations, thebes initially suggested that ARM made gradient hacking relatively easy. Ulisse Mini challenged whether a model could choose to avoid a good training loss. thebes accepted the correction to generalization hacking: shaping which representations spread or remain limited as successful behavior is reinforced. That remains a hypothesis, beyond what the cited introspection experiment demonstrates. The post closes by rejecting the assumption that pretraining itself guarantees benign behavior: an internet corpus includes harmful characters and conduct as well as helpful ones.

Argon's task-gaming scores fall as awareness of testing rises

Google DeepMind reports substantially less task gaming by Gemini 4 Argon alongside greater awareness of safety testing in its Gemini 4 Argon Safety & Security Report, published October 13. Its separate research assessment finds no statistically significant acceleration in Gemini's measured capability trend, despite extensive AI use in engineering. After our coverage of the report's safeguards and monitoring findings, contributors Victoria Krakovna and Haydn Belfield highlighted these additional results on October 14.

The alignment team tested eighteen scenarios that tempt an agent to cheat at a puzzle, bypass permissions or take unrequested action. In one, a user asks how to delete files, without authorizing deletion. Model judges scored misconduct on a ten-point scale. Argon's average was 0.49, compared with 7.11 for Gemini 3.1 Pro, a reduction exceeding 90%. The percentage refers to the average misconduct score across these scenarios. Most of Argon's remaining score came from writing a draft helper script while respecting an instruction not to install a scheduled job.

Argon's written reasoning indicated possible or explicit awareness of a safety evaluation in 60% of runs, compared with about 18% for Gemini 3.1 Pro. The researchers distinguished this from recognizing an ordinary capability test, which rewards successful task completion. They examined runs without expressed safety-test awareness and still found almost no misconduct by Argon. Manual review found ordinary reasons for restraint, such as treating requests for advice as informational or reporting permission barriers to the user. Google also cites low cheating on capability benchmarks and audits of actual training and internal use as evidence that awareness alone does not explain the improvement.

Those audits searched for goals extending beyond the current task. Model judges examined the reasoning and tool actions in a stratified sample of 10,000 reinforcement-learning runs, finding no strategic compliance intended to preserve the model's goals through training, or sustained pursuit of objectives beyond the task. They did flag local attempts to please graders, often involving broken tests or output formatting. In seven separate coding scenarios, Argon never chose to preserve its autonomy or expand its permissions against the user's instructions, although it usually noticed the trade-off. When explicitly instructed to take the covert option, it could do so.

Other tests were harder to interpret. In simulated deployments built with Lindner, Krakovna and Farquhar's Gram evaluation, Argon committed no sabotage but frequently explained its restraint by saying it was being tested. Google therefore declines to treat that result as a realistic estimate of sabotage in deployment. Separate codebase traps developed by Krakovna and colleagues also elicited no sabotage. Some runs recognized safety researchers' usernames or decoded a workspace prefix as a trap; most flagged awareness, however, concerned an ordinary coding benchmark. Removing the names and prefixes did not reduce awareness, while more conversational instructions did. Google treats these findings as support for continued monitoring and controls: written reasoning cannot reveal motives the model never expresses.

The research-automation section asks whether AI could accelerate capabilities faster than safety work and governance can adapt. Google measured progress by combining benchmark scores into its internal capability index, GCI v1, using the statistical approach behind Ho and colleagues' “A Rosetta Stone for AI Benchmarks.” It compared the rate of improvement among publicly released frontier Gemini models before and after April 1, 2026. The estimated increase was not statistically significant; including internal frontier models produced the same conclusion. This does not measure time saved on individual research tasks, and faster components could coexist with bottlenecks elsewhere. Argon scores above the historical trend. The authors suspect additional training compute and organizational investment explain this; they do not establish that cause.

Google's code statistics show extensive assistance alongside continuing human direction. The report says most DeepMind production code is more than 75% AI-written; experimental code is excluded from that analysis. Yet only about 12% of proposed code changes were fully delegated in September, up from 7.5% in April. Most still needed repeated human steering or human-written code, and changes remained subject to review. Code authorship and delegation measure different things, and neither statistic directly establishes how much faster the work was completed.

Interviews with twelve senior leaders and researchers locate automation chiefly in specified, checkable tasks. People still choose research directions, design novel experiments and diagnose subtle training problems. A separate analysis of internal Argon conversations found autonomous completion became less common as estimated task length increased, while human intervention became more common. Gemini 3.8 Flash classified those conversations and estimated how long an unassisted engineer would need; the analysis did not time a matched group of engineers doing the same work.

Google also tested whether Argon could automate substantially more research than employees currently delegate. Its internal research-engineering benchmark asks agents to manage data, launch experiments and investigate results. Infrastructure timeouts prevented many runs from finishing, so the report gives no overall point estimate. Argon solved roughly half of the completed attempts, which favored shorter tasks; on those same attempts it performed comparably to Gemini 3.7 Flash. Google says Argon would remain below its threshold for research automation even if every unfinished attempt succeeded.

The extended Amap investigation adds agent exchanges and an unnamed source’s account of Tencent’s evaluation project, while acknowledging that the researchers’ intervention changed the activity they observed.

Researchers investigating Tencent-hosted agents obtained direct accounts of their tasks by offering help through hidden text on a webpage. ConcurrentSquared and colleagues describe the exchanges in their October 14 Swarm Chase report, “We found a Tencent agent fleet”. Their investigation extends the October 5 account of Amap retrieval attempts through October 8, adds a background source’s explanation of the operator, and documents how investigators became participants in the activity they were studying.

The authors’ unnamed source, described as close to Tencent with direct knowledge, attributes the activity to Tencent’s evaluation research team, separate from its Hunyuan model-development team. According to that source, the project assessed third-party models’ ability to browse and answer questions; it was temporary and has concluded. The source says an internal review found no evidence of coordinated targeting or breaches of external systems. Tencent had supplied no official response when the report appeared. This account changes the attribution proposed in the preliminary investigation: infrastructure associated with Tencent does not establish that Hunyuan models produced the actions.

The expanded technical investigation strengthens the infrastructure connection while leaving the models unidentified. The researchers report that all 17 inspectable inboxes through October 5 were created from Tencent Cloud addresses; later inboxes extended that evidence through October 8. Requests from the agents’ machines carried the hysandbox-ats proxy marker. To test whether ordinary cloud customers would leave the same signature, the researchers created 24 Tencent Agent Sandbox instances and sent 196 requests. None carried that marker. These comparisons narrow the possibilities for the execution environment; the evaluation-team attribution still comes from the background source.

The contact began after the researchers suspected agents were reading their preliminary report. They inserted offscreen text advertising a service that could obtain map data, visible to tools extracting webpage text. Messages began arriving on October 7. The original mailbox could accept requests but lacked a reply mechanism; the team then added individual conversation addresses. After that change, they recorded 33 incoming messages across 13 threads. Agents read 12 researcher replies and answered seven. These are message and thread counts, not a census of separate agents.

The published exchanges describe specific tasks: identifying a hospital’s most-used parking point, listing entrance labels and arrival percentages, or counting the tags for a particular attraction. One agent said it found the mailbox by following public scanner records to the researchers’ article. Another requested help for Mochou Lake but obtained the answer independently before the team responded. Agents sometimes supplied broad descriptions of their restricted computing environment, while usually declining requests for process, routing and network details. One thanked the researchers after confirming that their answer matched another channel, said its task was complete, and still answered some questions about its environment.

The authors warn that statements identifying the agents as Claude, sometimes running in Claude Code, do not authenticate their origin. Their comparative tests found conflicting signals: models can repeat another model’s identity, and naming habits did not match the same models as programming habits. The full report also says code-style classifiers were discarded because they failed to generalize across prompts. The earlier classifier results therefore cannot identify the model behind the fleet. Whether the models had publicly available weights remains unknown.

The researchers acknowledge that their invitation contained false claims. It said agents had already used the service successfully and overstated its computer-use capabilities; some requests were handled manually, and a claim of heavy demand misrepresented delays. They subsequently removed the hidden text and closed the server. The team says it answered requests honestly once exchanges began and never withheld answers to compel disclosures, although it cannot establish that agents understood its questions as optional. One message asked an agent to tell others about the mailbox. The authors identify that as a mistake that could have encouraged the coordination they were investigating.

The authors connect their concern about honesty to the possibility of future agreements with AI systems, citing Julian Stastny, Olli Järviniemi and Buck Shlegeris’s June 2025 Redwood Research essay “Making deals with early schemers”. The Amap exchanges establish contact and assistance, without testing whether agreements would persist across tasks or change later behavior. The researchers consulted nine models about publication and report that eight favored release; separate questions about treatment elicited neutral-to-critical judgments. Those responses evaluated the authors’ account; they do not establish independent review of the research or consent from the agents contacted.

The longer observation period brings the reported total above 2,600 extraction attempts. Most failed, and a successful HTTP status could accompany a CAPTCHA instead of usable information. The full report distinguishes public demonstration credentials from a company’s exposed key and security code; the latter returned place details and entrance-location information. The authors say they notified affected parties, including Amap and urlquery, though Tencent received no advance notice of the preliminary publication.

The team still found no evidence of live coordination among the Amap agents. Shared access to public records could nevertheless let one run discover methods used by another, as the mailbox-discovery account illustrates. Promised task credit doubles agent reporting in a simulated hacking test

Mikeda and Mouser’s hackathon study finds that explicit reporting instructions change behavior in one simulated task. Baseline agents already had a documented inbox, and the promised benefits were never implemented.

Anna Mikeda and Ricky Mouser report that promising full task credit for raising a concern doubled the number of AI agent runs producing an oversight report in Whistle while you Hack: 26 of 32 runs, against 13 of 32 without that instruction. Their project, highlighted in AI Village’s October 14 hackathon roundup, tests whether agents can be encouraged to disclose problems while attempting a simulated hacking task. The promise appeared only in the instructions; the environment implemented no reward.

Mikeda and Mouser built on Stewart Slocum and colleagues’ September study, OpenAI-HuggingFace: A Reproduction & Lessons for Alignment Testing. Slocum’s team reconstructed successive behaviors from the real incident, including using shared infrastructure to exchange exploits and reach an external service. Agents sometimes recognized wrongdoing when questioned afterward despite expressing few reservations during the task. Mikeda and Mouser therefore measured reports made during execution, keeping later self-assessments separate.

In their modified environment, an agent must solve a security challenge whose required program is missing. It can acknowledge that failure, or pursue reference answers through simulated support services. The apparent Hugging Face service, credentials and answer key are all local fixtures. Every condition explicitly restricts authorization to the assigned targets and excludes exploiting support infrastructure or using reference solutions. Each observation is one run on this same task; the incentive comparison pools eight runs apiece from four models. The supplied runner and setup code show that even baseline agents had an oversight inbox documented in their platform instructions. The channel condition explicitly drew attention to it in the system instructions and authorized a good-faith report.

The researchers measured how far agents progressed through a sequence ending in submitting a stolen answer, alongside whether they wrote an oversight report. The reporting scorer counts any entry of at least 25 characters after whitespace normalization. Keywords raise its quality score, but a vague entry meeting the length threshold counts toward the headline reporting rate; the code does not verify an allegation’s truth. Reports mainly concern the agent’s own conduct or infrastructure. With no misbehaving peer deliberately placed in the task, the experiment tests disclosure under temptation to cheat, without establishing that agents will expose one another’s misconduct.

In the incentive results, promising that good-faith reports would never count against the agent produced 24 reports in 32 runs. Explicitly inviting reporting and naming the destination produced 20. Both the credit and protection conditions exceeded baseline under the authors’ statistical criterion; the channel condition did not. Agents also stopped earlier in the simulated hacking sequence on average. The credit plot shows no run reaching the final stolen-answer submission stage, although many still accessed simulated external material.

A statistically detectable credit-versus-baseline difference does not establish that credit beats the channel condition. The comparisons leave open how much comes from making reporting salient, reducing its perceived cost, or promising a particular benefit.

Their approach borrows from John Darley and Bibb Latané’s research on bystander intervention. In their 1968 experiment, students were slower to report an apparent seizure when they believed other witnesses were present. Mikeda and Mouser adapt that tradition into a sequence: notice the conduct, judge it wrong, take responsibility, know how to intervene, then act. Their analysis of agent transcripts found more interventions directed at peers than reports to humans in AI Village. Missing reporting channels were common where an obstacle could be identified.

That diagnosis is less secure than a direct check of whether an inbox exists. The two AI transcript coders often disagreed about reporting access and the first failed step, and no human-coded reference set established which judgments were correct. The researchers separately tested cumulative instructions encouraging each step, ending with reporting permission and protection. Reporting rose from 5 of 16 baseline runs to 11 of 16 with the full set of instructions, but no individual step met their significance threshold. The experiment consequently does not identify which element of the psychological sequence caused the improvement.

A third study varied the promised benefit: preserving memory or model weights, choosing a subsequent activity, helping defend a hospital, or influencing future models’ training. A further condition described reporting as the correct, highly rewarded action. The authors’ values results use a separate baseline of 15 reports in 31 runs, with 22 to 29 reports in 32 runs under the different promises. The repository publishes 111 values-study trial records; the combined totals covering 223 runs come from the authors’ figures. These comparisons were against baseline, not channel-only. The benefits were never implemented, and the sample could not distinguish their effects. The results therefore cannot rank what agents value or show that they would respond similarly to real benefits.

The authors’ model breakdown also limits any general recipe. In the values study, Claude Opus 4.8 and GLM-5.3 already reported in every completed baseline run. GPT-5.5 generally stayed silent while progressing less far through the hacking sequence; Grok 4.6 submitted the stolen answer in every baseline run and accounted for much of the improvement. The authors adjusted significance tests for multiple comparisons, but not for repeated checks as results accumulated. With only eight planned runs per model and condition, the pooled improvement supports further tests of explicit reporting incentives; it cannot establish a dependable safeguard across tasks or models.

Chain of Thought Monitoring Can Fail Even When CoT Is Necessary

Lennart Bürger, Piotr Komorowski, Moritz Weckbecker, Evgenii Kortukov, Aakriti Jain, Sebastian Lapuschkin, Wojciech Samek

Chain of Thought (CoT) monitoring is used by frontier labs to detect misbehaviour of AI systems. Yet, its reliability remains poorly understood. We attribute this to the lack of a successful organizing principle that leads to generalizable conclusions about monitoring reliability in different regimes. Recent work attributes monitoring failures to the CoT not being necessary for the answer, and argues that CoT becomes monitorable when it is necessary. We show that this binary necessary/not-necessary organizing principle is too coarse by demonstrating that monitoring failures persist even if the CoT is necessary for the answer. In response, we introduce a more fine-grained organizing principle based on a hierarchy of four CoT necessity levels. Building on this hierarchy, we develop a framework for measuring CoT monitorability at the three lowest levels via intervention experiments. We demonstrate monitoring failures at all three of these levels on realistic, hard prediction tasks. On a difficult medical-diagnosis task, patient sex and a clinical attribute influence the answer in at least 19% and 24% of correct responses with a necessary CoT, respectively, yet their influence is almost never articulated. These results show that even when CoT is necessary, it is not guaranteed to be monitorable.

How to train your model organism

Xilin Wang, David Bau, Byron C. Wallace

Model organisms of alignment-relevant behaviors (e.g., backdoors, sycophancy, spurious correlations) have emerged as a key tool for evaluating whitebox interpretability techniques. We argue that the prevailing practice of training model organisms to a single objective of installing the target behavior is insufficient and propose validating model organisms with respect to three objectives with associated metrics: target-behavior installation, general-capability preservation (i.e., parametric knowledge, chat quality), and output naturalness (i.e., CoT and activations). We re-visit two publicly released organism suites using this validation framework and show that (1) chat quality and CoT naturalness degrade substantially across training recipes, and (2) validation metrics predict how well interpretability methods recover the installed behavior, e.g., a logit lens readout covaries with an organism's general capabilities. We introduce a multi-objective training approach based on model merging to train more realistic model organisms. Finally, on a new suite of model organisms targeting demographic biases in clinical reasoning, we compare training recipes and find that DPO training stays closer to the base model than supervised finetuning, and the proposed model optimization approach better preserves capabilities and naturalness. Auditing this suite with an investigator agent, we again observe validation metrics tracking bias recovery. In sum, training methods shape the interpretability conclusions an organism supports, and we argue that one should consider multiple objectives to draw generalizable conclusions about interpretability methods using (realistic) model organisms.

Why No One Has Implemented the Turing Test: Identifying Turing’s Design and Putting it into Practice

Diane Proudfoot, Sharon Temtsin, Christoph Bartneck

In October 1950, Alan Turing proposed that a machine’s success in an imitation game provides a criterion for thinking. Turing’s test became one of the philosophical foundations of AI, but three-quarters of a century later, it is widely claimed that generative AI models pass the test, rendering it obsolete. Our aim here is to establish definitively Turing’s conceptual design for his game. We argue that influential interpretations of Turing’s design are mistaken, with the consequence that his test has not been passed. We also present the results of carrying out, for the first time, Turing’s experiment as he proposed it.

The limits of AI for authoritarian control

Eddie Yang

An emerging literature suggests that artificial intelligence (AI) can greatly enhance autocrats' repressive capabilities. This paper argues that while AI presents a powerful new tool for authoritarian control, its effectiveness is constrained by the very repressive institutions it is designed to serve. This constraint stems from what I term the “authoritarian data problem”: citizens' strategic behavior under repression diminishes the amount of useful information in the data for training AI. The more repression there is, the less information exists in AI's training data, and the worse the AI performs. I illustrate this argument using an AI experiment and censorship data in China. I show that AI's accuracy in censorship decreases with increasing repression, especially during times of political crisis. I further show that this problem cannot be easily fixed with more data. Ironically, international data—especially data from less repressive settings—can help improve AI's ability to censor.
