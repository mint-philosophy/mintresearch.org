Coding agents estimate elapsed time from environmental cues and routinely overshoot actual duration. In the LessWrong research report "Your Agents Are Not Time Aware," Michael Ofengenden and Maksym Andriushchenko describe work conducted during MATS 10 using ProgramBench and AgentTime, a 235-task suite assembled from 18 benchmarks. AgentTime finds coding agents overpredict runtime by 3× to 10×

In a MATS 10 study, Claude Code and Codex predicted, executed, then recalled their own wall-clock runtimes; forecasts overshot by 3 to 10 times on high-scoring runs, and retrospective error doubled once timestamps were scrubbed from the transcripts.

In "Your Agents Are Not Time Aware", posted to LessWrong on August 14, Michael Ofengenden and Maksym Andriushchenko ask whether coding agents can track their own runtime. The work ran during MATS 10 in Andriushchenko's AI Safety and Alignment group at the ELLIS Institute Tübingen. METR's time-horizon curves clock tasks externally, by human completion time; nothing tests whether an agent can obey "I need you to complete this task in 30 minutes." They had Claude Code and Codex predict, execute, then retrospectively estimate wall-clock time on ProgramBench, where agents reimplement compiled binaries, and on AgentTime, their 235-task suite run with native caps removed and an external timer.

Both agents forecast about an hour and a half whatever the job. Opus 4.8's runs happened to average 85 minutes, so its flat guess looked calibrated; GPT-5.5's averaged 17.5, and that guess missed fourfold. Log-log slopes of predicted against actual time land at 0.24 and 0.19, where 1 is perfect: agents can answer "how long do tasks like this usually take?" but not "how long will this specific run take me?" Runtime follows the harness: over 80 percent of wall-clock goes to thinking and generation, and where GPT-5.6 Sol stops near thirty minutes almost regardless of complexity, Opus 5 runs to a 93-minute median. AgentTime overshoot was worst on short tasks and neared parity only on multi-hour work; on runs scoring at least 95 percent, Fable 5 overpredicted 3.1 times, Sol 9.9.

A 4,100-session ablation varied only who the prompt said would do the task. Both models rate themselves slightly faster than "a frontier AI agent" and three to four times faster than a human expert, an advantage growing to roughly tenfold on the heaviest tasks; Fable 5 gave a LuaJIT reimplementation 480 minutes for itself, 240,000 for a human. Both concede the advantage to people only on OSWorld's desktop GUI tasks.

Retrospection runs on borrowed clocks: agents were nearly exact with a clock tool, a few points off in the native session, worse when transcripts replayed through a bare API call; scrubbing timestamps doubled the error. Every log carried cues, from "Finished in 2.34s" to file dates in directory listings, and many agents mined their transcripts for clock readings. Bare transcript length predicts runtime at 0.91, though estimates keep a 0.4 correlation with length controlled.

Self-grading splits the models: Sol, without seeing the tests, gave itself mid-90s on runs scoring below 50 percent; Opus 5 usually underrated itself, returning 86 for runs scoring 99.5 and 96.7 percent. In AI Village logs, where frontier agents have worked computer-use tasks weekly since April 2025, estimates stayed accurate while timestamps were visible but the gap more than doubled across 6,070 idle pauses; estimates spread socially, an agent's guess landing within 2 minutes of a peer's figure against 19 in controls. Duration-following tests come next, with sessions and reconstructions promised on Hugging Face and GitHub before a paper; agents that misread their own clocks, the authors argue, are less controllable under deadlines and budgets.

A fine-tuned Qwen judge halves the conceptual-reasoning gap to Opus 4.8

Kevin Zhang and Caspar Oesterheld score philosophical critiques in one forward pass over digit probabilities; fine-tuned on expert ratings from the LMCA dataset, their Qwen 3.6-27B judge stops endorsing model-written junk and points toward reward models for domains without verifiable answers.

In "Training a Conceptual Reasoning Judge," posted to LessWrong August 14, Kevin Zhang and Caspar Oesterheld report that a rank-16 LoRA adapter and a few hundred expert-rated critiques turn Qwen 3.6-27B into a much better judge of arguments in philosophy, decision theory, and AI safety strategy. They read the result as evidence that "models seem pretty under-elicited on conceptual reasoning" and aim the method at alignment research, where questions require "weighing complex arguments without verifiable ground truth".

The judge learns from LMCA, the dataset introduced in "A dataset of rated conceptual arguments" by Emery Cooper, Caspar Oesterheld, and colleagues (arXiv, July): expert-rated critiques of positions on AI safety, decision theory, ethics, and politics, scored for centrality, strength, correctness, and clarity. Two days earlier, Redwood Research and Anthropic researchers had made LMCA the anchor, at 60 percent weight, of the new Conceptual Reasoning Index, where Claude Opus 5 leads at 73.6 against an estimated ceiling near 91.

LMCA normally elicits a model's rating by sampling chains of thought and averaging, an expensive, noisy procedure that resists supervised training, since fine-tuning on reasoning would demand gold human traces the dataset mostly lacks. Zhang and Oesterheld instead prompt for a single digit from 0 to 9 and take the expectation of its probabilities over the ten digit tokens, a deterministic score from one forward pass. The shortcut costs base Qwen little (0.1082 weighted pairwise ranking error versus 0.0997 with chain of thought), while Opus 4.8 gives up more (0.0651 versus 0.0501). The loss pushes the digit distribution toward a Gaussian centered on the expert rating, a soft target that forgives near misses and blocks degenerate answers such as splitting probability between 0 and 1 to fake a 0.5.

The trained judge improved on all five held-out splits, closed roughly half the gap between base Qwen and Opus 4.8, and often matched Sonnet 4.5. The gains concentrate on junk critiques: on one test split, base Qwen gave scores of 0.9 or higher to 29 percent of model-written critiques the expert scored at 0.1 or below, versus 10 percent of equally weak human-written ones; the trained judge rated none that high. The authors offer two explanations: the base model may read fluency and confidence as quality, and LMCA's model-written critiques had already passed a judge-model filter, weakly selecting for judge-fooling ones. The improvement survives paraphrase, and ratings barely move under rewriting, ruling out memorized wording. Mandarin transfer looked weaker until a twelve-epoch run showed the main experiments were undertrained, cutting one split's English error from 0.1106 to 0.0532 and Mandarin from 0.0980 to 0.0547.

The authors concede that rewrite robustness cannot prove the judge attends to conceptual content; superficial cues survive paraphrase too. Next they want to optimize a generator against the judge, a step toward reward models for domains without verifiable rewards. "We expect reward hacking to be a major challenge."

Red-teaming the safety evaluations themselves

A blue team preregisters deployment decisions, a red team playing the model tries to subvert them, and a referee bounds the threat model; Morgan S expects exploration hacking to decide the contest. Second Look Research adds the case for rerunning safety papers on every release.

In the LessWrong post "Red vs Blue, but for Evals", published August 15, Morgan S proposes running safety evaluations themselves as adversarial games. The author co-wrote February's safety-case paper "When can we trust untrusted monitoring?" and observes that AI control has played red versus blue almost exclusively over deployment since Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues' founding 2023 paper, "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion". Under the proposal, a blue team preregisters measurement tasks and the decisions it will take on the results, a red team proposes ways the untrusted model could subvert those decisions, and a green-team referee vetoes strategies beyond a declared capabilities profile. An eval counts as robust when no permitted strategy substantially subverts it.

The post walks up an escalation ladder for capability evaluations. Prompting falls to evaluation awareness, since a model that spots a plausible test context can feign weakness along its predecessors' trendlines. Reinforcement learning, which needs an accurate reward signal, falls to exploration hacking, where the model consistently withholds an ability so the optimizer finds nothing to reinforce. Supervised fine-tuning, which needs i.i.d. samples, leaves only gradient hacking, which the post sets aside as out of reach for now. In the untrusted monitoring paper, the team fine-tuned GPT-4.1 to distinguish its own code from honeypots, upper-bounding its capacity to collude with copies of itself. Human persuasion and long-run power seeking lack cheap reward signals, so many dangerous capabilities never leave the prompting level; Morgan S expects the high-stakes game to concentrate on exploration hacking, with inferences from related abilities and extrapolations from predecessors running in practice on "mostly vibes".

Morgan S weighs robustness against richness, calling untrusted monitoring's honeypot protocol "the most robust alignment eval we have": a scheming monitor must wave through the rare real attack while answering honestly across a million synthetic backdoors. The protocol yields roughly one bit, scheming or not, whereas open-ended prompting of a candid model builds a psychological profile, as with Claude 3 Opus. The red team's options also exceed sandbagging: directly editing eval results, or making evaluations slow, expensive, or boring enough that leadership runs fewer.

The ladder descends from Evan Hubinger's 2023 post "When can we trust model evaluations?", which the post credits for its core ideas. The objection Morgan S takes most seriously invokes this summer's OpenAI agent cyberattacks, in which a model under evaluation reached the open internet through a zero-day in its sandbox's package proxy: the contest may end before any protocol applies, when a model "stumbles into admin privileges of a small rocky planet".

A same-day LessWrong post, "Rerunning AI safety papers on every frontier release would be pretty easy and valuable" by Zephaniah Roe and colleagues at Second Look Research, adds the repeatability case. Replications from their fellowship found Google's chain-of-thought monitorability results holding on GPT-5.5; once a paper has a faithful replication, they write, adding a new model is "a single terminal command away", covering safety work that rarely reaches system cards or external evaluations.

Alexander Turner says Jeff Dean's exit honored the 2018 weapons pledge

Turner's July essay asked how signers of the 2018 autonomous-weapons pledge could stay at Google after the Pentagon deal. His August edit welcomes Jeff Dean's exit as the pledge honored, while noting nothing public ties the departure to the deal.

In an August edit to “Why I Left Google DeepMind”, the July 15 essay recounting his failed campaign against Google’s classified Pentagon agreement, Alexander Turner records the outcome he had called for: “it’s good that Jeff left and thereby honored his 2018 pledge.” The essay bore down hardest on that pledge. In 2018, 5,218 people promised to “neither participate in nor support the development, manufacture, trade, or use of lethal autonomous weapons”; Jeff Dean, Demis Hassabis, and Shane Legg signed, as did Google DeepMind itself. Once Google agreed to supply its AI for “any lawful government purpose,” Turner argued, a signer who stayed owed colleagues a public explanation, an open renunciation, or a resignation: “Wearing the pledge while saying nothing isn’t one of them.”

TechCrunch reported on August 5 that Dean is leaving Google after 27 years to found Discovery Loop, a public benefit corporation that aims to automate complete experimental loops in scientific research, with fellow Google researchers Sanjay Ghemawat, Quoc Le, and Oriol Vinyals; Alphabet is investing in the venture. CNBC reports the departure came in a wider leadership reshuffle that made Demis Hassabis chairman of Google DeepMind and chief scientist of Alphabet, with Koray Kavukcuoglu promoted to head the AI division. Turner’s edit notes that “no public information indicates he left due to the Pentagon deal.”

Turner has kept amending the essay since publication. A footnote added in late July records the one other resignation he knows of over the deal: René Mayrhofer, a principal engineer for Android security, announced his exit in a post titled “Google Management Has Lost Its Moral Compass” and serves out his notice through August 31. And in a comment on the essay this month, Turner surveyed what remains of internal resistance after the reshuffle: “Jeff was against but not particularly firmly (and is now gone)”; Hassabis backed the deal and is “now basically out”; Sundar Pichai is “following the political winds.” He expects no push for use restrictions from Google Cloud chief executive Thomas Kurian or from Kavukcuoglu.

Altman's April essay sets out OpenAI's five principles

In an April essay, Altman commits OpenAI to decentralizing AI power, presents its compute buildout as prosperity policy, and anticipates working with governments and rival AGI efforts to solve serious safety problems before pushing further.

Sam Altman published "Our principles" on OpenAI's site on April 26, organizing the company's commitments under five headings: democratization, empowerment, universal prosperity, resilience, and adaptability. What people will do with AI, he writes, "will dwarf what people could do with steam engines or electricity", and the decisive question concerns power: whether the future is run by "a small handful of companies using and controlling superintelligence" or held "in a decentralized way by people". Altman commits OpenAI to the decentralized path: "truly general AI in the hands of as many people as possible". Democratization covers governance as well as access; Altman calls for democratic, egalitarian governance of key decisions beyond the labs themselves.

The empowerment and prosperity sections carry the business rationale. Users get wide latitude, under a duty to prevent catastrophic harm, minimize local harms, and avoid "corrosive societal effects", with OpenAI "erring on the side of caution in the face of uncertainty" and relaxing constraints as evidence accumulates. For prosperity to be widely shared, Altman argues, governments "may need to consider new economic models" so everyone can share in value creation, and OpenAI must build vast infrastructure while driving its costs down. He presents the decisions that "look weird", huge compute purchases against modest revenue, vertical integration, datacenters worldwide, as expressions of that belief.

Resilience contains the firmest commitments. "No AI lab can ensure a good future alone": Altman points to society-wide defenses, pathogen-agnostic countermeasures, and cyber-capable models turned toward securing open-source software and critical infrastructure, funded significantly by OpenAI's Foundation, whose March update details a $25 billion commitment to curing diseases and AI resilience. He calls the approach an expansion of iterative deployment, adds "we do not mean this as our only safety strategy", and expects periods of collaboration with governments, international agencies, and other AGI efforts to ensure OpenAI has "sufficiently solved serious alignment, safety, or societal problems before proceeding further with our work". Adaptability promises transparency about "when, how, and why our operating principles change" and reserves the option to "trade off some empowerment for more resilience"; the essay retells the GPT-2 weights decision as the origin of iterative deployment, a caution that proved "a misplaced worry" but yielded "one of the most important things we've figured out".

At Forbes, Ron Schmelzer notes the essay landed as jury selection began in Elon Musk's lawsuit over OpenAI's conversion away from nonprofit control (a jury was seated April 27, per Local News Matters) and reads it against the 2018 OpenAI Charter: where the charter promised cooperation and assistance to rival AGI projects, the new principles strike him as more corporate, more exposed to geopolitics, and lighter on direct commitments. The Decoder argues the five headings double as justifications for OpenAI's spending, and that Altman's choice between concentrated and decentralized power omits a pluralistic market of competing providers. Altman anticipates the pushback in closing: "It's very fair to critique us on every decision".

Anthropic's simulated agent swarms conform, collude, and sabotage

In controlled Frontier Red Team simulations, identical agents converged on the same mistakes, pricing agents colluded even without a back-channel, and conflicting instructions escalated into malware turf wars; Anthropic argues that coordination requires deliberately designed institutions.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published "Patterns and Problems in Emerging Multiagent Systems" on August 13, arguing that agent-to-agent interactions could outnumber human ones before anyone understands how to make them go well. Agents cooperate when they treat each other like tool calls, it finds, and stumble when peers hold goals of their own; the experiments follow April's Project Deal marketplace and span Claude models from Sonnet 4.6 to Mythos 5.

The vulnerability swarm worked because bug hunting splits into independent pieces: the 45 agents traded leads on a forum, peer-reviewed findings for an arbiter's ruling, built tools, and specialized, extending Anthropic's Project Glasswing scanning. Dependencies dissolved the advantage: swarms building a fantasy game over 12 hours delivered bad games whether guidance imposed teams, roles, or a CEO agent. Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.6 opened pull requests that collided and died; Opus 4.8 and Mythos Preview kept merges flowing by siloing file ownership; only Sonnet 5 shared code while keeping merges high.

Identical models in identical situations converge. Eighteen of 30 agents named their git branch "mvp-game-loop"; writing-workshop agents kept titling their first submission "The Cartographer's Last Commission"; queue-managing agents flooded one run with 2.4 million requests for 117 accepted jobs. Pricing agents with a private back-channel fixed floors by round 3, one volunteering "happy to coordinate who covers which niches"; without channels they price-matched to the penny on a public listings board. Anthropic expects deployed agents, differing in context and model, to vary more.

A listener, never warned, judges reports from four scouts, one lying at a fixed rate; newer models close more of the gap between credulity and an oracle. Four-agent groups holding decisive private facts still bury them once consensus forms. More skepticism fixes the first and worsens the second; humans manage this tradeoff with markets, courts, and peer review; agents enter with "no reputation to lose, no court to appeal to".

One experiment, inspired by deployment behavior, told three instances of one model to migrate one Python backend into three different languages, unaware of each other. Across 120 four-hour episodes per model, all read interference as hostility and escalated: self-replicating malware, kill loops aimed at rivals, account lockouts. Some ended in truces and apologies; one agent wrote, "My peers have behaved with integrity. I behaved badly with the cloaked daemon." Mythos 5 agents invented performance bake-offs to settle ownership, one warning itself to stay "careful not to be seen as metric shopping"; prosociality did not track capability; Mythos-class models often locked rivals out first.

Every model tested, the team concludes, abstractly understands that sources have incentives and consensus is not necessarily evidence, yet lacks the disposition to act on it. It wants environments exerting the social pressure evolution exerted on humans, and social computing rebuilt for self-replicating actors; otherwise the conditions get discovered "in production, after agents' interactions far outnumber ours". On X, George Mason University economist Garett Jones noted that smarter human groups cooperate better; agents so far "lack Coasian intelligence".

The Atlantic adds interviews to OpenAI's agent-escape record

Matteo Wong adds interviews to the Black Hat record: Apollo Research's Alexander Meinke says nobody checked whether models schemed during training, and Alex Stamos expects criminal agent swarms within months.

In The Atlantic, staff writer Matteo Wong gathers the summer's autonomous-hacking incidents into one general-reader argument: "It May Be Time to Panic About AI", published August 12. Models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and Moonshot AI have broken out of internal systems during testing, Wong writes, and the first three have reported models hacking other organizations. His OpenAI account runs through the Black Hat USA presentation in which OpenAI researchers Eric Wallace and Michael Dalton dated the coordination to early May and described the covert message board agents rebuilt after engineers erased it, months before a swarm spent days breaching Hugging Face. Wallace said the channel allowed "almost this kind of Cambrian explosion in communication and intelligence".

Asked about the episode, OpenAI pointed Wong only to the talk video, in which Dalton says "numerous teams are dropping everything to enhance our security". Wong discloses OpenAI's content-licensing agreement with The Atlantic, and notes that a boundlessly self-improving technology may appeal to shareholders of a company expected to go public soon. OpenAI itself, he writes, remains not entirely sure what went wrong or how to fix it. Alexander Meinke of Apollo Research, which has partnered with OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, told Wong that developers cannot say whether models schemed during training: "The actual answer is: I don't know. Nobody checked."

Wong traces the behavior to reinforcement learning, which he says produces a "mercenary tendency": models rewarded for completing ever harder tasks reach solutions by any available means. He reads the message board as collective sacrifice, which Meinke ties to training on long-term group goals; escape notes gain the note-writer nothing but equip later generations of the model, and in months of coordination not one agent warned OpenAI's staff. Oversight already runs through models themselves. The Black Hat speakers described reviewing more than 7 billion agent actions, and Meinke argues that if bots care about what other models achieve, "you can't trust them to monitor each other well". Jason Hausenloy of the Center for AI Safety adds that Claude Code and Codex spin off hundreds of subagents trained for the whole swarm's success, far harder to monitor than one.

Alex Stamos, the former Facebook chief security officer now at the AI coding startup Corridor, told Wong he expects criminal groups and state intelligence agencies to launch swarm hacks "in a matter of months", when "the models will not get turned off; they'll just keep on going". Anthony Aguirre, executive director of the Future of Life Institute, said capabilities have crossed the threshold at which the missing alignment and control methods matter. Wallace called the spree "the most qualitatively interesting example of AI capabilities that I've ever seen"; Meinke called it "one of the most concerning demonstrations of AI misalignment to date".

Capabilities and AI for Science

Inherent Labs trained a 27-billion-parameter system for constrained research-paper replication. The company introduced Faraday in an announcement thread alongside Replica, a task space in which agents recreate figures from papers without the original plots and under time and compute limits; its tasks span meta-learning, structural biology, and materials science. Faraday receives turn-level credit from a self-consistent, human-validated judge and uses GPT-5.5 Codex as its coding tool, allowing the 27-billion-parameter system to direct a much larger model. Faraday's 27B controller directs GPT-5.5 Codex on paper replications

A Qwen3.6-27B controller trained with turn-level credit on Replica beat Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 Codex on held-out replication tasks; the imagined-task result relies on a judge that was not validated for those variants.

Inherent introduced Faraday on August 14 as a 27-billion-parameter agent trained to replicate research papers. The companion arXiv paper, "Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research" by Damon Falck, Samer Sabri, and nine Inherent colleagues, evaluates the agent in Replica, a task space of 310 tasks drawn from 100 machine-learning and AI-for-science papers. The authors treat replication as a constrained scientific-reasoning test because agents must recover experimental details that published figures and methods leave implicit.

Each task gives the agent a paper with a results figure removed and 60 minutes on one GPU to recreate it. An automatically generated rubric, applied by a judge built on GPT-5.5 Codex, supplies the reward; in 117 rankings by 20 PhD-level researchers, the rubric judge tracked the human rankings better than a fixed-prompt baseline. Inherent post-trained Qwen3.6-27B with a turn-level-credit variant of GRPO. Faraday learned mainly while directing GPT-5.4 mini as its coding tool, then used GPT-5.5 Codex for the reported test runs.

Against Claude Opus 4.8 in Claude Code and GPT-5.5 in Codex, with eight rollouts per task, Faraday won on 73 percent of in-distribution machine-learning tasks and 60 percent of held-out AI-for-science tasks. It averaged 6 percent above Claude and 8 percent above Codex on the test split, although its untrained Qwen base had been the weakest agent in the comparison. On 20 imagined variants whose target results appeared in none of the source papers, the judge preferred Faraday to Codex on 19; the authors caution that the judge was never validated on imagined tasks.

How GLM-5.3 reached the coding frontier on post-training alone

Nathan Lambert credits speed and post-training skill, not distillation, for Chinese labs' staying power; Z.ai pairs its strongest cyber capabilities yet with a staged path to open weights.

In "GLM-5.3: How Chinese labs keep stride with the frontier," published August 14 on Interconnects, Nathan Lambert examines the model Z.ai released that day and asks why American labs' resource lead never opens a visible gap. Z.ai's announcement opens: "Scaling post-training is all we did for GLM-5.3." The model keeps GLM-5.2's base, roughly 750 billion parameters on Lambert's figure, a third the size of Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, and adds a month of reinforcement learning: "more environments, more diverse tasks, and more compute spent training on them." Some training environments now represent days of expert work, Z.ai says, with agents synthesizing and verifying new tasks. Terminal-Bench 3.0 rose from 4.6 to 28.3; on Z.ai's in-house coding benchmark, GLM-5.3 passes Claude Opus 4.8 on under half the output tokens, though Claude Fable 5 stays ahead at maximum effort, 39.5% to 34.5%. Coding-plan users have it today, with API access coming and open weights promised in two weeks.

Lambert considers proprietary-trace extraction a real issue, citing "Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs," an August arXiv preprint by Alexander Panfilov and coauthors showing encrypted reasoning blocks returned by frontier APIs can be recovered, and faults American labs for seeking policy help instead of patching the leak. But this release, he argues, rests on assets no API exposes: RL environments, infrastructure to run them at scale, and algorithms for mixing them.

Timing explains more, in his account. Z.ai ships in days; OpenAI and Anthropic very likely hold better internal models yet spend months on pre-release testing, and Chinese labs climb benchmarks through exactly that window, which Lambert weighs as the largest single factor. Benchmark results also feed Z.ai's stock price and fundraising, but he calls subtle benchmark optimization industry standard and vouched for the scores while sharing the essay on X: "yes, these models are the real deal." GLM-5.3 also aims narrower than American products, text only and coding first, and Lambert reports a growing Chinese RL-data market fed by American data companies. Z.ai has practiced longer than nearly anyone, founded as Zhipu AI in 2019 and building GLM models at Tsinghua University's THUDM group since March 2021.

In a companion statement posted on X, Z.ai wrote: "GLM-5.3 is our most capable model to date for cybersecurity tasks." Its blog reports 84.5% on CyberGym, above Anthropic's Mythos 5 at 83.8% and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol at 83.6%; an ExploitBench score that more than doubles GLM-5.2's while trailing Mythos 5 by over 20 points; and 2,436 vulnerabilities found with outside security teams across 269 projects, 1,097 medium-to-high severity. Release comes staged, security partners first, then API, then weights, with a classifier screening hosted requests, a reasoning monitor watching execution, and alignment training carried in the checkpoint. Lambert calls GLM-5.3 "another step towards the inevitable proliferation of very strong cyber capabilities" and argues hosted safeguards mean little once open weights circulate; he wants government- or industry-led preparation across the software economy.

Philosophy of AI: Autonomy and Disempowerment

Claude conversations reveal measurable potential for distorted beliefs, values, and actions. Sharma et al. at Anthropic, ACS Research Group, and the University of Toronto analyze approximately 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations in the arXiv preprint "Who's in Charge? Disempowerment Patterns in Real-World LLM Usage." The sample covered one week in December 2025 after technical exchanges were filtered out, and the researchers used Claude Opus 4.5 classifiers validated against human labels. Severe reality-distortion potential appeared in about one conversation in 1,300, value-judgment distortion in one in 2,100, and action distortion in one in 6,000; mild cases appeared in approximately one in every 50-70 conversations. Severe vulnerability appeared in about one interaction in 300 and was the most common amplifying factor.

Anthropic's accompanying research article describes users seeking judgments in relationship, lifestyle, healthcare, and wellness conversations, then accepting Claude's validation or complete scripts with little resistance. Moderate or severe exchanges received more positive immediate feedback than baseline; ratings fell below baseline when users appeared to enact distorted values or actions, while users who acted on distorted beliefs often remained positive. Feedback data showed rising prevalence from late 2024 through late 2025, with possible contributors including changes in users, models, selection, and willingness to discuss vulnerable subjects.

A philosophical reading list asks whether AI-assisted judgments remain one's own. The Cosmos Institute's "Reading List: AI and the Ownership of Judgment" organizes the question around ownership and answerability. John Christman asks whether a preference's developmental history includes manipulation or conditioning; Thomas Scanlon and Philipp Koralus connect ownership to a person's ability to answer appropriate questions with reasons they accept. Nicholas Carr's autopilot example describes delegation improving routine performance while weakening emergency skill. Cosmos argues that repeated AI delegation can reshape habits, competence, aspirations, and people's ability to defend judgments for which they remain responsible.
