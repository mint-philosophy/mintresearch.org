Models fall short of Epoch’s employee standard

Fable 5.2 leads the revised assessments, and two models reach contractor quality on data-center research. All six fall short of Epoch’s employee standard and usually prefer their own work to better human outputs.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 17 November 2026 805 words · ~4 min

AI systems that lead conventional benchmarks still struggle to choose useful research questions and judge whether their experiments answer them, according to Kelly Hong’s November 17 Epoch AI report, “How good are Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.2 Astra at real work?” None of six systems met Epoch’s employee standard on any of seven tasks drawn from its work. In the announcement thread, Epoch reports average grades of 82% for Fable 5.2 and 65% for GPT-6.2 Astra. These measure assessed work quality against a 100% employee standard; they do not measure the share of jobs automated.

The new round follows Kelly Hong and Greg Burnham’s October 8 study, “Can AI automate Epoch?”, which we covered in October. Epoch revised the tasks and grading, then reran Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra alongside newer models. Scores cannot be directly compared with the original round. The additions include research on unfamiliar chess rules and hidden messages in images, plus a test of whether models recognize better human work when judging it against their own.

Epoch’s methodology pairs models with their usual agent software: Claude Code, Codex and Antigravity. Each ran once per task at its highest reasoning setting, with contractor-like context and memory and skill writing disabled. Human graders scored rubric criteria from 1 to 4. Seven tasks cover graphic design, short Data Insight articles, data-center analysis and research. Within categories, Epoch weights difficulty, assigning easy, average and difficult tasks relative weights of 2:3:2 where present. The headline score averages the four categories, so the single data-center task contributes a quarter of the overall grade.

On that task, Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5 produced analyses that Epoch’s data-center analyst judged comparable to contractor work, which usually needs revision before publication. The models researched EcoDataCenter 1 using news and regulatory documents, ordered satellite imagery and analyzed it using Epoch’s contractor guide. Hong reports that both handled the core research well. Fable needed improvements to image annotations and concision; Opus also needed corrections to equipment classification. Epoch is considering shifting contractors toward reviewing AI outputs and other parts of the research.

GPT-6.2 Astra omitted information because it demanded more explicit evidence than the task required. Its sources indicated several AI buildings, but it covered only one, then withheld estimates of compute and chip quantities because it wanted independent confirmation of every input. Epoch says the supplied guide and available evidence supported those estimates. The published grader notes praise its source retrieval and analysis while criticizing its overly conservative judgment. Gemini 4 Argon made a different error: it assumed every building used Nvidia B200 chips, citing a disclosure that applied to only one.

The chess task asked models to design experiments testing whether other models can handle situations unlike their training data. The rules needed to be unfamiliar without making the puzzles so much harder that difficulty explained any decline. Every system used existing variants. Astra relabeled pieces while preserving classical chess; Fable 5.2’s purportedly original Kingsgate variant reproduced Midline Invasion. Gemini tested whether models already knew its variants, found that they did, and proceeded anyway. Astra also led its report with a performance decline that its later tests, using a larger reasoning budget, contradicted.

On the hidden-messages task, models investigated whether other models could conceal information in computer-drawn images and recover it from the images alone. Hong found that they tended to stop after initial attempts, even when the results failed to test the intended capability. Most of the images generated in Fable 5.2’s study displayed their messages openly. Human researchers had iterated toward subtler encodings, including varying the heights of stars to represent letters. In the Data Insight task, Fable and Opus chose suitable topics but wrote too densely. Astra compared Chinese-language tokenization efficiency in two models, missing Epoch’s instruction to identify a broad trend of interest to its audience.

Epoch then gave models their grading rubrics and pairs of outputs: their own work and a human example that met its standards, without identifying which was which. Models chose their own output in every tested comparison except one, when Gemini preferred the human hidden-messages study. Hong found that their explanations often exaggerated small defects in human work or rewarded the number of experiments without considering whether those experiments answered the research question. Opus, for example, criticized the human study for failing to tell the image-making model that a recipient would see only the image, even though other instructions already ruled out hiding the message in invisible source code.

Epoch’s assessments compare complete model-and-agent setups on its own work, with one run per task and subjective human grading. Software differences can affect performance, and the small task suite does not represent the whole economy. The concrete failures nevertheless show what graders expected: estimates supported by reasonable assumptions, experiments that test the intended question, and summaries that reflect the results.
