Prosecutors use Pangram scores to challenge defense briefs

In Khalwinder Bhengura’s prosecution, AI-detector exhibits accompanied a dispute over release before sentencing. His lawyers say a medical emergency left four days to prepare for trial, and that they reviewed the AI-assisted work.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 17 November 2026 806 words · ~4 min

Federal prosecutors used Pangram AI-detector reports to accuse Khalwinder Singh Bhengura's defense team of submitting largely machine-written legal arguments, Jason Koebler reports in 404 Media. In a November 2 filing opposing release before sentencing, they told the court they had been economizing their responses because the case had become “defense by ChatGPT.” The defense had disclosed AI assistance. Its lawyers told Koebler that a medical emergency left them four days to prepare for trial and that they had reviewed and edited the work.

Bhengura, a 70-year-old lawful permanent resident from India, was convicted on October 27 of a false citizenship statement connected to voter registration and voting as an alien in a federal election. The jury acquitted him on a third count. His release brief records that he was taken into custody after the verdict, with sentencing set for February 11, 2027. His lawyers say his English is limited and that he misunderstood the registration process; Herrera told Koebler that Bhengura faces both imprisonment and deportation.

Assistant US Attorney Matthew Capoccia's 20-page opposition argues against release on legal and factual grounds, including flight risk. The detector claims appear in a footnote. Prosecutors reported that Pangram classified one defense brief as 76% AI-generated and three others as 63%, while assigning four government filings scores they described as 0%. They argued that much of the defense text classified as human consisted of quotations from evidence or case law, and inferred that defense lawyers had written little substantive material themselves. The filing asks the judge to deny release; it makes no separate request for permission to leave future filings unanswered.

The Pangram 4.1 exhibits classify portions of each document as AI-generated, AI-assisted or human-written. The reports describe mixed authorship in the defense filings, with some segments classified at low confidence. Even very short fragments receive labels. Quoted legal material often appears as human-written, while argumentative passages are frequently marked AI-generated. These are the detector's classifications; prosecutors used them to make a further claim about how much independent work defense counsel had done.

Judge Mark T. Pittman denied release on November 4, criticizing the defense's statutory interpretation and its use of precedent. The defense argued that a sentencing range potentially beginning at zero months removed the presumption of detention. Pittman rejected that reading and said the eventual guidelines calculation could recommend imprisonment. He also concluded that Bhengura had not established that he would not flee, citing his assets, foreign contacts and a newly issued immigration detainer.

Pittman described the arguments as mostly AI-generated and criticized the defense's use of United States v. Mincey, a real 2007 decision concerning supervised release. His order never names Pangram. In a footnote, however, he cites the government's complaint about “defense by ChatGPT” and says he would sanction counsel but lacked time for a hearing before Thanksgiving. He warned that further waste of court and government resources could bring sanctions, including loss of permission to practice in the district.

The Northern District of Texas's Local Criminal Rule 47.2(e) requires a brief prepared using generative AI to disclose that fact on its first page. A judge can require identification of the specific passages, and a filing without the notice certifies that no part was prepared with generative AI. The rule does not prohibit AI assistance. Bhengura's release brief includes the required first-page heading and states that portions were prepared with such a tool.

Defense lawyers Nicole Knox and Mario Herrera told Koebler that Knox suffered a medical emergency while they were preparing with prosecutors at the US Attorney's Office. Herrera said the lead attorney was hospitalized and he had four days to prepare a federal jury trial. The government's opposition confirms an October 19 medical episode at its office and says it did not oppose Herrera's application for a continuance. Prosecutors nevertheless argued that the preparation was manageable for experienced lawyers, describing the main discovery as roughly 120 pages and a short video.

Herrera told 404 Media that a detector's assessment of writing patterns cannot establish authorship or whether the legal analysis is correct. He said prosecutors had identified no false citations and that he had personally written the arguments Pittman criticized. AI-assisted portions were reviewed and edited by him, he said, and he stood behind the final filings. Knox likewise maintained that the defense's argument came from the lawyers. Neither ECF 81 nor Pittman's order identifies an invented case; their criticism concerns the arguments and the use of existing authorities.

Pangram chief executive Max Spero told Koebler that, anecdotally, the law-firm uses he had encountered were mostly internal checks on whether junior attorneys and paralegals used AI to draft briefs. In Bhengura's case, prosecutors submitted detector results against opposing counsel, whose AI assistance was already disclosed in the release brief. The Justice Department did not respond to 404 Media's request for comment.
