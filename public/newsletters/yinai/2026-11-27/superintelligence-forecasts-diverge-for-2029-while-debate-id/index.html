Erdil and Kokotajlo disagree on how quickly AI research can accelerate

Ege Erdil expects the cost of testing ideas to slow automated AI research. Daniel Kokotajlo gives better than even odds on full automation of AI research in 2027 if labs keep racing. He wants them to slow down now.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 November 2026 619 words · ~3 min

Introducing his November 27 conversation with Ege Erdil of Google DeepMind and Daniel Kokotajlo of the AI Futures Project, Dwarkesh Patel gave the odds each guest assigned to a rapid acceleration that produces AI more capable than any human expert before 2029. Patel put Erdil at around 20% and Kokotajlo above 50%. In the discussion, Erdil put a 10–20% probability on software improvements alone allowing AI researchers to compress years of progress into months within the next three years. Kokotajlo gave slightly better than even odds on fully automating AI research during 2027, unless labs slow down voluntarily or governments intervene. After that, he thinks a large acceleration is more likely than not.

Erdil explained why he expects machines to find AI research harder than mathematics. A researcher can check a formal mathematical proof on a personal computer. To check whether a change makes training a large model more efficient, a lab might have to train a large model. A method that succeeds in a small experiment can fail at scale, and one that fails in a cheap trial can prove valuable in an expensive one. Adding more automated researchers gives a lab more ideas to test. It also increases demand for the chips needed to decide which ideas to keep.

Kokotajlo expects better AI researchers to discover ways of learning from much less experience than today’s models need. Erdil expects some conspicuous improvements even on his slower forecast. By the end of 2027, he thinks an AI will probably be able to download an unfamiliar game from Steam and play it through. He also expects small, fast models to let robots do simple physical jobs at roughly human speed. Asked about automating everything people do, Erdil gave a median of 15–20 years from now assuming AI does not accelerate its own development.

The two disagreed about what the public could learn during a slowdown. Kokotajlo expects people to learn a great deal from using the systems labs have already built. He sees opportunities to study the agents that could soon automate research. For instance, making it easier to inspect a model’s reasoning could also make the model more expensive to run. He doubts labs racing each other would willingly bear that cost. Patel asked him about Thomas Larsen and Romeo Dean’s proposal, which we covered on November 18. The authors would have companies disclose how they use their chips, have auditors check the figures and redirect some computing power to customers and safety research. They roughly estimate that a 15% cap on computing power for capabilities research starting in January 2027 would buy a year.

Erdil doubts a pause today would give voters enough experience to judge what comes next. A community might oppose a data center on local grounds and support a slowdown for that reason. Seeing a system do work that people recognize as their own could change what they want governments to do. Erdil applied the same argument to decisions about what companies disclose. A lab may sincerely believe that keeping a result secret protects the public. He wants citizens and their representatives to have enough information and authority to decide whether they agree.

Erdil argued that Washington’s language about China could undermine the prospects for an agreement. In Erdil’s account, even a five-year US lead would leave the question of what happens when other countries catch up. If Chinese officials think Washington intends to use its advantage against them, they have reason to distrust an American offer to slow down together. Kokotajlo agreed that a durable settlement would probably require an agreement with China and other countries. Neither guest thought governments needed to settle the odds of superintelligence before starting that discussion.
