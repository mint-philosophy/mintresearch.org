Vincent Conitzer on the human work behind three AI-assisted proofs

Conitzer found a shorter proof for one result. A coauthor worked through the second argument, and Conitzer was still trying to understand the third when he published his account.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 November 2026 703 words · ~4 min

On November 27, Vincent Conitzer wrote about apparent solutions to three questions in theoretical computer science that had bothered him for more than a decade. When a colleague sent him a long AI-generated answer to the first, Conitzer struggled to follow it, though other models judged it correct. Knowing which conclusion to aim for, and perhaps having picked up some intuition from the argument, he found a much shorter proof himself. He was still trying to understand the proof of the third.

The first question grew out of his work with Tuomas Sandholm on designing rules for people who have private information. Conitzer posed it in 2015. Could a computer efficiently find the rule that gives participants the greatest expected total benefit, subject to a few requirements? Participants should be willing to take part and to tell the truth, the rule must choose a definite outcome, and payments among participants must balance. The proof that Ryan O’Donnell sent him says that finding the optimum is computationally hard even with just two participants. Conitzer could give his own argument for that conclusion without having mastered the one that led him to it.

In the second case, the researchers kept the model’s approach. In their arXiv paper How Many Votes Is a Lie Worth?, Ratip Emin Berker, Conitzer and their coauthors ask how many like-minded voters you would have to recruit, all voting honestly, to get an outcome at least as good for you as the best lie on your own ballot. Under the Borda count, which awards points according to rank, you can always do at least as well by bringing in as many honest voters as there are candidates minus two. With five candidates, for instance, three additional voters who share your preferences suffice, however large the electorate.

Conitzer had known how to prove an optimality result in restricted settings. The final theorem now covers any rule that treats voters alike and allows each candidate to win in some election. Across all electorate sizes, no such rule consistently beats Borda’s bound. The authors say they found the argument through repeated exchanges with GPT-6.1 Sol. Earlier versions of the paper contained results the researchers had obtained themselves. For the new theorem, Berker worked through the model’s construction and the authors rewrote substantial parts so readers could follow the reasoning.

The last question concerned a game in which one player divides limited protection among several locations and the other chooses one location, with preferences the first player only partly knows. Both choose simultaneously. Conitzer wanted to know whether a computer could efficiently find an equilibrium, a pair of strategies in which each player is already doing as well as possible given the other’s choices. In his paper on exact equilibria, Shiva Kintali gives two algorithms whose running times grow polynomially with the size of the input. Kintali credits successive rounds of work with Codex for parts of the proofs and improvements to the running times. Conitzer says this solution passed AI checks. He had yet to understand it himself.

When Conitzer posted that last challenge, people sent him so many related arguments that he could scarcely read them. One contributor sent a draft of more than 30 pages and told him candidly that they could not vouch for it. Conitzer could imagine a student spending a doctorate on this family of questions. He wonders what incentive a student now has to spend years understanding a result for which someone else has already received the credit. Venues that require human review may already be asking referees to spend longer checking an argument than its authors spent producing it.

Scott Aaronson addressed another instance of an AI proof claim spreading faster than readers could assess it. In a fictional Thanksgiving conversation, a caller for a financial newsletter confuses Anthropic’s previously reported proof claim about algebraic complexity with a threat to encryption. Aaronson wrote that he had specific information about a forthcoming theoretical advance in cryptanalysis and that it posed no imminent danger to encryption systems in use. He had also heard rumors about other results that could threaten deployed systems. He said he knew too little about those rumors to act on them.
