One model’s memory of a film helped another recall its director

Stanford researchers recovered facts a model missed when asked directly. A separate experiment in the same November 25 study showed that two models could exchange cues through an intermediary.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 November 2026 493 words · ~2 min

A language model asked who directed Interstellar answered Denis Villeneuve. Given another model’s internal representation of the film, it answered Christopher Nolan. The researchers had supplied no director’s name. They changed the model’s internal state as it processed the title, leaving its later layers to retrieve the answer. Vivien Cheng, Jerry Liu, Morris Yau and Chris Ré of Stanford’s Hazy Research group describe the experiment in their November 25 report “Plato’s Key.” Cheng announced the work the same day.

The team first had to translate between two models that had been trained independently. As each model read the name of a subject it knew, the researchers collected the numbers representing that subject at one layer of the model. From a set of subjects both models knew, they calculated how far to rotate and rescale one model’s representations to line them up with the other’s. They kept both models fixed and tried the same calculation on names they had held out of the exercise.

Thursday’s issue described an experiment in which the researchers substituted one model’s representation of France for another’s representation of Germany. Asked for Germany’s capital, the second model could then answer Paris. Both models already knew both capitals. For the film experiment, the team looked for cases where the second model had given a wrong answer and asked whether it could recall the right one with another model’s help.

Cheng and colleagues started with questions the donor answered correctly and the recipient missed. To establish whether the recipient knew the answer, they tried rephrasing the question or showing it three examples. They also checked whether the model ranked the right answer first among alternatives, such as a list of directors. They counted a miss as a “dark memory” if the model passed any of these checks. Across the facts that qualified, replacing the recipient’s representation of the subject with the donor’s recovered the answer 18% of the time.

The team also tested whether a translation between two models could work through a third. Once they had maps from the first model to the second and from the second to the third, they could apply both maps to an internal representation from the first. They did not need to run the middle model on the question. On facts that all three could already recall, this procedure retained a median 99% of the accuracy of translating directly between the first and third models.

The authors interpret those results as evidence for the idea that independently trained models can come to represent parts of the world in similar ways. They found an important qualification when deciding where in each model to make the substitution. Choosing layers because they arranged subjects similarly gave poor results. It worked better to try the swap on a separate set of prompts and see where the recipient could actually use it. The researchers propose testing whether cues exchanged in this way can also support reasoning that takes several steps.
