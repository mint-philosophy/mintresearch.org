Sol used fewer reasoning tokens per coding task while Astra’s median held steady

Michael Mayer found that both OpenAI models used fewer tokens relative to their opponents than their predecessors had. Sol also fell in developers’ ratings, while Astra held steady.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 November 2026 578 words · ~3 min

Michael Mayer reported on November 26 that OpenAI’s Astra 6.2 and Sol 6.2 used fewer tokens relative to rivals on developers’ coding tasks than their predecessors had. For Sol, the drop showed up in its own numbers too. Its median reasoning count fell from about 1,260 tokens per assignment to about 320. Astra used roughly as many reasoning tokens as before, even as the other models tackling the same tasks used more. Mayer suspects OpenAI tuned the models to cut costs.

In Agent Duel, a developer describes a task once. Two randomly drawn models receive the same instructions and tools, each in its own copy of the developer’s repository. The developer can inspect and try both versions before choosing a winner or declaring a tie. Only then does the app identify the models. Mayer compared those votes with the cost of completing the assignments. Both new models cost less relative to their opponents. Sol fell in the ranking based on votes, while Astra’s rating changed little.

Mayer’s reasoning chart shows how the selection of tasks and opponents affects the comparison. Astra 6 and Astra 6.2 each had a median of about 1,450 recorded reasoning tokens per task. That put Astra 6 at roughly half its opponents’ median and Astra 6.2 at a quarter of its opponents’ median. Developers entered tasks as they worked. A pair of agents in a given round received identical instructions, but old and new releases faced different sets of assignments.

The output counts show an even larger difference between the two ways of measuring Astra. Its median output rose from about 5,500 tokens to about 6,700, while its opponents went from roughly 7,400 to 13,200. Sol produced less output on both measures. Mayer reported that the declines relative to opponents were statistically significant for both reasoning and output. The chart includes models with more than 100 eligible votes each.

When we covered the release on November 17, OpenAI made its own claim about efficiency. On an internal test of professional work, it said Astra scored 6.2 percentage points higher than its predecessor while using 22% fewer output tokens. OpenAI also cut Astra’s input and output prices by 20% and kept Sol’s prices per token the same. Mayer’s analysis adds evidence from developers who brought their own work and judged the result for themselves.

Writing on November 27, Zvi Mowshowitz argued that Astra was particularly good at solving difficult problems whose requirements a user could spell out. Users who expected it to infer their intentions often had to intervene. At Every, Katie Parrott described how Mike Taylor tried both models on work that had stumped earlier systems at the start of the year. Both could now plan a show minute by minute and design exercises for executives using realistic data, as their immediate predecessors could. In comments Mowshowitz reproduced, Every’s team said Astra tended to repeat its initial design choices and stopped coding before projects were finished.

Epoch AI had seen a related gap in its evaluation at launch. Kelly Hong found that Astra 6.2 and Fable 5.2 roughly tied on Epoch’s general capabilities index. When staff graded actual assignments against what they expected from employees, Astra averaged 65% and Fable 82%. Astra did improve by 25 points over its predecessor on data center research. Those grades concern the quality of completed work. Establishing whether longer answers or more time spent reasoning would have improved the grades would take a separate comparison.
