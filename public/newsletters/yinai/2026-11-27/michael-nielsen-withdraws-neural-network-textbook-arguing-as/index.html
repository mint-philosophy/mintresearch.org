Michael Nielsen withdraws his neural networks textbook

Nielsen once expected societies to adapt as AI improved. He explains why he revised that forecast and why he now urges researchers to turn down frontier-lab jobs.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 November 2026 616 words · ~3 min

Michael Nielsen has replaced his online textbook Neural Networks and Deep Learning with a statement explaining why he took it down. He announced the withdrawal on November 27. Nielsen wrote the book between 2011 and 2015, when he expected that people learning the subject would have decades to prepare for machines with general intelligence. He still thinks AI has done more good than harm so far. But he now believes that helping train people to work at frontier labs contributes to a risk the public never agreed to take.

Nielsen traces his change of mind to an error in his forecast of how much money investors would put into AI. By 2015 he already suspected that neural networks could produce general intelligence with relatively few further conceptual breakthroughs. He expected developers to be able to raise hundreds of millions of dollars, perhaps billions, by the mid-2020s. He now thinks he may have underestimated the capital becoming available by a factor of a thousand. On his reckoning, that brought the prospect of general intelligence and then superintelligence forward by perhaps 15 to 20 years. He had counted on social institutions learning how to handle these systems as researchers developed them.

Nielsen asks readers to imagine a century’s worth of discoveries arriving in a single year. People would enjoy the benefits sooner. They would also have to negotiate international agreements and build public infrastructure before another century’s worth of discoveries arrived the following year. He compares the price of a match with the expense of keeping communities safe from fire. Building standards, trained firefighters and public services all require continuing investment.

That argument applies even if a future superintelligence follows human instructions faithfully. Nielsen expects that some of the most consequential discoveries will be things nobody can now anticipate. A company that improves its model’s ability to follow instructions can solve a real safety problem while making the product more attractive to governments and customers. In his view, that commercial success can accelerate development and leave society even less time to adapt. He thinks the same difficulty would face humans whose own intelligence had been enhanced enough to make discoveries at that pace.

Nielsen then considers the decision to take a job at a frontier lab. In Claude’s Constitution, Anthropic argues that if powerful AI is coming anyway, the public benefits from having a developer that cares about safety among the leaders. Nielsen grants that a small improvement in the odds would be enormously valuable. In his hypothetical, lowering the probability of human extinction from 10% to 9% would save some 80 million lives in expectation. He asks what evidence a researcher could put before an independent jury to show that joining a particular lab would improve those odds.

One reason for his own verdict is Anthropic’s account of its early restraint. In its 2023 statement on AI safety, the company said it generally withheld research that would speed up advances in AI capabilities. It had initially kept Claude for safety research and started deploying it as other publicly available systems caught up. In its subsequent Claude 3 announcement, Anthropic advertised Opus as outperforming competitors on widely used tests. Nielsen sees that change in what the company was willing to put on the market as evidence against trusting developers to hold back when rivals press ahead.

Nielsen accepts that slowing development would cost people some of AI’s benefits. He nevertheless urges researchers to decline frontier-lab jobs and work toward mutual restraint, including between countries. In his view, even people who think their country or company would be the safest winner have to account for the time the competition itself takes away from everyone else.
