Will Oremus sees a limited role for AI-text detection

Will Oremus says Brian X. Chen’s tests and Pangram 4’s reported error rate support using detection as an indicator, while false accusations and evasion still limit institutional use.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 August 2026 273 words · ~2 min

Will Oremus argued on Bluesky that Pangram may now be useful as an indicator or deterrent, while its scores should never settle authorship by themselves. He favors false negatives because a false accusation can harm students and professional writers. Oremus pointed to a New York Times column in which Brian X. Chen ran about 50 tests using mixed human and AI passages, Dickens, and suspected chatbot posts. Chen reported no text-classification failure in those tests, although Pangram’s image scanner passed three of 21 debunked AI fakes as real.

Pangram’s July 29 model card reports 41 false positives across one million human-written web passages, roughly one in 24,000. It also reports a 0.34 percent miss rate across 26 AI generators, down from 1.99 percent for the prior model. Those are Pangram’s own evaluations, and Oremus’s qualified endorsement turns on the combination of low reported error and restrained use.

Accuracy for non-native English writers remains central because a 2023 study by James Zou and colleagues, summarized by Stanford HAI, found that seven older detectors flagged 61 percent of non-native students’ TOEFL essays as AI-generated. Pangram cofounder Max Spero replied with company results showing one false positive across about 24,600 English-learner samples and none on that study’s TOEFL essays. Oremus still called any bias problematic.

Matteo Wong’s Atlantic essay “America Has a Pangram Problem” describes the institutional scale of rare errors: Pangram integrates with Canvas, which serves millions of students, while a humanizer repeatedly rewrote chatbot articles until the detector called them human. Oremus’s position therefore remains deliberately narrow: evidence can justify investigation or deter casual misuse without turning a probability score into a verdict.
