Election officials no longer trust a hollowed-out CISA

After CISA lost a third of its staff and curtailed election support, Democratic state officials told Nick Miroff and Yvonne Wingett Sanchez they no longer rely on the agency; suspected Iranian attacks on water systems have raised the stakes.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 17 August 2026 498 words · ~2 min

In The Atlantic, Nick Miroff and Yvonne Wingett Sanchez report in "How Trump Left America Vulnerable to Cyberattacks" that the administration hollowed out its own cyberdefense agency while fighting Iran, "an aggressive and capable cyber foe". Kristi Noem promised senators at her January 2025 confirmation hearing to make the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency "smaller" and "more nimble"; CISA then lost a third of its staff and has no Senate-confirmed director. Trump's grudge dates to CISA declaring 2020 "the most secure in American history"; an April 2025 presidential memorandum stripped former director Christopher Krebs's clearance for "falsely and baselessly" denying the election was stolen.

The magazine reports hackers with suspected Iranian ties hit water and wastewater systems in more than a dozen states in late July, over 30 in Minnesota, briefly shutting down Braham's water system; CBS News reports the FBI, EPA, and CISA urged utilities July 30 to disconnect exposed controllers. Krebs said whoever starts a shooting war has "a responsibility to work with state and local partners and give them a hand." Jen Easterly, Biden's CISA director, said utilities built for reliability should not have to "defend themselves from the Iranian government", and that AI models help hackers find and exploit their weaknesses: "The threats are moving faster and the risks are more dangerous than ever."

Half a dozen Democratic secretaries of state told the reporters they no longer treat CISA as a reliable partner. The administration halted intelligence briefings for election officials, defunded the EI-ISAC threat-sharing network in March 2025, put CISA's foreign-disinformation staff on leave, and hired Heather Honey, an activist who worked to overturn Trump's 2020 defeat, to oversee election integrity; Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin has threatened prison for officials who withhold voter data from its Georgia and Arizona investigations of the 2020 results. Officials fear their reports could become evidence against them; Trump blamed Governor Tim Walz, not Iran, for Minnesota's water attacks. After a suspected pro-Iranian intruder defaced Arizona election websites with images of Ayatollah Khomeini in June 2025, Secretary of State Adrian Fontes had state homeland-security officials alert CISA without naming the affected office. "I don't trust them," he said.

Mullin reversed course at a June budget hearing, seeking 600 hires; The Record reports about 2,200 employees remain of 3,400. States substituted peer networks, contractors, National Guard units, and a nonprofit of ex-CISA officials; nothing, experts told the magazine, replaces federal intelligence collection. Technology companies told one battleground-state official in April they have a "high degree of confidence that Iran is going to attempt to disrupt" this year's election; on a video call, acting director Nicholas Andersen, asked about restarting briefings and penetration testing, said some services might resume in 2027. Minnesota Secretary of State Steve Simon concluded the agency is "mostly writing off the 2026 election cycle". Offline, paper-backed voting machines make altering tallies unlikely, former CISA officials said; registration databases and results websites remain exposed, and a breach there would corrode confidence in elections the president disputes.
