The Cosmos Institute asks who owns AI-assisted judgment

A five-session seminar reading list follows judgment from the extended mind through answerability, skill loss, formation, and institutional power, defining ownership through reasons a person can still defend.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 17 August 2026 438 words · ~2 min

On August 14, the Cosmos Institute published the reading list from a five-session seminar on the ownership of judgment, convened with Liberty Fund in Aspen for philosophers, builders, and founders. AI can let people do more while contributing less to decisions for which they remain answerable, the introduction argues; "formal authorship can survive as substantive authorship weakens". Because exercising judgment develops the capacity, habitual dependence can erode a person's ability to notice the loss. The institute defines authorship as "the ability to discern and set your own ends" and acknowledges that people always form judgments through social practices, institutions, and tools.

Andy Clark and David Chalmers's "The Extended Mind", published in Analysis, supplies one account of tool-assisted cognition: a notebook can function as integrated memory. Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa reserve cognition for states with non-derived content, while Michael Polanyi describes tools incorporated into skilled thought. John Christman's historical condition complicates present endorsement, since a cult member may endorse beliefs formed under domination. T.M. Scanlon then connects ownership to responsiveness to reasons; Nomy Arpaly's Huckleberry Finn recognizes Jim's personhood despite his explicit morality. Philipp Koralus calls a judgment defensible under sustained questioning an erotetic equilibrium, reached when its holder can explain it "in terms we are happy with, across an appropriate range of questions". Kant rejects outsourced understanding, Rousseau's tutor directs a pupil who feels free, and McDowell locates reflection within an upbringing.

Readings on character and automation begin from Aristotle's view that action forms the agent. Whitehead argues that mechanizing routine operations frees attention for harder work; Nicholas Carr's The Glass Cage describes autopilot improving aggregate safety while pilots lose emergency skill. Tocqueville imagines an overhelpful state teaching citizens to forget self-government, and Elizabeth Anderson's Private Government carries the concern into workplaces. Oakeshott, Humboldt, Arendt, and Shannon Vallor consider how institutions and technologies form ends and habits. Isaiah Berlin warns about coercion by people claiming to serve a "higher" self; Philip Pettit links autonomy to protection from arbitrary power, and Albert Hirschman turns to voice when exit costs too much. Gadamer and MacIntyre close with traditions sustained through argument and revision.

Koralus, Oxford's McCord Professor of Philosophy and AI and a Cosmos team member, wrote the syllabus's only reading centered on AI: the April 2025 arXiv preprint "The Philosophic Turn for AI Agents". He argues that growing complexity can overwhelm agency while externally controlled choice architectures scale manipulation. His design alternative favors decentralized, Socratic truth-seeking and user control over system configuration. Cosmos, which calls itself "the Academy for Philosopher-Builders", runs fellowships, grants, and seminars and maintains a research home at Oxford's Human-Centered AI Lab.
