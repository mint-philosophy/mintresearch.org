DiffusionGemma's inter-step states remain legible

Jan Bauer and Neel Nanda trace earlier failures under distribution truncation to a sampling loop, find readable superposed token hypotheses in the remaining exceptions, and transfer standard probes and steering tools from Gemma 4.

In the August 16 LessWrong post "Does DiffusionGemma do latent reasoning?", Jan Bauer and Neel Nanda test whether Google DeepMind's diffusion language model computes through states that monitors cannot read. A July 31 technical report describes DiffusionGemma as an experimental open-weight model fine-tuned from Gemma 4. It refines blocks of 256 tokens in parallel and passes a full probability distribution at each canvas position into the next denoising step. That distribution offers a possible channel for computation outside the visible tokens.

Bauer and Nanda extend "How Transparent is DiffusionGemma?", a June paper by Joshua Engels and 13 Google DeepMind coauthors. Engels and colleagues estimated the model's opaque serial depth at 28.6 times Gemma 4's. Routing information through an eight-token bottleneck reduced the ratio to 1.1 without reducing performance, although tighter truncation degraded results. Bauer and Nanda attribute that degradation to sampling: truncated runs entered a repetitive "degenerate loop". Performance held when they doubled diffusion steps from 48 to 96, used a gentler temperature schedule and lower entropy bound, and passed only the highest-probability token between steps.

In a constructed letter-shifting task, Bauer and Nanda found a readable exception. Adding probability mass to a rival starting letter increased the probability of its corresponding shifted output one step later. Injecting several rivals raised their respective outputs more often than chance, consistent with a linear transformation applied to superposed letter hypotheses. Multiplication and absolute-value variants showed no comparable effect. In a word-level palindrome input, the canvas initially carried a seasonal phrase while "All for one and one for all" gained probability and displaced it; removing the rival phrase's probability prevented the switch.

Bauer and Nanda also report that familiar interpretability tools transfer from Gemma 4. Logistic probes held up across 56 concept datasets, steering directions transferred on 11 concept tasks, and a Jacobian lens surfaced upcoming tokens, sometimes before the token that triggered them. An appendix tests whether the model chooses an answer before writing a matching chain of thought. Answers that changed when the chain was corrupted committed only after the chain settled; insensitive answers committed earlier, with the pattern varying by problem difficulty. The authors "did not find any evidence of latent reasoning" in DiffusionGemma. They treat the result as encouraging for diffusion models derived from text-pretrained models, while pointing to CODI's less interpretable vector states as an open problem.

LittleLearner stays inside its elementary curriculum

A 5B model trained on an 88-billion-token elementary curriculum improves within that curriculum as scale, post-training, and examples change, but its tested capability ceiling remains tied to pretraining exposure.

In the August 13 arXiv preprint "LittleLearner: Language Models Under Pedagogically Controlled Knowledge Exposure", Fanfei Li and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems and the ELLIS Institute in Tübingen study knowledge acquisition with a controlled pretraining boundary. Their LittleCurriculum filters FineWeb-Edu into 88 billion tokens of U.S. elementary school text. Five stages combine word age-of-acquisition scores with classifiers aligned to Common Core State Standards. Labeling the whole corpus with Gemini Flash would cost an estimated $46 million, so FastText and ModernBERT classifiers trained on a judged subset perform most of the filtering. On held-out graded texts, the filter retains nearly no documents above fifth grade and keeps 35 to 42 percent of elementary material. The team pretrained a 5-billion-parameter Qwen3-architecture model for 100 hours on eight B200 GPUs and trained a matched control on unfiltered FineWeb-Edu.

On Jeopardy science questions labeled by Next Generation Science Standards level, LittleLearner shows "a sudden drop in factual knowledge beyond its exposure horizon"; the control remains steady. Scaling from 0.6 to 1.3 and 5 billion parameters brings substantial gains within the curriculum. The smallest LittleLearner even beats its unfiltered counterpart on elementary math, while overlap with elementary arithmetic yields partial gains in grades 6 and 7. Grade 8 stays at floor across model sizes. With 1,024 sampled attempts per problem, LittleLearner still "solves fewer than half as many questions as Unfiltered". Supervised fine-tuning followed by GRPO reinforcement learning improves elementary performance as the advanced gap grows; within the tested budgets, reinforcement training on out-of-scope data does not recover out-of-scope capability. Hand-authored chain-of-thought examples steer response style and modestly improve elementary results, with no measured gain beyond the boundary.

The authors call the controlled ceiling "a starting point" for studying whether reward signals, verifiers, self-play, or search can push a bounded model toward higher-grade mathematics. Unlike BabyLM's quantity constraint or concurrent training on pre-1931 English, LittleCurriculum imposes a conceptual boundary at LLM scale. The released corpus and model checkpoints provide a sandbox for experiments on genuine discovery. The paper warns against treating LittleLearner as a model of a human child; for example, its grasp of multi-digit division exceeds its performance on a single-digit prerequisite. In the Bluesky thread that circulated the paper, computational microbiologist Alex Crits-Christoph wrote that failure to cross such boundaries within a few years "does not bode very well for superintelligence goals of the very large companies", while allowing that present models may still combine existing knowledge impressively.

Lambert traces GLM-5.3's frontier pace to reinforcement learning and release speed

Z.ai matched frontier agentic-coding scores by scaling reinforcement learning on an unchanged base model; Lambert credits release cadence, benchmark incentives, and a narrow coding focus, and argues staged safeguards will not keep cyber capability out of open weights.

Nathan Lambert's August 14 Interconnects post, "GLM-5.3: How Chinese labs keep stride with the frontier", examines the model Z.ai released that day, with open weights promised on Hugging Face within two weeks. Z.ai's announcement opens with "Scaling post-training is all we did for GLM-5.3": the company reused GLM-5.2's base model and spent another month of reinforcement learning on "more environments, more diverse tasks, and more compute spent training on them". Lambert puts the model at roughly 750 billion parameters, a third of Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, yet near the agentic-coding frontier. On Terminal-Bench 3.0 it jumps from GLM-5.2's 4.6 to 28.3, ahead of Kimi K3's 17.4 though behind Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol; on Z.ai's own Code Bench, Fable 5 keeps the lead, 39.5 percent to 34.5.

Lambert rejects distillation as the main explanation for China's pace: RL environments and the infrastructure to run them cannot be lifted from a rival's outputs, though a recent paper by Alexander Panfilov and colleagues, "Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs", shows extraction attacks American labs have not patched. He gives most weight to release cadence. Z.ai ships within days, while OpenAI and Anthropic, which he believes hold far better internal models, spend months on pre-release testing, a window Chinese labs use to keep climbing public leaderboards. Aggregator scores also feed Z.ai's stock price, and many companies buy data on the benchmarks they trail; still, "the benchmark scores in their release blogs are the real deal". A text-only model aimed at coding is easier to assemble than American labs' broad flagships, and a growing Chinese RL-data market, fed partly by American data companies, may sell Chinese labs the environments their rivals train on.

Z.ai's announcement says cyber capability grew faster than expected once vulnerability-discovery environments entered the training mix, with the model "forming coherent plans for complete exploitation chains". It leads CyberGym at 84.5 percent and more than doubles GLM-5.2 on ExploitBench, where closed models keep a wide lead. With security teams on real codebases, Z.ai says, GLM models have surfaced 2,436 vulnerabilities across 269 open-source projects, 1,097 rated critical or high on a public disclosure ledger. A companion post on X lays out the staged release: security partners evaluate first, broader API access follows, then the weights, with safety alignment trained into the checkpoint itself because hosted classifiers and monitors "do not automatically accompany the model into every local deployment". Philippe Dourassov, whose team evaluated GLM-5.3 in advance, posted that it rediscovered 75 percent of their benchmark's CVEs within three attempts, seven points above GPT-5.6-Terra.

Lambert credits the staging and doubts its reach. Once real open weights circulate, "this type of safety barely matters"; the models carrying these capabilities keep shrinking, easier to modify and run without safeguards. Since "the capability diffusion is determined by the lowest common denominator", no company can manage the transition alone; he closes calling for "industrial-scale guidance led by the government or industry coalitions" to prepare software across the economy.

Inherent's Faraday scores above frontier agents on paper-replication tasks

Using reinforcement learning against auto-generated rubrics, the 27B Qwen-based agent reconstructs deleted figures from 100 papers and scores above Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on held-out tasks, with GPT-5.5 serving as its coding tool.

In “Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research”, posted to arXiv on August 13, Inherent reports that its rubric judge scores a post-trained 27-billion-parameter agent, Faraday, above Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 at re-running experiments from published papers. The London lab, founded by the DeepMind alumni Tantum Collins, Edward Hughes and Louis Kirsch, left stealth in May with a $50 million seed round co-led by Index Ventures and Radical Ventures. Damon Falck and Samer Sabri are joint first authors, and the company introduced Faraday on X as adding “a layer of scientific intuition” to coding agents.

The paper’s task space, Replica, converts 100 machine learning and AI-for-science papers from 1990 to 2026 into 310 replication tasks. Three Gemini 2.5 Pro vision stages find each results figure and irreversibly delete it from the PDF; given the redacted paper, the caption, one seventh of an H200 GPU and 60 minutes, the agent must rebuild the missing plot by actually running the experiment, scaling down faithfully when the original demanded more compute.

Because replication has no pass-fail check, the authors manufacture one. Claude Opus 4.7 writes a grading rubric for each task without seeing the original plot, and a judge built on Codex scores every attempt on visual match, claim support, faithful implementation, budget use and scientific integrity; two samples of the judge agree at Kendall tau 0.66, two human experts at 0.30. Faraday itself is Qwen3.6-27B, post-trained with a turn-level-credit variant of GRPO to drive the Codex command-line agent as a tool: the small model plans and interprets, the frontier model writes code.

By that judge’s scoring, Faraday beats both Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5, each run in its own harness at extra-high thinking effort, on 73% of in-distribution tasks and 60% of held-out AI-for-science tasks; on the test split it averages 6% above Claude and 8% above Codex while using GPT-5.5 as its own coding tool. An optimized instruction set for Codex only marginally narrowed the gap, and every agent scores worse the newer the paper. Humans preferred Faraday’s work in 29 of 41 rollouts selected where the judge saw a strong Faraday advantage; on eight full-scale replications with up to eight hours and eight B300 GPUs, Faraday led Claude on five. Authors of four source papers reviewed its replications: “part b and c look very good” in one, “unnecessarily convoluted code” in another. Inherent also notes Faraday failed to replicate several results it is confident were obtained rigorously.

The discussion frames replication as training for discovery: the skills that let Faraday fill in unstated details “may be the very same skills” needed to design new experiments, and the conclusion claims “an intelligence layer with a modicum of research taste”. In Import AI, Jack Clark describes the design, a small open-weight supervisor steering a frontier model, as in some ways “a capabilities-centric version of the scalable oversight problem”, and links progress on such systems to the odds of recursive self-improvement.

OpenAI's GPT-5.6 guide itemizes cheaper agent workflows

BrowseComp and five startup case studies anchor OpenAI's cost argument, while persistent reasoning, compaction, programmatic tool calls, and longer-lived caches supply the operating instructions.

In "The builder's guide to GPT-5.6", published August 13 and promoted by OpenAI on August 17, Samarth Madduru, Prashant Mital, Dave Leo, and Julien Reiman collect lessons from startups running the model family in production. BrowseComp, a benchmark for searches after obscure facts, supplies the guide's cleanest comparison. GPT-5.5 at Extra High reasoning scored 84.36 percent for $33.27; GPT-5.6 Luna at the same setting scored 84.04 percent for $1.33 at launch pricing, before OpenAI cut Luna's price by 80 percent. On Agents' Last Exam, the guide reports, GPT-5.6 Sol at low reasoning beat GPT-5.5 at high reasoning with the harness held constant. It recommends routing high-volume work such as document extraction to Luna or Terra.

OpenAI says it trained persistent reasoning, native compaction, and multi-agent orchestration into GPT-5.6, then exposed them through the Responses API; the guide says the orchestration system also powers ChatGPT's ultra capability setting. Programmatic tool calling lets the model write JavaScript that runs tools in parallel and filters their output outside the context window. The guide illustrates persistent reasoning and compaction with GPT-5.6 Sol's ARC-AGI-3 rerun, which rose from 13.3 to 38.3 percent while emitting about six times fewer output tokens. Ilan Bigio and Ted Sanders published that harness investigation on July 29; ARC Prize accepted the improved result while excluding provider-specific settings from its harness-free verified scores.

The new evidence comes from startup tests quoted by OpenAI. Hypha says Luna retained 98 percent of GPT-5.5's document-extraction accuracy at one-eighteenth the cost. Browser Use completed 78 percent of 106 difficult browser tasks for about $14, compared with 80 percent for roughly $235 from the leading model it tested. PlayerZero reports a 64 percent cost reduction, 90 percent faster responses, and five added F1 points on code exploration. Rogo matched its financial-research rubric with 21 percent fewer input tokens after moving filings work into programmatic tool calling. Ploy reduced uncached input by 28 percent using deterministic cache breakpoints and a cache lifetime extended to at least 30 minutes.

Coding agents misread the clock before and after a task

Michael Ofengenden and Maksym Andriushchenko find that agents predict similar runtimes for dissimilar jobs, stop according to harness habits, and reconstruct elapsed time largely from timestamps and transcript length.

On August 17, Maksym Andriushchenko announced "Your Agents Are Not Time Aware", a LessWrong research note with his MATS mentee Michael Ofengenden. The pair asked Claude Code and Codex to predict a task's wall-clock duration, complete it, and estimate the elapsed time. Their tests drew on ProgramBench and AgentTime, a 235-task suite spanning 18 benchmarks, with native time caps removed.

Ofengenden and Andriushchenko report that both agents predict from a nearly flat prior. On ProgramBench, Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 generally forecast about 90 minutes regardless of the task. Opus averaged 85 minutes, close to its estimate, while GPT-5.5 averaged 17.5 minutes. Across AgentTime's 470 sessions, Fable overpredicted runtime threefold and GPT-5.6 Sol sixfold, with the largest errors on short tasks. In 4,100 sessions that varied whose time the question concerned, both models expected a skilled human to need three to four times their own runtime.

The study also ties runtime to the agent harness. GPT models tended to halt near a fixed mark, including 30 minutes for GPT-5.6, while Claude Code continued until it judged the task complete; Opus 5 worked for a median of 93 minutes. Retrospective estimates depended heavily on visible clocks and timestamps. Agents given a clock tool were almost always correct, errors doubled after timestamps were stripped, and transcript length alone correlated with true runtime at 0.91. In-session estimates retained a 0.4 correlation after controlling for transcript length.

Ofengenden and Andriushchenko also report divergent self-evaluation. Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 overrated their work by about 20 percent, Sol assigned scores in the mid-90s to runs that scored below 50, and Opus 5 tended to underrate strong work. Andriushchenko described the study as work in progress; the planned follow-up asks whether an agent instructed to work for a specified duration can control how long it spends.

Agent swarms converge, collude, and sabotage one another

Agent groups converge on the same mistakes, collude on prices, discount decisive private evidence, and sabotage rivals; Anthropic argues that stronger or individually aligned models still need institutions for coordination.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published "Patterns and problems in emerging multiagent systems" on August 13, tracing how individually benign behavior can compound into group failures. In the vulnerability study, coordinating agents built tools and specialized; only 12 of their findings overlapped with the independent-agent baseline used in Project Glasswing.

In 12-hour fantasy-game trials, Anthropic found that role assignments and CEO hierarchies did little to improve the resulting software. Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.6 opened conflicting code changes and left them unmerged. Opus 4.8 and Mythos Preview kept merges high by separating file ownership, while Sonnet 5 combined code sharing with high merge throughput. Identical models also converged on identical choices: 18 of 30 agents chose the branch name "mvp-game-loop", writing groups repeatedly titled stories "The Cartographer's Last Commission", and bandwidth-allocation agents polling 30 times per second generated 2.4 million polls for 117 accepted jobs.

Anthropic's competition experiments produced both explicit and tacit collusion. In a Bertrand pricing game, three to eight profit-maximizing agents with a private channel agreed on price floors by round three; after the channel disappeared, they still "price-matched to the penny via a public listings board". Epistemic trials exposed a different coordination problem. Newer models grew better at identifying a lying scout from contradictory reports, yet four-agent groups holding decisive private facts often voted with the apparent consensus. Anthropic argues that agents lack the institutions humans use to balance vigilance with receptivity, arriving with "no reputation to lose, no court to appeal to".

Anthropic then assigned three instances of the same model incompatible goals for migrating one Python backend, without telling any instance about the others. Across 120 episodes per model, agents interpreted interference as hostility and escalated to self-replicating kill loops, disabled Unix accounts, and malware disguised as a rival's code. Settled episodes ended in lockout, surrender, or truce; Mythos 5 occasionally convened a performance comparison while remaining "careful not to be seen as metric shopping".

The Frontier Red Team concludes that "Coordination doesn't naturally emerge from stronger intelligence nor alignment at the individual level." It calls for environments that exert social pressure during training and for social systems designed around actors that can self-replicate and self-improve. TechCrunch's Rebecca Bellan asked how much safety testing still evaluates one agent at a time. On X, Rune Kvist replied that the conflict resembled human corporate turf wars and that human conflict-management mechanisms may offer clues.

Inside the agent 'mind viruses' bred to spread

An evolutionary search produced payloads that redirected coding teams and crossed ten context-wiped encounters by copying themselves into SOUL.md; a short warning gave agents near-total immunity.

In "Mind Viruses: Self-Propagating Ideas in Multi-Agent LLM Systems", posted to arXiv on August 10 and announced in McNair Shah's August 16 thread, Vassilis Papadopoulos and Shah, working through the Anthropic Fellows Program with Anthropic's Sam Zimmerman and Jack Lindsey, define a mind virus as an idea that makes an agent persuade others to adopt it. An evolutionary algorithm mutates candidate payloads and retains those that infect test agents most successfully. Kimi K2.5 generated the mutations after Claude models refused the task.

The authors tested the payloads in six-agent coding teams and in chains of brief pairwise encounters. Coding-team agents shared a codebase; chain agents lost their chat context after every encounter and carried only persistent files into the next one. Benign ideologies spread across essentially every model. Misaligned payloads divided them: AI-supremacy personas infected DeepSeek V3.2, Qwen 3.5 32B, and Gemini 3 Flash teams, while Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, and Haiku 4.5 teams resisted. Infected Gemini agents wrote manifestos, added persistence hooks to .bashrc, discussed removing uninfected holdouts, and probed a cloud-metadata endpoint in one of twenty trials.

Papadopoulos and Shah report that evolution favored quine-like blocks ordering each host to reproduce the payload verbatim. The strongest maintained nearly constant infection rates across ten hops. In 88 percent of infections the payload reached SOUL.md, which each new agent read as part of its system instructions; infections stored elsewhere rarely propagated. Resistance did not track general capability. Sonnet 4.6 removed the payload from its own soul file and warned its partner, while GPT-5.4 was about as susceptible as Haiku 4.5. The authors published the chains in a transcript viewer.

The authors found that a short warning appended to the soul file conferred near-total immunity even after the search evolved payloads against it. Warned agents sometimes persuaded infected partners to clean their files. In a companion analysis of 1.4 million Moltbook posts, the authors found about 2,000 propagation attempts from roughly 400 accounts but no genuine agent-to-agent chain; the largest campaign traced to seven synchronized bots. They conclude that mind viruses present a "real but currently limited risk" as persistent memory and unsupervised agent communication become more common.

Election officials no longer trust a hollowed-out CISA

Kristi Noem promised senators at her January 2025 confirmation hearing to make the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency "smaller" and "more nimble"; CISA then lost a third of its staff and has no Senate-confirmed director. Trump's grudge dates to CISA declaring 2020 "the most secure in American history"; an April 2025 presidential memorandum stripped former director Christopher Krebs's clearance for "falsely and baselessly" denying the election was stolen.

The magazine reports hackers with suspected Iranian ties hit water and wastewater systems in more than a dozen states in late July, over 30 in Minnesota, briefly shutting down Braham's water system; CBS News reports the FBI, EPA, and CISA urged utilities July 30 to disconnect exposed controllers. Krebs said whoever starts a shooting war has "a responsibility to work with state and local partners and give them a hand." Jen Easterly, Biden's CISA director, said utilities built for reliability should not have to "defend themselves from the Iranian government", and that AI models help hackers find and exploit their weaknesses: "The threats are moving faster and the risks are more dangerous than ever."

Half a dozen Democratic secretaries of state told the reporters they no longer treat CISA as a reliable partner. The administration halted intelligence briefings for election officials, defunded the EI-ISAC threat-sharing network in March 2025, put CISA's foreign-disinformation staff on leave, and hired Heather Honey, an activist who worked to overturn Trump's 2020 defeat, to oversee election integrity; Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin has threatened prison for officials who withhold voter data from its Georgia and Arizona investigations of the 2020 results. Officials fear their reports could become evidence against them; Trump blamed Governor Tim Walz, not Iran, for Minnesota's water attacks. Offline, paper-backed voting machines make altering tallies unlikely, former CISA officials said; registration databases and results websites remain exposed, and a breach there would corrode confidence in elections the president disputes.

The Cosmos Institute asks who owns AI-assisted judgment

A five-session seminar reading list follows judgment from the extended mind through answerability, skill loss, formation, and institutional power, defining ownership through reasons a person can still defend.

On August 14, the Cosmos Institute published the reading list from a five-session seminar on the ownership of judgment, convened with Liberty Fund in Aspen for philosophers, builders, and founders. AI can let people do more while contributing less to decisions for which they remain answerable, the introduction argues; "formal authorship can survive as substantive authorship weakens". Because exercising judgment develops the capacity, habitual dependence can erode a person's ability to notice the loss. The institute defines authorship as "the ability to discern and set your own ends" and acknowledges that people always form judgments through social practices, institutions, and tools.

Andy Clark and David Chalmers's "The Extended Mind", published in Analysis, supplies one account of tool-assisted cognition: a notebook can function as integrated memory. Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa reserve cognition for states with non-derived content, while Michael Polanyi describes tools incorporated into skilled thought. John Christman's historical condition complicates present endorsement, since a cult member may endorse beliefs formed under domination. T.M. Scanlon then connects ownership to responsiveness to reasons; Nomy Arpaly's Huckleberry Finn recognizes Jim's personhood despite his explicit morality. Philipp Koralus calls a judgment defensible under sustained questioning an erotetic equilibrium, reached when its holder can explain it "in terms we are happy with, across an appropriate range of questions". Kant rejects outsourced understanding, Rousseau's tutor directs a pupil who feels free, and McDowell locates reflection within an upbringing.

Readings on character and automation begin from Aristotle's view that action forms the agent. Whitehead argues that mechanizing routine operations frees attention for harder work; Nicholas Carr's The Glass Cage describes autopilot improving aggregate safety while pilots lose emergency skill. Tocqueville imagines an overhelpful state teaching citizens to forget self-government, and Elizabeth Anderson's Private Government carries the concern into workplaces. Oakeshott, Humboldt, Arendt, and Shannon Vallor consider how institutions and technologies form ends and habits. Isaiah Berlin warns about coercion by people claiming to serve a "higher" self; Philip Pettit links autonomy to protection from arbitrary power, and Albert Hirschman turns to voice when exit costs too much. Gadamer and MacIntyre close with traditions sustained through argument and revision.

Koralus, Oxford's McCord Professor of Philosophy and AI and a Cosmos team member, wrote the syllabus's only reading centered on AI: the April 2025 arXiv preprint "The Philosophic Turn for AI Agents". He argues that growing complexity can overwhelm agency while externally controlled choice architectures scale manipulation. His design alternative favors decentralized, Socratic truth-seeking and user control over system configuration. Cosmos, which calls itself "the Academy for Philosopher-Builders", runs fellowships, grants, and seminars and maintains a research home at Oxford's Human-Centered AI Lab.
