Authors' filings trace OpenAI's LibGen downloads and deletions

The authors' statement of facts, refiled on September 17 with fewer redactions, quotes OpenAI staff on the LibGen books behind GPT-3 and GPT-3.5; OpenAI and Microsoft argue the downloads served a transformative purpose.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 September 2026 1440 words · ~7 min

The Authors Guild's September 21 statement accuses OpenAI and Microsoft of knowingly exploiting pirated books, citing the author plaintiffs' summary-judgment memorandum and 162-page statement of facts. The Guild is itself a plaintiff. Its chief executive, Mary Rasenberger, accused the companies of contempt for authors and their work. The filings quote discovery documents and testimony to argue for liability. They were refiled September 17 under a September 3 sealing order requiring parties to remove redactions nobody sought to retain. Much of the LibGen detail had been obscured in the September 5 public version.

According to the statement, OpenAI began downloading books in 2018 from Library Genesis, or LibGen, a shadow library the U.S. Trade Representative lists as a notorious piracy market. Alec Radford downloaded more than 117,000 books from a LibGen mirror that October, using a script run on a personal computer at his home. A month later OpenAI priced lawful copies at $10 to $15 each, but it did not go ahead with buying any in 2019. David Lansky, then OpenAI's general counsel, proposed obtaining books from LibGen without paying in February 2019. That April Sam Altman shared with Bill Gates a document, also sent to other Microsoft executives, saying OpenAI had added about 11 billion words from LibGen; Microsoft agreed that July to invest $1 billion.

Between September 2019 and January 2020, the statement says, OpenAI employees Benjamin Mann and Chris Hallacy torrented about 35 terabytes, all of LibGen's fiction and nonfiction, which then totaled roughly 4.6 million book files. Two training datasets built from the downloads, LibGen1 and LibGen2, held about 570,000 books. OpenAI's own summary-judgment brief acknowledges that LibGen compilations trained GPT-3 and GPT-3.5, and says no other model at issue used LibGen material.

The plaintiffs also describe how OpenAI kept LibGen's name out of its research papers. In July 2019 Sam McCandlish proposed removing it from a draft of the scaling-laws paper, worried about Hacker News readers criticizing OpenAI for getting copyrighted material from a disreputable Russian website; the published version refers to "Internet Books." The GPT-3 paper of May 2020 describes Books1 and Books2 as collections of books obtained online; when Jack Clark asked for more detail in a draft, Mann replied that it was "deliberately vague since it’s libgen." Two months later Ryan Lowe weighed continued use of LibGen in a book-summarization project against the risk of controversy on Twitter. OpenAI served LibGen books in chunks to outside contractors for that project, the plaintiffs say, and has no records of whether they kept them. The Guild statement recirculated on Hacker News on September 27.

The plaintiffs quote a late-2021 exchange in which John Schulman, supposing that Anthropic had scraped LibGen more thoroughly, considered copying that approach and overruling the lawyers. OpenAI deleted its LibGen datasets the following summer. On June 15, 2022, Bob McGrew, then vice president of research, argued that OpenAI should now remove LibGen data from its systems and storage. Removal would end OpenAI's ability to reproduce GPT-3 or GPT-3.5, he noted, but "would be very valuable for legal reasons." Employees first named the Slack channel for the effort "excise-libgen"; Jason Kwon, then general counsel, renamed it "project-clear." The deletion list ran past 498 entries, and the plaintiffs say the LibGen compilations are the only training datasets OpenAI has ever deleted.

In his February 6, 2026 order, Judge Sidney H. Stein recounts the dispute over OpenAI's explanation. Its lawyers cited non-use in March and April 2024, attempted to withdraw that account in June 2025, then said OpenAI would advance no nonprivileged reason. Magistrate Judge Ona Wang found a privilege waiver that November; Stein set it aside, holding that the non-use statements disclosed no privileged advice. He called instructions blocking questions about nonprivileged facts improper, but upheld rejection of the crime-fraud exception. OpenAI had recovered and produced versions of the datasets; plaintiffs disputed their completeness. The authors also cite a June 2022 message about active instruction-following experiments, while OpenAI's designee testified that no model was training on the datasets when they were deleted.

The plaintiffs' memorandum quotes February 2026 depositions in which Altman and Satya Nadella agreed generally that piracy is illegal. For their claim that Microsoft is vicariously liable, the authors cite its representative's acknowledgment that Microsoft knew before GPT-3's release that OpenAI used LibGen to train it. Microsoft's brief denies any involvement in acquiring the datasets and argues that their source does not alter the fair-use analysis.

The authors seek liability rulings covering 194 works, challenging downloading, training and distribution separately. They rely on Judge William Alsup's June 2025 ruling in Bartz v. Anthropic, which found training Claude on books to be fair use while reserving claims over pirated copies retained in a central library for trial. Alsup also wrote that neither subsequent training nor deletion would excuse pirating books available for purchase, while expressly saying that his decision did not require that broader conclusion. Anthropic later settled for $1.5 billion. The authors argue that OpenAI's LibGen copies likewise stocked a library that employees and contractors could use for purposes of their own choosing, with millions of the books never used for training. They distinguish Judge Vince Chhabria's 2025 ruling for Meta in Kadrey v. Meta, where Meta downloaded books only to train.

OpenAI argues that it downloaded books solely to pretrain GPT-3 and GPT-3.5, treating acquisition and training as one transformative process for extracting linguistic patterns. It cites the Second Circuit's 2015 Google Books decision, a loss for the Authors Guild, which in OpenAI's reading judged Google's scanning of more than four million books together with its search purpose. OpenAI says there is no evidence it downloaded the books to assemble a library for purposes beyond training, and both companies call Alsup's broader language non-binding dicta. The U.S. Copyright Office's May 2025 pre-publication report treats knowing use of datasets containing pirated or illegally accessed works as a factor counting against fair use, without making it sufficient to decide the outcome. The Justice Department, in a September 1 statement in this litigation, argued that training is fair use and that the report deserves no deference.

On market harm, the authors cite a May 2020 memo, also covered in our September 17 report on the news publishers' parallel brief, in which Jack Clark, then OpenAI's policy director, warned that genre fiction authors would fear AI-generated writing replacing their work on Amazon. They add posts by Tarun Gogineni, whom OpenAI hired in 2022 to work on its models' writing, predicting that GPT-5 would finish George R.R. Martin's series. Their expert Shawn Shan found that GPT-4o could produce a book of roughly 20,000 words for about 90 cents in under ten minutes. OpenAI reports 14 matches to plaintiffs' books in 20 million ChatGPT conversation logs containing 95 million outputs: 0.00007% of conversations. Its economist reported that sales trends did not show a systematic decline. OpenAI argues that copyright does not shield authors from competition by new works that do not infringe. The authors lean on Chhabria's reasoning in Kadrey that competition from AI-generated works can substitute for the originals indirectly, though he ruled for Meta because those plaintiffs offered no evidence of it.

On September 23 OpenAI and Microsoft moved to strike a study the statement relies on for that argument: "Generative AI floods and dilutes the market for books" by Tuhin Chakrabarty, Xinyue Liu, Jane C. Ginsburg and Paramveer Dhillon, an arXiv paper whose finding that AI-heavy books took a growing share of genre-fiction sales we covered on July 29. The companies say a version of Chakrabarty's CV listed $100,000 from the class counsel, Susman Godfrey, as an unrestricted research gift beginning in December 2025; Susman, they say, did not mention the gift when it disclosed Chakrabarty and Dhillon as experts. Neither served an expert report; the paper reached the case through the plaintiffs' damages expert, Michael Lasinski. According to the motion, Chakrabarty has since revised the CV, correcting the entry to describe research for In re Mosaic LLM Litigation for which his institution received a lower payment. Class counsel, the motion says, did not deny that Susman funded the research. The motion seeks to exclude the study and Lasinski's supplemental report.

In a September 25 memorandum, the plaintiffs asked the court to exclude an OpenAI expert's opinion that using Library Genesis was widespread in the industry, arguing that its prevalence could not excuse copyright infringement. Oppositions to the summary-judgment motions are due October 9 and replies November 6. Provisionally sealed exhibits that no one moves to seal must be filed publicly by January 27, 2027, and the Guild anticipates a hearing early in 2027.
