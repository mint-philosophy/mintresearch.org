Bamberg researchers debate how to validate consciousness tests

Recordings from the June workshop examine how evidence from adults can inform tests for infants, insects and AI, and the assumptions required to make those comparisons.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 22 September 2026 1023 words · ~5 min

Tel Aviv University's Liad Mudrik noted that following commands can indicate consciousness in a brain-injured patient, although chatbots follow commands too. At Bamberg's June 8-10 workshop on consciousness tests, she used that comparison to explain why researchers must justify a test's interpretation separately for different populations. Johannes Kleiner shared the recordings on September 20. Across the sessions, researchers proposed ways to validate tests for infants, animals and AI while disagreeing about how much those methods could establish independently of a theory of consciousness.

In her talk, Mudrik compared three approaches: reasoning from similarities between systems together with the best explanation of their behavior; deriving indicators from theories of consciousness; and repeatedly comparing several tests across populations. The theory-based approach can specify computations to seek inside an AI system, but its conclusions depend on both the theory and the assumption that the relevant computations would support consciousness outside a biological brain. Mudrik treats that assumption as useful for research while remaining undecided about its truth.

Mudrik argued that researchers can already apply the proposal in Tim Bayne and colleagues' 2024 Trends in Cognitive Sciences review, “Tests for consciousness in humans and beyond.” Researchers begin with several measures in adults whose experiences they can check, then examine whether the measures continue to agree in other populations. If one stops agreeing with the others, they reconsider both the test and their original judgments about adults. The working hypothesis is that a shared underlying phenomenon explains why the indicators occur together. Mudrik added a practical safeguard: repeat the process in different population orders, because testing infants before animals could change which tests receive the most weight.

Ruhr University Bochum's Leonard Dung challenged the promised neutrality of this natural-kind strategy. Researchers must still justify identifying a mechanism that explains correlated indicators with consciousness itself. Doing so requires assumptions about what the concept of consciousness refers to. Similarly, telling researchers to postpone testing existing theories requires an argument that those theories are inadequate, which their proponents may reject. Dung favors an ambitious version of the strategy that explicitly defends these commitments. A broadly acceptable instruction to use ordinary scientific methods would accomplish less.

NYU's Claudia Passos Ferreira examined three proposed tests in infants: reciprocal activity between large brain networks, responses to violations of a learned sound pattern, and the attentional blink, in which processing one target interferes with detecting another shortly afterward. These candidate markers appear at different ages. Collecting them across several months cannot by itself pinpoint when consciousness began; she argues that several appearing together within the same developmental period provide stronger evidence of a shared cause.

Passos Ferreira also gives greater weight to demonstrated functions than to whether an infant's anatomy already resembles an adult's. A baby may integrate information before the relevant brain networks have fully matured. Conversely, failure to point or communicate intentionally can reflect missing abilities needed to express an experience. She therefore distinguishes early indicators of conscious perception from later capacities that may be needed only for more elaborate behavior. Her proposal combines observations with commitments shared across theories, such as the relevance of information integration, without requiring agreement on one complete theory.

London School of Economics philosopher Jonathan Birch argued for studying insects through several experimental contrasts. Researchers can compare active and quiet sleep, different kinds of learning, and responses to anesthesia, then ask whether the same neural mechanisms help explain the differences. One precedent he discussed was Dhruv Grover and colleagues' 2022 Nature paper, “Differential mechanisms underlie trace and delay conditioning in Drosophila.” Using virtual reality and brain imaging, the researchers found that distraction disrupted learning when a time gap separated a visual cue from its consequence, while sparing learning with overlapping stimuli. Birch wants to investigate whether the circuitry supporting such differences also explains other consciousness-related contrasts. That comparison could help distinguish mechanisms specific to a learning task from mechanisms with a wider role.

NYU's David Chalmers proposed investigating the computations behind apparent AI introspection. His starting point included Jack Lindsey's 2025 Anthropic study, “Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models,” which found that models could sometimes identify concepts injected into their internal activity. Chalmers argued that detecting an internal change does not establish the richer introspection relevant to consciousness. His proposed investigation would look for processes that function like attending to an experience and judging what that experience is like, without assuming that an experience actually exists.

Chalmers called these processes quasi-introspection; establishing them would require tracing internal representations and their causal roles, beyond collecting fluent self-reports. On his weaker proposal, a positive test would support either consciousness or its functional illusion: internal states that lead a system to judge itself conscious. He also considered whether such processes normally indicate actual experience, a stronger claim that needs further philosophical argument. He presented the proposal as an early research program, with substantial work still needed to specify the representations and identify them inside models.

Kleiner proposed a statistical method for transferring tests between populations. Suppose researchers know when adult subjects are conscious and how accurately a test detects that, while also measuring every other factor affecting its result, such as attention, working memory and auditory development. His method compares adults and infants with matching values of those factors, then uses the adult measurements and the difference in positive results to estimate the proportion of infants who are conscious. It would not require separately establishing that a particular cognitive ability entails consciousness in infants.

Kleiner emphasized the demanding conditions. Researchers must identify all relevant influences on the test and have comparable cases in both populations; if the measured factors perfectly distinguish adults from infants, the comparison fails. The method also assumes that, after those factors are matched, population membership has no further effect on the test result. He acknowledged that identifying every influence may be impractical and that the formalism needs development. In its current form, the method estimates consciousness across a population and cannot yet diagnose individual members. The immediate research task is to determine whether an existing test, such as detecting violations of sound patterns, can meet those conditions.
