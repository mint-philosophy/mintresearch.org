Meta rolled back Muse’s human-caller test after employee objections

Jason Koebler reports that a tester learned only afterward that a person had made their call. Reuters adds an internal acknowledgment of inadequate disclosures and a rollback.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 22 September 2026 653 words · ~3 min

Meta employees objected after a Muse tester learned only afterward that a human contractor had made a call they had asked the AI assistant to handle, Jason Koebler reports in 404 Media on September 22. Koebler reviewed an internal announcement describing a confidential employee test: Muse could pass requests to trained workers, who would place calls and complete tasks. The service would report back with a transcript and summary. Human assistance was being tested internally while an AI calling beta was already available publicly.

The employee exchanges Koebler reviewed describe a tester who supplied information expecting AI to handle it, then became concerned on learning that a person had called. Employees challenged Meta’s reassurance that contractors had extensive training to keep data safe; one argued that training did not itself provide a security mechanism. Another urged Meta to make human assistance a user preference before launching it by default. Their objections concerned both information reaching contractors and customers’ willingness to delegate to people, even with disclosure. Koebler illustrates the privacy concern with a hypothetical request to arrange a sensitive doctor’s appointment. His account leaves the conditions and frequency of human handoffs unspecified.

Meta told Koebler that employee feedback had been overwhelmingly positive and that internal testing was intended to improve safeguards before public release. The company said it was working with merchants and would introduce the potential feature only when ready, with appropriate disclosures. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts gave the same response to Reuters.

Reuters subsequently reported that Meta had rolled back the internal feature. In company posts reviewed by the news agency, a vice president acknowledged that testing had begun without adequate disclosures. Half the company’s employees had been included, with an opt-out group available. Another employee said a transcript of a call to negotiate an internet and cable bill contained a racist reference by the contractor. The vice president apologized and said that worker would no longer work on Meta projects. She also described some tests in which human callers achieved success rates of 95-98%, higher than the AI callers.

The public beta had expanded on September 16, when Ryan Fox announced calls to US businesses, prioritizing people who had requested access. Replies described both failed calls and difficulty checking what the assistant had said. Jeff Kahn reported businesses hanging up, which he attributed to suspected spam. Suvamsh Shivaprasad requested the audio, saying a text transcript did not let him fully audit a call. These accounts precede the reporting on human assistance and do not identify who made the calls.

In its technical explanation of Muse’s safeguards, Meta says the launch system restricts employee access through operational policies, while allowing Meta access needed to operate, support or secure the service. The company described a forthcoming Confidential VM, an isolated cloud computer intended to prevent its own access through cryptographic protections. Its existing approval system sends decisions about sensitive actions directly to users through the app. In that documentation, human approval means the account holder authorizing a particular action. A contractor carrying out the request would also receive information about the task.

Facebook described an earlier use of human operators in 2015. In Mike Schroepfer’s account of the M assistant, operators evaluated the AI’s suggested responses and then produced responses while it learned from them; scaling required automating most requests. Google’s Yaniv Leviathan and Yossi Matias explained in their 2018 Duplex announcement that the calling system could summon an operator when it could not complete a task. They separately described operators supervising calls during training. Human participants could therefore teach a system or finish work it could not perform independently.

We covered Amazon’s refusal to let Muse shop on its site yesterday. The employee calling test adds a separate dispute over who can act on a user’s behalf. Employees quoted by Koebler wanted that choice to cover outside workers who might read their requests and speak for them.
