Can an LLM make an assertion if someone else is accountable?

A new Synthese paper argues that treating chatbot replies as assertions while assigning responsibility to their makers unsettles the conversational norms that make assertions useful for inquiry.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 22 September 2026 592 words · ~3 min

When a chatbot gives an answer, offers reasons and retracts a claim after a challenge, it can look as though it is making an assertion. The problem is identifying who is answerable for that assertion. In a September 22 paper in Synthese, philosophers Germán Massaguer Gómez and Antonio Gaitán Torres argue that two attractive answers to these questions are unstable when combined in conversations aimed at gaining knowledge.

The first answer is nonpsychological functionalism: an utterance can count as an assertion because of what it does in a public exchange, without requiring a speaker who believes or intends its content. A model's answer may present a proposition as true, invite requests for evidence and be revised when challenged. The second answer is a proxy account of responsibility: because the model itself cannot be held responsible in the usual way, a designer, developer or deploying organization is treated as the human principal behind its outputs. One account explains the apparent conversational act; the other attempts to close its responsibility gap. Massaguer Gómez and Gaitán Torres ask what happens when both are made visible and workable in the same exchange.

The authors do not claim a logical contradiction, and they restrict their argument to knowledge-oriented conversation rather than every chat with an AI system. Their concern is what they call an ideal-relative tension. Functional accounts with serious epistemic ambitions depend on participants being able to hold one another to conversational commitments. The paper identifies three practical norms: knowing to whom a claim is attributable, being able to revise or retract it in response to evidence, and being able to check its reliability. It argues that explicitly locating responsibility outside the exchange changes what those norms can accomplish inside it.

Attribution provides a concrete example. A lawyer speaking for a client has a recognizable principal whose position is pertinent to the conversation. An LLM's possible principals are more diffuse: the people who developed, trained and deployed it need not have adopted the particular answer it generated. An interface could disclose the institutional relationship, but disclosure does not create a participant who can defend this claim in real time. Similarly, a model may replace an answer when challenged without making the epistemic adjustment a responsible speaker would make. Citations can help a user verify an answer, but much of the weighing of evidence and reconciliation of competing reasons remains the user's work.

Those difficulties matter, the authors argue, because the functionalist view draws its force from two ideals. Internal regulation means participants themselves, rather than an external authority, monitor claims and enforce standards of inquiry. Conversational civility involves reciprocal respect and equal standing in that exchange. If the human principals are absent and the model has no accountable standpoint of its own, a conversation with it cannot straightforwardly deliver those ideals even if its outputs satisfy several outward tests for assertion. Effective proxy signaling clarifies who may ultimately bear responsibility, but makes the institutional mediation harder to ignore.

The paper considers replies to this objection, including more gradual disclosure, different standards for different conversational settings, and the obvious usefulness of LLMs for learning and exploration. Its conclusion leaves that usefulness intact. A model can be an epistemic enabler, helping a person find, frame and test ideas; the authors argue that the gain should not be mistaken for a reciprocal conversational partner's contribution to collective inquiry. This is a conceptual argument about how two theories fit together, not an empirical finding that every model answer fails or that no organization can bear legal responsibility for one.
