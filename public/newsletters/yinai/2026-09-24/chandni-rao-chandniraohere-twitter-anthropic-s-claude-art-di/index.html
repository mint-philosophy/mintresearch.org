Rao and Leonescu challenge Anthropic's account of Claude's enzyme discovery

Rao wants clearer credit for earlier human research. Leonescu asks what the experiments establish. The paper cites the precursor and confirms RNA expression, while leaving the system's function unknown.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 September 2026 921 words · ~5 min

Chandni Rao and Ana Leonescu have challenged how Anthropic presents Claude's contribution to its enzyme-system finding. Rao argues that the discovery narrative gives too little prominence to earlier human research; Leonescu questions language that compresses candidate identification, experimental validation and biological understanding into a single AI achievement. Both accept that the finding is meaningful. The underlying array-associated reverse transcriptase (ART) system, its human laboratory tests and its unknown function were covered here on September 23.

Rao posted her criticism on X that evening. Her starting point was “Comparative Genomics of Three Novel Jumbo Bacteriophages Infecting Staphylococcus aureus,” published in Journal of Virology in 2021 by Abby M. Korn, Andrew E. Hillhouse, Lichang Sun and Jason J. Gill at Texas A&M University. The team identified genes predicted to encode reverse transcriptases, which copy RNA into DNA, in three large bacterial viruses. In one, MarsHill, they identified a 1.2-kilobase upstream noncoding region and proposed that it contained an associated RNA. They considered an antiviral role but could not identify a partner protein that would execute it. They left open the possibility that such a partner existed elsewhere in the genome.

Rao credits Claude with recognising the repeated DNA and intervening spacers in that region, finding similar arrangements across related viruses and identifying associated partner proteins. She calls the repeat architecture “a meaningful discovery”. Her objection is that readers should encounter the earlier team's contribution within the account of how the discovery happened. She supplied a replacement passage, which she said she asked Claude to write, naming Korn and colleagues and describing their observations before the agents' new finding.

Anthropic's 40-page technical report, “Autonomous AI agents discover reverse transcriptases with tandem repeat arrays,” by Peter H. Yoon and colleagues, cites Korn's paper twice in its prose: in the Methods' comparison of two viral genomes and the final Discussion paragraph. It is absent from the Introduction and Results citations. In that closing paragraph, the authors describe the MarsHill example as evidence that agents can find patterns missed by human attention. They acknowledge the earlier enzyme and proposed RNA, then say the genome report “yet described neither the repeats nor the partner gene”. Rao reads this as a backhanded compliment. Anthropic's public announcement acknowledges that previous studies identified the underlying enzyme, without naming Korn's paper or team.

On September 24, Ross Wilson asked whether an earlier version had lacked the citation. Rao clarified that she had not intended to imply Anthropic failed to cite Korn. Her complaint was the framing: “their work reads like a footnote”.

Rao also asks how much the agents' inference depended on prior knowledge. Figure 1E shows an agent naming MarsHill's 1,241-base upstream region before the repeat discovery, which she interprets as following existing clues. The authors describe a supervisor asking a worker to look for the upstream RNA expected in retron systems. Their transcript analysis says neither the research instructions nor that task mentioned repeats; the worker recognised them after reading unannotated DNA from a different viral sequence. These observations establish what the worker saw immediately before recognition. They do not settle whether earlier literature or training data informed its investigation.

The Methods' use of the MarsHill enzyme to define the family concerns subsequent human-directed analyses, so it does not establish that the autonomous search began from Korn's paper. The report does not identify whether the agents retrieved that paper. Rao's proposed wording would make the earlier contribution more visible, but its suggestion that the agents started from that work remains her interpretation.

Leonescu's September 24 LessWrong essay addresses how the result was communicated to nonspecialists. She identifies herself as a former laboratory chemist working in chemical and biological security and AI evaluations, unaffiliated with Anthropic. She expressly limits her analysis to the announcement and website, excluding the technical preprint. She welcomes substantial AI assistance with generating hypotheses and selecting candidates, while arguing that describing the whole process as Claude's discovery obscures the humans' responsibility for experimental validation and interpretation.

Leonescu distinguishes identifying a candidate system, demonstrating its existence, establishing what it does and understanding how it works. She accepts the result's validity and acknowledges that further experiments, patents or journal publication could explain withholding details. Her criticism is the decision to announce publicly with little explanation of what was tested, how robust the findings were and which functions remained hypothetical. She worries that readers will infer AI has surpassed specialist scientists from evidence of a narrower contribution.

Anthropic's announcement does identify human laboratory work and says the system's function is unknown. It describes the first experiments as showing that the array produces distinct short RNAs. The technical report makes the division of labour more explicit: the initial search ran autonomously, while the authors directed subsequent analyses in interactive Claude sessions.

A comment by eniteris objects that the proposed mechanism exceeds the experimental evidence. The study did produce experimental results: researchers expressed an ART system from phage SA1 on plasmids in E. coli and sequenced its RNA, finding distinct short transcripts. They compared these with existing data from SA1 infection of Staphylococcus lentus, where array-derived RNAs were abundant. The infection experiment was earlier published work, separate from Anthropic's new plasmid assays.

Yoon and colleagues describe the mechanism in Figure 3I as putative, with dashed arrows marking hypothesised steps. Their Discussion states that they have not shown the reverse transcriptase to be active or the array's RNAs to be its substrates. Whether the enzyme interacts with its proposed partner, and what the system does for the virus, remain unknown.
