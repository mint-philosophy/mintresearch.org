Paris Call sets out evaluation, shutdown and whistleblower commitments

The November 9 declaration extends developers’ shutdown commitments to training and internal use, promises public evaluation findings, and assigns whistleblower channels to governments. Its voluntary pledges depend on national implementation.

The Paris Call for AI safety and security, published by the Élysée on November 9, commits governments, researchers and developers to shared evaluation methods, public findings and earlier warning of serious AI incidents. Developers separately promise shutdown mechanisms that reach into training and internal use; governments undertake to provide protected reporting channels for whistleblowers. We covered Adam Gleave’s response yesterday. The declaration’s detailed provisions explain how its signatories propose to make advanced AI safer, and which decisions they leave to national authorities.

The Élysée presents frontier AI risks as inherently international: development, deployment and harms cross borders, so no government or company can manage them alone. Its signatories include France, Canada and other governments alongside developers such as Anthropic, OpenAI and Mistral AI. Civil-society organizations have also endorsed it, and endorsements remain open. The declaration is a voluntary political commitment. It creates no international enforcement authority or sanctions, and specifies no implementation deadlines.

Under the first commitment, policymakers, researchers and developers will build a common framework for evaluating and managing frontier-model risks, using existing frameworks as a starting point. The named dangers include cyberattacks and chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear misuse, alongside loss of control as models become more autonomous or act contrary to human intentions. Signatories want technical standards and testing methods that keep pace with those capabilities. They identify the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science, or NAAIMES, as one existing network that could help make results comparable and accessible across countries.

The signatories also agree to publish non-sensitive evaluation findings in a standard format and create a shared public repository. Possible contents include methods for adversarial testing, benchmark tools, risk assessments and stress-test results. Publishing the methods alongside findings would help readers compare assessments that otherwise use different tests. The text does not assign an organization to build the repository or set a date for publication, and the commitment concerns non-sensitive findings, leaving room to withhold information whose disclosure would create risks.

The second commitment puts national authorities in charge of the safety standards that signatories pledge to follow throughout a model’s life. Those standards may be established or recognized by the relevant authority; oversight should include competent, independent third-party evaluation. Policymakers acknowledge that legal frameworks need to clarify responsibility for large-scale damage, including cross-border harm, both to protect those affected and to give developers legal certainty. These provisions describe work for national governments and regulators, with legal duties depending on implementation in their jurisdictions.

Developers undertake to disclose capabilities, limitations and known risks. Their support for qualified independent evaluation is expressly conditional: “where appropriate and proportionate to the risk”, and consistent with existing practices and arrangements. The text does not guarantee unrestricted access to a model’s internals or specify how much time evaluators receive. Its general endorsement of independent testing therefore leaves substantive decisions about access and testing conditions unresolved.

Developers also commit to fail-safe mechanisms for stopping model operations they control, expressly covering training and internal use. The examples range from restrictions and pauses to shutdowns. That reach includes a developer’s own use of a model before making it available to customers. Developers further recognize the need for continuous risk management, monitoring of emerging capabilities and internal adversarial testing, and commit to work on governance with defined responsibilities, sufficient resources and independent oversight. On open weights, they acknowledge the danger that safeguards can be removed or bypassed while affirming the benefits for research, innovation and safety.

The declaration names the 2024 Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments among its predecessors. Seoul’s company signatories undertook to define thresholds of intolerable risk and, in the extreme, refrain from developing or deploying systems when mitigations could not keep risks below those thresholds. Paris specifies shutdown mechanisms but does not define equivalent thresholds or a rule that triggers their use. The two texts address related decisions: maintaining the ability to stop operations, and deciding when development or deployment must stop.

Under the third commitment, policymakers, researchers and developers pledge to cooperate on information sharing, reporting and early warnings. They propose building rapid alerts on existing incident-response arrangements, including cybersecurity systems. The text recognizes the value of aligning incident categories and exchanging timely information about serious cyber and AI-control incidents, as well as warning signs of serious harm. Exchanges could take place between companies and governments, among governments or among companies. A further cooperation mechanism remains something to explore; the declaration specifies no reporting deadlines or central incident authority.

Governments take on the explicit whistleblower-channel commitment. Policymakers promise secure, confidential routes through which employees and other stakeholders can raise reasonably grounded concerns about systemic risks without legal or professional retaliation. Developers separately recognize that internal reporting arrangements should protect researchers. Confidential channels and protection from retaliation are therefore stated objectives, with governments responsible for supplying the channels and national implementation determining how those protections work.

Gleave’s response identifies practical conditions for effective evaluation: he says FAR.AI commonly receives less than a week to test a model and wants access to inspect internals and conduct further training. The AI Evaluator Forum’s AEF-1 standard says substantially novel systems often require at least 20 business days, with time proportionate to the confidence needed and the system’s novelty. Yoshua Bengio welcomed the meeting while calling for mandatory audits by evaluators developers do not choose, and demonstrations of safety before a model can pose grave danger, including before deployment. Both responses identify requirements that the Paris declaration leaves for further decisions.

Alignment and Control

Language models can learn multistep computation using meaningless dots in place of readable intermediate reasoning, Wuschel Schulz and tvb report in "LLMs can learn (some) multi-step reasoning on filler tokens" on LessWrong. Working through MARS 5 and the Meridian Visiting Researcher Programme, they checked predictions at each dot during training against the correct intermediate result while feeding only dots into subsequent positions; two models learned the behavior on three of seven synthetic task families, outperforming training on final answers alone. The visible intermediate text contained only dots; the study did not test a monitor.

Models learn to reason through strings of dots

Schulz and tvb trained two models to solve multistep tasks with dots in place of written reasoning. Their method teaches the intermediate answers explicitly, then keeps those answers out of the text the models read.

Language models can be trained to solve multistep problems while the text between question and answer consists entirely of dots. Wuschel Schulz and a coauthor writing as tvb report this in “LLMs can learn (some) multi-step reasoning on filler tokens”, a November 11 LessWrong research update from MARS 5 and the Meridian Visiting Researcher Programme. Their specially trained models improved on three of seven synthetic task families compared with training on final answers alone. One model learned to track changes to three lamps through 60 moves. The experiment shows how useful computation can proceed without readable intermediate text under an engineered training procedure.

Schulz and tvb fine-tuned Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and gpt-oss-20b using small trainable additions to the models, called adapters. At each dot position, they trained the model to predict the corresponding intermediate state of a worked solution. The next input remained a dot: the predicted state was never written into the text for the model to read back. Information could still travel between positions through the network’s internal representations. During evaluation, the researchers supplied the dots in advance and read predictions from the model. Left to generate its own text, the trained model would write out its reasoning. This differs from ordinary next-token training, and the result does not establish that deployed assistants spontaneously reason this way.

The researchers compared their procedure with a precedent from Jacob Pfau, William Merrill and Samuel Bowman at New York University. Their 2024 arXiv paper, “Let’s Think Dot by Dot: Hidden Computation in Transformer Language Models”, showed that models could use meaningless extra tokens to solve algorithmic problems they struggled with when answering immediately. Its training mixed worked solutions with examples containing dots followed by an answer. Schulz and tvb instead supervised the intermediate prediction at every dot. When they compared the two approaches on the same progressively lengthening tasks, with repeated training runs, their method outperformed answer-only training; the older recipe did not.

The authors’ results table indicates that the three clear successes were running sums with remainders, tracking a ball under shuffled cups, and tracking the lamps. In the lamp task, each move either passes the lamps’ states around a ring or toggles one lamp depending on another. Qwen answered 79% of the 60-move test problems correctly; its answer-only counterpart passed no curriculum stage. The researchers advanced training after success on a small checkpoint test, so passing a stage did not mean perfect accuracy on the larger final test. Across the other tasks, gains were small or absent. Models using dots struggled with chained arithmetic, lookup tables and logical deductions, while models trained to write their reasoning reached the longest tested length in every task family.

Pfau and colleagues also explained a theoretical limit: adding filler positions does not give a fixed-depth transformer unlimited capacity for successive computation. Schulz and tvb accept that limit. They investigate how much of a finite sequence of operations can fit within the model’s existing layers. A task described as 60 successive moves need not require 60 network layers, because an algorithm can combine some operations. Their result therefore concerns the practical reach of computation at finite task lengths; it does not show that dots remove the architectural limit.

To explore that reach, Schulz and tvb used Opus 5.5 agents to construct algorithms within an executable approximation of each transformer. They checked that the algorithms used permitted operations and returned the correct intermediate states and answers, then searched for constructions requiring fewer layers. The shallowest algorithms they found grew deeper as tasks lengthened, but usually much more slowly than the number of task steps. These constructions establish upper bounds on sufficient depth. They neither prove the minimum depth required nor show which algorithms the trained models learned. The numerical curves also depend on a chosen assumption that one internal processing operation can act as a lookup table over at most 32 bits of input.

The experiments show that fitting an algorithm into the available layers does not guarantee that training will discover it. Chained arithmetic and function composition had comparatively shallow constructions but stalled after only a few steps in practice. Schulz and tvb suggest that models have difficulty moving larger amounts of information, such as lookup tables or logical premises, between token positions. Their own research on steganographic reasoning motivated the study: when a model answers correctly after producing apparently unrelated text, success alone cannot establish that the words secretly encode the calculation. The model might be using those positions for computation without relying on their textual content.

For oversight, the authors worry that pressure to avoid verbalizing parts of a calculation could eventually teach similar behavior. That possibility is an extrapolation from their specially supervised training runs. They did not test a monitor, train against one, or investigate whether the internal computation could be decoded. Identical dots contain no intermediate reasoning for a text reader to inspect, but the network’s internal states provide a separate source of evidence.

Kaley Brauer, Claudio Mayrink Verdun and Samuel Marks examined that possibility in the separate arXiv paper “Reading Between the Dots: Decoding Hidden Computation across Filler Tokens.” On tasks with identifiable intermediate values, they recovered those values from the hidden states of DeepSeek V3 and Kimi K2 with 82–94% accuracy using their best decoding setup. Their result does not establish that Schulz and tvb’s trained models are equally readable. It shows why losing the written reasoning and losing access to the computation are different questions. Schulz and tvb’s immediate recommendation concerns experiment design: researchers studying concealed reasoning should first check whether their chosen tasks can be solved using filler positions alone.

Philosophy of AI

AI constitutions should preserve disagreement while allowing citizens to revise the values used in model training, Beltoft et al. of the University of Southern Denmark argue in "Living constitutions: Participatory alignment for sovereign foundation models", submitted to arXiv November 10. They propose ongoing participation, with provisions marked as settled, contested or underdetermined. In simulations, the process resisted attempts to introduce harmful provisions with half the citizens acting maliciously; the deciding committee split its seats evenly between randomly selected citizens and developers assumed to act in good faith.

Replacing human intellectual work with equivalent AI outputs can remove opportunities to develop judgment and character. Sasha Mudd of Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile argues this in "Beyond Technocentrism: Kantian Humanism and the Ethics of AI Substitution", published November 5 in the Canadian Journal of Philosophy. Drawing on Kant, she asks institutions to assess how activities develop people's capacities and whether users still learn to explain and defend conclusions; her account permits extensive AI assistance where those opportunities survive.

Also yesterday: Claes Bäckman argues on Substack that AI reviewers can check errors well but may misjudge contributions and reward concealed limitations; Weimin Zeng argues in Science and Engineering Ethics that neglecting required checks can make people morally responsible for autonomous-vehicle harms (PhilPapers record); Da Pelo et al. challenge CENTAUR's claims to explain human cognition in LAPITHS; Justin Weinberg examines norms for AI writing assistance on Daily Nous through Kyle van Oosterum's delegation proposal; Cordelia Berz argues in Topoi that three standard logics fail her requirements for explaining which changes would alter algorithmic decisions; Florian Richter, covered October 24, argues that AI moral advisers require a defensible account of moral competence; Saad and Mogensen survey AI consciousness, mental states and identity in Philosophy Compass; Basl and Behrends propose coordinated AI ethics institutions modeled on healthcare in Communications of the ACM.

ChinaTalk traces US training-environment sales to Chinese labs

Aqib Zakaria and Quinn Ennis report vendor links with varying evidence, explain the value of tested RL environments, and propose targeted controls while opposing broad restrictions.

American vendors are selling Chinese AI labs the practice tasks and grading systems used to improve models after their initial training, Aqib Zakaria and Quinn Ennis report in ChinaTalk’s November 11 investigation, “Piggybacking to the Frontier”. They identify nine US-linked vendors with reported or inferred Chinese customers. Their most specific finding concerns Mercor: sales data they accessed shows software-engineering reinforcement-learning environments sold to Alibaba. Across the nine, however, the evidence often establishes a customer relationship without identifying which training products changed hands.

Zakaria and Ennis explain the product through a PowerPoint example. A vendor builds a clone of the application, then hires consultants or designers to write criteria for grading slide decks. A model repeatedly attempts tasks inside the clone and learns from rewards assigned to its work. Coding environments can instead start with purchased company repositories containing bugs and the fixes engineers applied. The developer presents the buggy code to a model and checks whether it can repair it. Vendors sell both the practice setting and the expertise needed to choose useful tasks and judge success.

The authors argue that Chinese buyers save experimentation by purchasing environments already used by American labs. Broken application clones, misleading grading rules and poorly chosen tasks can waste expensive training runs. Buying a tested product helps avoid those failures; vendors can also reuse what they learned when building a similar product for another customer. ChinaTalk cites Epoch AI’s account of two founders who put the price of exclusivity at roughly four to five times that of a non-exclusive deal. Even exclusivity, Zakaria and Ennis argue, cannot prevent all transfer of practical know-how.

They distinguish this process from distillation, in which a smaller or weaker model learns from a stronger model’s outputs. A lab might collect good slide decks from a stronger model and train its own model on those examples; an RL environment lets the model practise making decks and receive feedback on its attempts. The authors consider access to environments particularly valuable for the final improvements made during post-training. They also acknowledge conflicting evidence about the relative contribution of supervised fine-tuning and RL across model releases. Their argument establishes a route for transferring useful development work, without quantifying the capability gain from any particular sale.

ChinaTalk’s vendor evidence ranges from the Mercor sales data to contractor disclosures and anonymous accounts. One anonymous Chinese-lab source told the authors it bought from both Mercor and Surge. For Surge’s other customers, the investigation draws on earlier reporting: SemiAnalysis stated its belief that Surge sells to Moonshot and Z.ai, while Forbes reported a Tencent procurement executive’s account of reliance on Surge. ChinaTalk adds evidence it describes as possibly indicating Tencent. Turing’s links to Tencent and Alibaba come from contractor posts and other evidence the authors describe; the AfterQuery finding relies on Forbes, with no independently verified sale in ChinaTalk’s investigation. Pareto’s ByteDance relationship concerned human data collection, illustrating the wider range of products covered by the vendor count.

The authors say subcontractors disclosed client names on LinkedIn and in resumes. They withhold most underlying sources because disclosure might prompt their removal, offering to show them to government officials. Their findings therefore remain attributed reporting; nine named vendors does not mean nine demonstrated transfers of sensitive RL environments. The scale estimate also concerns a broader trade. In Anna Tong’s August Forbes investigation, which we covered in August, two data-labeling entrepreneurs relayed estimates from Tencent and ByteDance executives that the top six Chinese labs spend about $500 million annually with American data-labeling companies. Neither report measures how much of that spending buys RL environments or products relevant to cyberattacks or biological weapons. Mercor declined to comment to Forbes; Surge and AfterQuery said they do not disclose customers.

Zakaria and Ennis describe commercial arrangements that make the trade difficult to track. Mercor’s April 30 customer policy reserved the right to refuse entities majority owned from unsupported countries; by May 15, its wording excluded customers located outside supported countries. China and Hong Kong were absent from the lists, while Singapore was included. The authors report that ByteDance’s data-buying desks operate in Singapore and San Jose, and that contractors working under client code names may not know the ultimate buyer.

The authors warn that coding and biology environments could improve exploit-finding or biological-weapons capabilities. They want the Bureau of Industry and Security to consider relevant environments under existing controls concerning chip design, cyberattack capabilities and biological weapons. They propose individual notices telling vendors when military end users or biological-weapons end uses require licenses. They also want the Pentagon to use federal and defense acquisition rules to prevent its contractors from selling to Chinese labs of concern. These are their proposed applications of regulatory and procurement powers. Above all, they argue for collecting better information about sales, potentially by announcing possible restrictions and seeking industry disclosures before deciding how far to intervene.

Zakaria and Ennis pair those proposals with objections to broad restrictions. Buying environments validated by American labs requires waiting for those labs to develop and test them, so they see the practice as helping followers close a gap. Training on Western applications may transfer poorly to Chinese equivalents. Environments are domain-specific: ordinary coding practice does not automatically improve offensive cyber capabilities. The authors judge dangerous environments a small minority of the trade and say American labs increasingly build sensitive products internally, while environments using a client’s proprietary data cannot be freely resold. They favor continued international competition in benign tasks and warn that denying Chinese labs access to environments for safety and alignment training could increase the risk of AI incidents.

The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement

Tom Cunningham, Lukas Althoff, Basil Halperin, Brian Jabarian, Andrew Koh, Arjun Ramani, Phil Trammell, Parker Whitfill, Cheryl Wu

We model the economics of recursive self-improvement (RSI) and assess its plausibility and impacts. First, we build a sequence of increasingly rich models of AI progress to highlight the feedback loops behind RSI. We represent our models as directed graphs and show that net acceleration in AI capabilities depends on the product of elasticities across each feedback loop. Second, we distinguish between "narrow" and "broad" AI capabilities, capturing the possibility that AI systems improve narrowly at optimizing AI R&D benchmarks without improving at broader economically valuable tasks. Third, we document existing estimates of key parameters and provide a wish list of empirical objects that AI companies can measure and feasibly share publicly. Finally, we calibrate the model with existing data. A back-of-the-envelope calculation suggests that feedback loops are not currently strong enough to generate a self-sustaining acceleration, though they appear to be strengthening. We conclude by assessing the plausibility and implications of such an acceleration.

Extended generalization and improved safety with the System‐2 features of the Scientist AI

Yoshua Bengio

Neural networks usually answer queries with a single forward pass—an analog of Kahneman's System 1. In unfamiliar or high‐stakes regimes, this can be brittle and overconfident. The Scientist AI framework augments a fast Predictor trained to approximate the Bayesian posterior with System 2 inference: structured probabilistic reasoning over intermediate natural‐language statements and causal hypotheses. The units that are generated and related via probabilistic dependencies are not the words or tokens but these Boolean statements about arbitrary properties of the world. Because inference‐time compute is spent only on the query at hand, marginalizing over latent statements can reduce computational uncertainty, produce better calibrated conditional probabilities and confidence intervals, and generate explanations as explicit argument graphs. With data transformed into these statements processed to make a syntactic difference between communication acts and factual hypotheses and the Predictor trained to be consequence‐invariant —insensitive to downstream effects of its outputs (Fornasiere et al., 2026)—this epistemic caution supports a safety case: the system is not imitating humans, has less incentive to acquire implicit goals, and is better suited as a guardrail for untrusted agents.

Safety in numbers: Tackling the deliberative, performative, and infrastructural roots of multi‐agent misalignment

Jeffrey M. Bradshaw, Matthew Johnson, Niranjan Suri, Alessandro Amato, K. Brent Venable, Rebecca Montanari, Nicolò Romandini, Roberto Fronteddu, Larry Bunch, James Lott, William de Beaumont, Andrzej Uszok, Sara Cavicchi, Mica Vignati, Ben Shinnick, Brian Jalaian, Jane Pinelis, Michael A. Goodrich, Jurriaan van Diggelen, David D. Woods, Patrick J. Hayes, Margaret Warren, Manuele Tele Junior Fernandez

Multi‐agent systems (MAS)—a uniquely important subset of the wider field commonly called “Agentic AI”—enable complex work processes involving distributed networks of multiple AIs, people, and technology tools. However, they also carry big risks. Root causes of catastrophic safety failures in multi‐agent collaboration (as in human collaboration) can be classed into three overlapping categories: 1. Measuring Definition Sensitivity in LLM Annotation of Social Science Concepts

Bogdan Mamaev, Qizhou Wang, Christopher Leckie, Matteo Vergani

In social science research, text annotation is a fundamental task that is traditionally a costly, hard-to-scale manual process. Researchers have used large language models (LLMs) to perform annotation by writing a definition of a concept into a prompt - a rule that determines whether a label should be attributed to a text depending on its content. However, concepts such as hate speech, fake news, and terrorism typically have several theoretically defensible competing definitions. It is also unknown whether the model actually applied the definition to provide the label or the label was based on the model's pretrained notion of the concept. To test this, we have the model annotate the same texts twice, each time with a different definition. We call the disagreement between the two sets of annotations definition sensitivity. As a control, we paraphrase one definition but leave its requirements unchanged - the disagreement between the annotations with the two wordings becomes the paraphrase floor. Across six concepts and three LLMs, a swap of competing definitions always changes more annotations, on average, than a paraphrase. This shows that the annotations depend on the prompted definition, so any set of annotations should be interpreted together with the definition that produced it. The LLM annotator's definition sensitivity and its paraphrase floor should both be reported in such work.

Autonomous Vehicles, Moral Responsibility Gap, and the Ethics of Knowing Responsibly

Weimin Zeng

“I could not have known” is a characteristic defense against moral blame when a driverless vehicle, such as an Apollo Go robotaxi, causes harm and no human made the decision at issue. This article argues that the defense holds only where the ignorance survived the inquiry that the actor’s role and situation required. Existing responses address a narrower problem: meaningful human control secures the control condition of moral responsibility, and explainable AI makes a particular output intelligible after the fact. Neither supplies a standard for how actors should investigate, weigh, and communicate evidence before deployment and during operation. Drawing on Lorraine Code’s responsibilist epistemology, the article develops such a standard. Responsible inquiry requires actors to test their evidence against what would count against it, and to organize what they learn so that it reaches the decisions it bears on. How much it demands varies with stakes, capacity, role, and subject matter. Ignorance produced or sustained by a breach of this standard constitutes epistemic negligence. Such negligence supports moral blame when it informs a decision that exposes others to unjustified risk or compromises justified reliance. Individuals answer for inquiry and communication, organizations for integrating distributed knowledge, institutions for the evidential and corrective structures within which both operate. The Apollo Go incidents supply the article’s principal test case.

Exploring the Logic of Counterfactuals for Explaining Algorithmic Decisions

Cordelia Berz

Interpretable machine learning (ML) is crucial for gaining insight into complex ML models. Among its most valuable tools are counterfactual explanations, identifying changes to input features that would alter an ML prediction. However, the reliability and usefulness of these explanations depend fundamentally on their logical foundation — a critical aspect that has largely been overlooked in the ML literature. This paper addresses this gap by establishing normative desiderata for a counterfactual logic specifically designed for explaining algorithmic decisions. We analyse and argue for the validity of logical properties such as simplification of disjunctive antecedents and modus ponens , while demonstrating the unsuitability of others such as antecedent strengthening and conjunction conditionalisation . Our findings reveal that none of these standard frameworks fully satisfy the requirements, highlighting the urgent need for alternative frameworks.
