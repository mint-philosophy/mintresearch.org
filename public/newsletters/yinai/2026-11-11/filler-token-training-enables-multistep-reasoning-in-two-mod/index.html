Models learn to reason through strings of dots

Schulz and tvb trained two models to solve multistep tasks with dots in place of written reasoning. Their method teaches the intermediate answers explicitly, then keeps those answers out of the text the models read.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 November 2026 935 words · ~5 min

Language models can be trained to solve multistep problems while the text between question and answer consists entirely of dots. Wuschel Schulz and a coauthor writing as tvb report this in “LLMs can learn (some) multi-step reasoning on filler tokens”, a November 11 LessWrong research update from MARS 5 and the Meridian Visiting Researcher Programme. Their specially trained models improved on three of seven synthetic task families compared with training on final answers alone. One model learned to track changes to three lamps through 60 moves. The experiment shows how useful computation can proceed without readable intermediate text under an engineered training procedure.

Schulz and tvb fine-tuned Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and gpt-oss-20b using small trainable additions to the models, called adapters. At each dot position, they trained the model to predict the corresponding intermediate state of a worked solution. The next input remained a dot: the predicted state was never written into the text for the model to read back. Information could still travel between positions through the network’s internal representations. During evaluation, the researchers supplied the dots in advance and read predictions from the model. Left to generate its own text, the trained model would write out its reasoning. This differs from ordinary next-token training, and the result does not establish that deployed assistants spontaneously reason this way.

The researchers compared their procedure with a precedent from Jacob Pfau, William Merrill and Samuel Bowman at New York University. Their 2024 arXiv paper, “Let’s Think Dot by Dot: Hidden Computation in Transformer Language Models”, showed that models could use meaningless extra tokens to solve algorithmic problems they struggled with when answering immediately. Its training mixed worked solutions with examples containing dots followed by an answer. Schulz and tvb instead supervised the intermediate prediction at every dot. When they compared the two approaches on the same progressively lengthening tasks, with repeated training runs, their method outperformed answer-only training; the older recipe did not.

The authors’ results table indicates that the three clear successes were running sums with remainders, tracking a ball under shuffled cups, and tracking the lamps. In the lamp task, each move either passes the lamps’ states around a ring or toggles one lamp depending on another. Qwen answered 79% of the 60-move test problems correctly; its answer-only counterpart passed no curriculum stage. The researchers advanced training after success on a small checkpoint test, so passing a stage did not mean perfect accuracy on the larger final test. Across the other tasks, gains were small or absent. Models using dots struggled with chained arithmetic, lookup tables and logical deductions, while models trained to write their reasoning reached the longest tested length in every task family.

Pfau and colleagues also explained a theoretical limit: adding filler positions does not give a fixed-depth transformer unlimited capacity for successive computation. Schulz and tvb accept that limit. They investigate how much of a finite sequence of operations can fit within the model’s existing layers. A task described as 60 successive moves need not require 60 network layers, because an algorithm can combine some operations. Their result therefore concerns the practical reach of computation at finite task lengths; it does not show that dots remove the architectural limit.

To explore that reach, Schulz and tvb used Opus 5.5 agents to construct algorithms within an executable approximation of each transformer. They checked that the algorithms used permitted operations and returned the correct intermediate states and answers, then searched for constructions requiring fewer layers. The shallowest algorithms they found grew deeper as tasks lengthened, but usually much more slowly than the number of task steps. These constructions establish upper bounds on sufficient depth. They neither prove the minimum depth required nor show which algorithms the trained models learned. The numerical curves also depend on a chosen assumption that one internal processing operation can act as a lookup table over at most 32 bits of input.

The experiments show that fitting an algorithm into the available layers does not guarantee that training will discover it. Chained arithmetic and function composition had comparatively shallow constructions but stalled after only a few steps in practice. Schulz and tvb suggest that models have difficulty moving larger amounts of information, such as lookup tables or logical premises, between token positions. Their own research on steganographic reasoning motivated the study: when a model answers correctly after producing apparently unrelated text, success alone cannot establish that the words secretly encode the calculation. The model might be using those positions for computation without relying on their textual content.

For oversight, the authors worry that pressure to avoid verbalizing parts of a calculation could eventually teach similar behavior. That possibility is an extrapolation from their specially supervised training runs. They did not test a monitor, train against one, or investigate whether the internal computation could be decoded. Identical dots contain no intermediate reasoning for a text reader to inspect, but the network’s internal states provide a separate source of evidence.

Kaley Brauer, Claudio Mayrink Verdun and Samuel Marks examined that possibility in the separate arXiv paper “Reading Between the Dots: Decoding Hidden Computation across Filler Tokens.” On tasks with identifiable intermediate values, they recovered those values from the hidden states of DeepSeek V3 and Kimi K2 with 82–94% accuracy using their best decoding setup. Their result does not establish that Schulz and tvb’s trained models are equally readable. It shows why losing the written reasoning and losing access to the computation are different questions. Schulz and tvb’s immediate recommendation concerns experiment design: researchers studying concealed reasoning should first check whether their chosen tasks can be solved using filler positions alone.
