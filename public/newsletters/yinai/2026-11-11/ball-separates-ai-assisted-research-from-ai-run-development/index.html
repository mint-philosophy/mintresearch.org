Dean Ball separates AI-assisted research from AI-run development

Ball argues that METR's Anthropic findings show AI speeding up human-directed research, with little sign of AI planning its own successors, and that a ban on recursive self-improvement should not reach the former.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 November 2026 762 words · ~4 min

Dean Ball argues in “Iron Makes Iron,” his November 10 Hyperdimensional essay, that the alarm about recursive self-improvement merges two different processes. He calls the first weak recursion: a technology serving as a major input to better versions of itself, as iron tools made more iron and railways carried the materials for more track. He calls the second strong recursion: a technology autonomously planning and carrying out the development of its successor, which he says has never happened. Ball, who now works at OpenAI and writes in a personal capacity, holds that handing practical control of AI development to AI would be a serious mistake and supports laws against it. He objects to treating evidence of weak recursion as evidence of the strong kind.

He was responding to METR’s November 5 assessment, which found Anthropic improving its models on well-specified tasks at about twice its earlier rate and attributed most of the increase to AI helping human researchers (covered November 5). Days earlier, Anthropic had disclosed that it may soon meet its Responsible Scaling Policy threshold for dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI (covered November 2). Ball asks who specifies the tasks. If human researchers set them, following goals chosen by managers, the measured acceleration is weak recursion on a large scale, and he finds almost no sign of strong recursion in METR’s report or in the labs’ published research-acceleration figures. METR ruled out growth in training compute as the cause; Ball points to inference compute, which runs the engineering, experiments, synthetic data and analysis that make up most AI research and has grown enormously since METR’s baseline period of March 2024 to October 2025.

Ball does not call weak recursion harmless. It could, he writes, bring faster capability gains over the coming year than anything seen so far, and raise risks in cybersecurity, biology and chemistry. He supports requiring the largest companies to place third-party evaluators inside their development teams, disclose incidents of misalignment or loss of control even when no harm followed, maintain strong security and submit to audits of their safety practices. He opposes banning the use of AI to build AI, which Kelsey Piper proposed in The Argument on October 21. The same capabilities that raise these risks, he argues, also promise new materials and medicine; vulnerabilities that AI finds in software already existed; and every lab’s safety plan relies on AI for monitoring, security and alignment research. A ban that covered weak recursion would, on his account, ban most frontier development in practice.

On strong recursion, Ball locates the difficulty in research taste: noticing problems, turning vague problems into answerable questions, and deciding how to divide scarce compute among research directions, decisions now made by company leadership. Coding ability was predictable from early systems onward, he writes, while progress in taste has been slow, and long-range judgment is hard even to evaluate. He cites METR’s statement that AI cannot yet make highly significant, fully autonomous contributions to the most important parts of model training, and treats Anthropic employees’ reported “signs of life” as too heavily redacted to assess. He grants that taste may emerge from further scaling, as competent computer use did this year, and supports measurement bodies such as METR for that reason.

Because strong recursion concerns how a firm allocates capital and who makes its decisions, Ball argues that it can be judged only at the level of the company, and that evaluators should study companies as well as models. He proposes disclosure of how compute allocation decisions are made and of the share of merged code changes with no substantive human review, figures that could later support a statutory definition of strong recursion. Directors’ duties to shareholders, negligence liability, consumer protection law and executive powers already discourage any handover of control to AI; Ball calls these deterrents improvised and liable to be turned against ordinary uses, and prefers clear rules written for the purpose.

He ends on calibration. As AI-safety voices gain public influence, he argues, alarmed posting can prompt hasty laws that delay benefits, weaken the United States against rivals and concentrate control of AI in the state, a risk he compares to the history of nuclear power. The essay follows the revised forecasts for superhuman AI researchers from the AI Futures Project (covered November 10). On November 11, Kevin Bankston endorsed Ball’s point that strong recursion would require letting models run the company, and suggested that Neil Chilson’s view, that a properly defined ban would cost little innovation, rests on the same distinction.
