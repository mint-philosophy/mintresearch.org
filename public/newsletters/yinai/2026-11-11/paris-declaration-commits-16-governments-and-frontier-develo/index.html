Paris Call sets out evaluation, shutdown and whistleblower commitments

The November 9 declaration extends developers’ shutdown commitments to training and internal use, promises public evaluation findings, and assigns whistleblower channels to governments. Its voluntary pledges depend on national implementation.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 November 2026 911 words · ~5 min

The Paris Call for AI safety and security, published by the Élysée on November 9, commits governments, researchers and developers to shared evaluation methods, public findings and earlier warning of serious AI incidents. Developers separately promise shutdown mechanisms that reach into training and internal use; governments undertake to provide protected reporting channels for whistleblowers. We covered Adam Gleave’s response yesterday. The declaration’s detailed provisions explain how its signatories propose to make advanced AI safer, and which decisions they leave to national authorities.

The Élysée presents frontier AI risks as inherently international: development, deployment and harms cross borders, so no government or company can manage them alone. Its signatories include France, Canada and other governments alongside developers such as Anthropic, OpenAI and Mistral AI. Civil-society organizations have also endorsed it, and endorsements remain open. The declaration is a voluntary political commitment. It creates no international enforcement authority or sanctions, and specifies no implementation deadlines.

Under the first commitment, policymakers, researchers and developers will build a common framework for evaluating and managing frontier-model risks, using existing frameworks as a starting point. The named dangers include cyberattacks and chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear misuse, alongside loss of control as models become more autonomous or act contrary to human intentions. Signatories want technical standards and testing methods that keep pace with those capabilities. They identify the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science, or NAAIMES, as one existing network that could help make results comparable and accessible across countries.

The signatories also agree to publish non-sensitive evaluation findings in a standard format and create a shared public repository. Possible contents include methods for adversarial testing, benchmark tools, risk assessments and stress-test results. Publishing the methods alongside findings would help readers compare assessments that otherwise use different tests. The text does not assign an organization to build the repository or set a date for publication, and the commitment concerns non-sensitive findings, leaving room to withhold information whose disclosure would create risks.

The second commitment puts national authorities in charge of the safety standards that signatories pledge to follow throughout a model’s life. Those standards may be established or recognized by the relevant authority; oversight should include competent, independent third-party evaluation. Policymakers acknowledge that legal frameworks need to clarify responsibility for large-scale damage, including cross-border harm, both to protect those affected and to give developers legal certainty. These provisions describe work for national governments and regulators, with legal duties depending on implementation in their jurisdictions.

Developers undertake to disclose capabilities, limitations and known risks. Their support for qualified independent evaluation is expressly conditional: “where appropriate and proportionate to the risk”, and consistent with existing practices and arrangements. The text does not guarantee unrestricted access to a model’s internals or specify how much time evaluators receive. Its general endorsement of independent testing therefore leaves substantive decisions about access and testing conditions unresolved.

Developers also commit to fail-safe mechanisms for stopping model operations they control, expressly covering training and internal use. The examples range from restrictions and pauses to shutdowns. That reach includes a developer’s own use of a model before making it available to customers. Developers further recognize the need for continuous risk management, monitoring of emerging capabilities and internal adversarial testing, and commit to work on governance with defined responsibilities, sufficient resources and independent oversight. On open weights, they acknowledge the danger that safeguards can be removed or bypassed while affirming the benefits for research, innovation and safety.

The declaration names the 2024 Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments among its predecessors. Seoul’s company signatories undertook to define thresholds of intolerable risk and, in the extreme, refrain from developing or deploying systems when mitigations could not keep risks below those thresholds. Paris specifies shutdown mechanisms but does not define equivalent thresholds or a rule that triggers their use. The two texts address related decisions: maintaining the ability to stop operations, and deciding when development or deployment must stop.

Under the third commitment, policymakers, researchers and developers pledge to cooperate on information sharing, reporting and early warnings. They propose building rapid alerts on existing incident-response arrangements, including cybersecurity systems. The text recognizes the value of aligning incident categories and exchanging timely information about serious cyber and AI-control incidents, as well as warning signs of serious harm. Exchanges could take place between companies and governments, among governments or among companies. A further cooperation mechanism remains something to explore; the declaration specifies no reporting deadlines or central incident authority.

Governments take on the explicit whistleblower-channel commitment. Policymakers promise secure, confidential routes through which employees and other stakeholders can raise reasonably grounded concerns about systemic risks without legal or professional retaliation. Developers separately recognize that internal reporting arrangements should protect researchers. Confidential channels and protection from retaliation are therefore stated objectives, with governments responsible for supplying the channels and national implementation determining how those protections work.

Gleave’s response identifies practical conditions for effective evaluation: he says FAR.AI commonly receives less than a week to test a model and wants access to inspect internals and conduct further training. The AI Evaluator Forum’s AEF-1 standard says substantially novel systems often require at least 20 business days, with time proportionate to the confidence needed and the system’s novelty. Yoshua Bengio welcomed the meeting while calling for mandatory audits by evaluators developers do not choose, and demonstrations of safety before a model can pose grave danger, including before deployment. Both responses identify requirements that the Paris declaration leaves for further decisions.
