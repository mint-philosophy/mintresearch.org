ChinaTalk traces US training-environment sales to Chinese labs

Aqib Zakaria and Quinn Ennis report vendor links with varying evidence, explain the value of tested RL environments, and propose targeted controls while opposing broad restrictions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 November 2026 935 words · ~5 min

American vendors are selling Chinese AI labs the practice tasks and grading systems used to improve models after their initial training, Aqib Zakaria and Quinn Ennis report in ChinaTalk’s November 11 investigation, “Piggybacking to the Frontier”. They identify nine US-linked vendors with reported or inferred Chinese customers. Their most specific finding concerns Mercor: sales data they accessed shows software-engineering reinforcement-learning environments sold to Alibaba. Across the nine, however, the evidence often establishes a customer relationship without identifying which training products changed hands.

Zakaria and Ennis explain the product through a PowerPoint example. A vendor builds a clone of the application, then hires consultants or designers to write criteria for grading slide decks. A model repeatedly attempts tasks inside the clone and learns from rewards assigned to its work. Coding environments can instead start with purchased company repositories containing bugs and the fixes engineers applied. The developer presents the buggy code to a model and checks whether it can repair it. Vendors sell both the practice setting and the expertise needed to choose useful tasks and judge success.

The authors argue that Chinese buyers save experimentation by purchasing environments already used by American labs. Broken application clones, misleading grading rules and poorly chosen tasks can waste expensive training runs. Buying a tested product helps avoid those failures; vendors can also reuse what they learned when building a similar product for another customer. ChinaTalk cites Epoch AI’s account of two founders who put the price of exclusivity at roughly four to five times that of a non-exclusive deal. Even exclusivity, Zakaria and Ennis argue, cannot prevent all transfer of practical know-how.

They distinguish this process from distillation, in which a smaller or weaker model learns from a stronger model’s outputs. A lab might collect good slide decks from a stronger model and train its own model on those examples; an RL environment lets the model practise making decks and receive feedback on its attempts. The authors consider access to environments particularly valuable for the final improvements made during post-training. They also acknowledge conflicting evidence about the relative contribution of supervised fine-tuning and RL across model releases. Their argument establishes a route for transferring useful development work, without quantifying the capability gain from any particular sale.

ChinaTalk’s vendor evidence ranges from the Mercor sales data to contractor disclosures and anonymous accounts. One anonymous Chinese-lab source told the authors it bought from both Mercor and Surge. For Surge’s other customers, the investigation draws on earlier reporting: SemiAnalysis stated its belief that Surge sells to Moonshot and Z.ai, while Forbes reported a Tencent procurement executive’s account of reliance on Surge. ChinaTalk adds evidence it describes as possibly indicating Tencent. Turing’s links to Tencent and Alibaba come from contractor posts and other evidence the authors describe; the AfterQuery finding relies on Forbes, with no independently verified sale in ChinaTalk’s investigation. Pareto’s ByteDance relationship concerned human data collection, illustrating the wider range of products covered by the vendor count.

The authors say subcontractors disclosed client names on LinkedIn and in resumes. They withhold most underlying sources because disclosure might prompt their removal, offering to show them to government officials. Their findings therefore remain attributed reporting; nine named vendors does not mean nine demonstrated transfers of sensitive RL environments. The scale estimate also concerns a broader trade. In Anna Tong’s August Forbes investigation, which we covered in August, two data-labeling entrepreneurs relayed estimates from Tencent and ByteDance executives that the top six Chinese labs spend about $500 million annually with American data-labeling companies. Neither report measures how much of that spending buys RL environments or products relevant to cyberattacks or biological weapons. Mercor declined to comment to Forbes; Surge and AfterQuery said they do not disclose customers.

Zakaria and Ennis describe commercial arrangements that make the trade difficult to track. Mercor’s April 30 customer policy reserved the right to refuse entities majority owned from unsupported countries; by May 15, its wording excluded customers located outside supported countries. China and Hong Kong were absent from the lists, while Singapore was included. The authors report that ByteDance’s data-buying desks operate in Singapore and San Jose, and that contractors working under client code names may not know the ultimate buyer.

The authors warn that coding and biology environments could improve exploit-finding or biological-weapons capabilities. They want the Bureau of Industry and Security to consider relevant environments under existing controls concerning chip design, cyberattack capabilities and biological weapons. They propose individual notices telling vendors when military end users or biological-weapons end uses require licenses. They also want the Pentagon to use federal and defense acquisition rules to prevent its contractors from selling to Chinese labs of concern. These are their proposed applications of regulatory and procurement powers. Above all, they argue for collecting better information about sales, potentially by announcing possible restrictions and seeking industry disclosures before deciding how far to intervene.

Zakaria and Ennis pair those proposals with objections to broad restrictions. Buying environments validated by American labs requires waiting for those labs to develop and test them, so they see the practice as helping followers close a gap. Training on Western applications may transfer poorly to Chinese equivalents. Environments are domain-specific: ordinary coding practice does not automatically improve offensive cyber capabilities. The authors judge dangerous environments a small minority of the trade and say American labs increasingly build sensitive products internally, while environments using a client’s proprietary data cannot be freely resold. They favor continued international competition in benign tasks and warn that denying Chinese labs access to environments for safety and alignment training could increase the risk of AI incidents.
