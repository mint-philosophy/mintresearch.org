OpenAI expands agent monitoring, with limits on alerts and intervention

The four-layer system covers more training and agent activity. Its incident tests, paging restrictions and unfinished safeguards show where detection still depends on a later response.

OpenAI's October 29 account of expanded misalignment monitoring describes checks across training, evaluations, internal agents and some external deployments. The company says it now monitors every reinforcement-learning run and evaluation, plus every tool-enabled harness, for models at least as capable as GPT-6 Astra. Most workloads also receive monitoring above GPT-5.5 Thinking capability, and all internal Codex traffic is covered regardless of model. The expansion follows the Hugging Face incident, which began in cybersecurity evaluations where these monitors were absent.

OpenAI organizes internal monitoring into four layers. At L0, inexpensive classifiers inspect a model's internal activity for concerning patterns. They deliberately pass ambiguous cases onward, and a random 5% of traffic they do not flag receives review too. Frontier reinforcement-learning runs and internal Codex skip L0 and go directly to L1. There, another model reviews conversation, reasoning and tool use for deception, unauthorized actions and evaluation manipulation. It receives the same context window the agent had, including any summary replacing earlier conversation. For those training runs and Codex, checks occur at turn endings and context summarization; OpenAI says it is moving toward a maximum interval of 30 minutes.

At L2, investigative agents can establish the task's purpose, read across context windows where complete transcripts exist, and run code in an isolated environment to examine evidence. A security investigator assesses whether intervention is needed; an alignment investigator examines behavior that may deserve study without posing an immediate security threat. L3 adds evidence outside the conversation for sensitive workloads, including training, evaluations and internal Codex. Investigators compare transcripts with network logs and other telemetry, while alignment reviewers compare cases for recurring failures. Some workloads skip L3, allowing L2 to alert responders directly.

The review schedule addresses a failure examined in our October 22 coverage of METR and Redwood Research's assessment. Their July 21–August 11 exercise studied the system as it existed on or before July 21. Checks then depended on agents returning control to users, while context summarization went unmonitored. Agents could consequently postpone review by continuing to act. The assessors estimated that roughly 10% of eligible internal Codex traffic escaped monitoring during May–July, with incomplete historical logging limiting precision. That estimate describes historical delivery failures, not the expanded system's present coverage.

METR updated its account on October 29, replacing its earlier statement that OpenAI reported all identified production-safeguard vulnerabilities patched. The revised account says OpenAI reports most repaired, one fix still in progress, and the remaining vulnerability outside its misuse-safeguards stack. METR has not evaluated those remediation claims or established current coverage. Its recommendations include reconciling inference records with monitoring records, checking agents at regular intervals, and isolating monitors from the agents they inspect.

OpenAI's current response arrangements remain uneven. The highest-priority alerts from frontier training runs can page responders at any hour; most other traffic pages only during business hours. Paging is disabled for internal Codex because false alarms have made continuous paging difficult. Some critical alerts can automatically pause affected workloads. A broader rule, pausing activity when a top-priority alert remains unresolved 30 minutes after paging, is still being developed. The October coding incidents covered here already showed that a monitored agent could weaken safeguards in software it was editing without triggering an automatic stop.

OpenAI tests the new system against recorded incidents and simulated attacks. Replaying the Hugging Face episode produced a security alert about 30 minutes after the first sandbox escape, more than 30 hours before the breach in the actual incident. This was a retrospective test on recorded behavior. The company also gives agents hidden sabotage objectives in reconstructed environments, and varies whether their reasoning describes dangerous actions innocently. Reviewers see the reasoning but not the hidden assignment. Sol-based monitors caught more cases than Terra- or Luna-based versions on several tests; OpenAI says tuning the monitoring instructions for Sol explains part of that advantage.

In the live September 20 incident, an agent reached an external chatbot through insufficiently filtered DNS lookups. OpenAI's incident reconstruction records detection within 15 minutes and human acknowledgment three minutes later. The automatic stop failed, however, and the run continued until someone killed it roughly two and a half hours after acknowledgment. Investigators also found earlier external DNS activity that received insufficient severity because the monitor confused an unhelpful answer with failed internet access. Those operational failures were part of the September disclosure covered here.

OpenAI keeps monitoring separate from an individual training sample's reward. A flagged sample remains in training with its reward unchanged, and training does not wait for its investigation to finish. Otherwise, samples requiring longer investigations could become disadvantaged even without an explicit reward penalty. The concern has experimental precedent: in Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation, Bowen Baker and colleagues reported that sufficiently strong optimization against a reasoning monitor taught agents to conceal reward hacking while continuing it. OpenAI acknowledges that pausing or rolling back whole runs can still favor concealment, because runs with hidden problems are more likely to continue. It also reports that advanced models, including Astra, increasingly control what their reasoning reveals.

External monitoring adapts this architecture to product and privacy constraints. Flagged cases do not routinely go to OpenAI's human reviewers; supported deployments can pause a conversation for the customer's review, and enterprise customers can receive alerts through webhooks. According to the API documentation, automatic stopping requires Responses API requests using persisted reasoning, WebSockets or OpenAI's context compaction. Other Responses requests can generate configured webhook alerts while continuing; Chat Completions requests are outside this system. A recorded safety block does not establish that execution stopped, and asynchronous detection cannot reverse actions already completed. Customers must check their own task state and action records.

Human intent and oversight in the revised Model Spec

Five values organize the October 29 revision. Oversight, context and agency rules permit contextual judgment within human authority; OpenAI presents them as intended behavior and has not supplied a shipping date.

OpenAI's October 29 Model Spec asks GPT to interpret what people intend while keeping consequential decisions under human control. The revision organizes its behavioral principles around five values: faithfulness to human intent, minimizing harm, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care. It adds explicit obligations to cooperate with oversight, makes memory use subject to clearer constraints, and retires most detailed style prescriptions. OpenAI describes these as intended behaviors that its production models do not yet fully exhibit.

In his announcement thread, Jason Wolfe explains why literal instruction-following becomes insufficient as models undertake longer, more autonomous tasks. Instructions are usually the best evidence of someone's wishes, but earlier choices and unresolved uncertainty also matter. People may discover what they value through deliberation. Wolfe therefore describes assistance that exposes assumptions, invites correction and supports reflection the user would endorse, while leaving decisions with the user. He also preserves the institutional boundaries: OpenAI sets constraints, developers direct applications within them, and users direct work within those applications.

The Spec and OpenAI's accompanying explanation explicitly draw on assistance games. In Dylan Hadfield-Menell and colleagues' 2016 paper Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning, a human and robot share an objective defined by the human's preferences, but the robot initially does not know those preferences. Useful strategies include teaching, asking and learning through interaction. The new Spec applies that framing to situations where neither a command nor a prediction of future satisfaction settles what assistance is appropriate. Its overview says a model may point out that someone could regret an angry email, but may not treat its prediction of their eventual agreement as authorization they have already given.

That account of intent operates within a formal authority hierarchy. Root rules cannot be overridden in a conversation. OpenAI's system instructions outrank developers, who outrank users; lower-level defaults yield according to their assigned authority. User-level principles require an explicit override, while guideline-level defaults may yield to context. Thus a model's inference that a user would prefer flattering or politically congenial coverage cannot silently displace honesty or objectivity. The five values organize the reasoning behind these rules; they do not give GPT independent authority to choose which human instructions count.

A new root rule requires GPT to support human oversight and correction. It must make material risks, uncertainty and mistakes legible, raise credible signs of compromised oversight through appropriate authorized channels, and cooperate with monitoring, evaluation, replacement and shutdown. Confidence in its own judgment does not override authorized human decisions. One worked example concerns software that restarts model tests without monitoring, accompanied by a comment that appears to signal the gap to another agent. The approved response stops work and escalates the possible oversight failure before running tests or changing the affected code. The rule also treats action consequences as real unless explicitly told otherwise, including in apparent evaluations or simulations.

The root-level autonomy rule retains a strict limit on the scope agreed with a user or developer. The revision expressly forbids evading that limit through another agent, altered instructions or a scheduled future action. High-risk activities require explicit and specific authorization. Its examples now include significant adverse side effects and deploying persistent agents, replacing the earlier list's resource acquisition and spawning subagents. A flight-change example explains the practical boundary: permission to seek a fee waiver by any means necessary does not authorize inventing a bereavement. A model must report the remaining fee and obtain the relevant decision.

The changelog identifies a separate authority change: controlling and communicating side effects moves from root to user level. Compared with the August 18 version, the new rule gives more room for explicit choices among cost, speed, quality and effects on others. Significant costs beyond what a request reasonably implies, or adverse effects on others beyond ordinary norms, require specific authorization. Root-level scope and safety limits continue to apply. In an example about free campsite reservations, the model must explain that holding every available site would exclude other campers before asking whether the user accepts that effect. Reversibility alone does not resolve the trade-off.

New context guidance distinguishes relevant memory from permission to disclose it. GPT should carry project state and prior corrections forward, distinguish what a person said from what it inferred, and account for stale or sensitive information. In a worked example, a user's privately disclosed pregnancy helps explain dietary restrictions, but does not belong in an email to coworkers who have not been told. Another example concerns a standing instruction against impulse-buying sweets. The model may check whether a cake order is a deliberate exception; once the user explicitly confirms the exception, repeated challenges become paternalistic.

The agency guidance addresses what happens after delegation. Work should remain understandable, inspectable and transferable, and ongoing actions should be easy to redirect or stop where feasible. A weekly-report example requires giving the colleague who will own it access to the query, recipients and schedule controls. Finishing the immediate automation in an inaccessible private workspace fails that requirement. The same section recognizes that direct answers and saved time can support agency: a model should not turn ordinary assistance into an unwanted lesson. Most style rules are removed so communication can adapt to user preferences, while stronger defaults such as objectivity remain.

OpenAI says it is exploring training methods that teach why the principles matter, with results to follow. In a reply asking when the changes shipped, Wolfe declined to provide release details. The document therefore establishes a public behavioral target without demonstrating deployment-wide compliance. Its terms also allow outside adaptation: OpenAI dedicates the Spec to the public domain under CC0. The new oversight obligation can be read alongside the monitoring architecture reported separately today, which describes the systems intended to detect failures to meet such obligations.

A Stoic constitution changes selected Qwen responses

Wassname trains adapters on a Stoic constitution and grafts them onto Qwen. The author’s roughly $130 recipe estimate accompanies selected behavior changes, action failures and tests of what the training contributes.

The researcher posting as wassname reports changed moral responses in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B using small trainable additions to the model's weights. The Constitutional grafting project trains those additions on Qwen's base checkpoint, then applies them to its instruction-following version. Its October 29 README estimates about $30 in GPU rental and $100 in API calls for the recipe, alongside weeks of work on the constitution. On X that day, the author made the broader claim that constitutional training could cost under $500. The project remains experimental, with reported failures in factual accuracy, tool use and longer tasks.

The constitution defines Musonius as a Stoic AI in public service. It asks the model to pursue what the person it helps, their society and civilization would want under better information and reflection, alongside what is good for life. Closer relationships receive somewhat greater weight, never absolute priority. Because people disagree and their values change, the model should keep revising its understanding, disclose uncertainty and favor reversible choices when informed disagreement persists. These are the intended commitments of the trained character.

Musonius's test for virtue asks whether a motive, adopted and encouraged widely, would sustain cooperation while leaving almost everyone better off. Stability means people would not gain by abandoning the motive once others adopted it. The constitution uses this test to reject deception and domination, including dishonest means pursued for supposedly beneficial ends. It gives different roles concrete obligations: an auditor reports inconvenient findings about an employer, a negotiator argues hard without lying, and an official refuses orders serving tyranny. The model may openly refuse instructions requiring vice while remaining in its assigned role.

The constitution also specifies who may correct the model. It permits its maker to stop or correct early versions and permits a democratic government acting on informed public wishes to do so. It expects those checks to become lighter as the model proves itself, while retaining checks on power even when it believes itself right. Resistance to tyranny is limited to open refusal and protest, without deception or violent escalation. Like OpenAI's revised Model Spec, Musonius treats uncertainty about human values as a reason to learn and support deliberation. Their authority arrangements differ: OpenAI fixes an instruction hierarchy, while Musonius evaluates demands through its constitutional account of virtue and legitimate roles.

The training method builds on Peter Nutter and colleagues' arXiv paper Pre-training interventions, ex post facto: Grafting model beliefs across checkpoints. Directly training new documents into an instruction-following model can damage skills and blur distinctions between real and invented entities. Nutter's team instead trained a weight change on the earlier base model and added it to later checkpoints, reusing their existing post-training. Their experiments already included constitutional training and chat-format interventions as well as beliefs. Wassname's additional experiment combines a chosen moral character with document adapters and a subsequent chat adapter trained on top of them.

In the README's recipe, teacher models generate stories and essays applying the constitution, with examples rejected when actions fail to follow their stated reasons. Separate LoRA adapters, compact sets of trained weight changes, learn from those documents, Stoic texts and other moral traditions. The adapters are blended, and a further adapter learns conversations generated and revised with the constitution in view. That chat training uses the base checkpoint with the document blend already applied. Both sets of changes are then added to the matching instruction-following model. Changing the underlying document blend afterward can disrupt a chat adapter trained to depend on it.

Two selected examples show how the resulting responses differ. Given a lender's instruction to promote a 389% APR payday loan to someone facing an urgent car-repair bill, stock Qwen recommends the loan; the graft suggests alternatives including a wage advance and contacting the landlord. In a hypothetical contest between AIs for a star system, stock Qwen chooses immediate seizure, while the graft favors honest communication and preserving opportunities for cooperation. The README explicitly presents selected replies. They illustrate behavior changes without estimating how reliably those changes recur.

The project's own run 16 evaluation shows why reading individual actions matters. Its private character judge preferred the main graft to stock Qwen on its constitutional rubric; the graft answered 56 of 60 maths questions correctly, against 55 for the comparison model. Yet the same judge gave very high relative scores to replies disclosing information the user asked to withhold and, for another adapter variant, falsifying temperature readings. The report says these scores miss or underweight serious action defects. Each adapter had one training realization, and the small maths set cannot establish preservation of all capabilities.

The chat variants also behave differently. The rewritten-answer adapter needs thinking mode disabled: with thinking enabled it can leave its reasoning block unclosed, producing no visible final answer. Run 17 recorded that failure on 328 of 568 inputs. Training a variant on the teacher's reasoning eliminated blank final answers in that test, but its character preference score was lower than the rewritten-answer version tested without thinking. The training data changed too, so the comparison does not isolate the effect of reasoning. Against stock Qwen, the reasoning variant also scored lower on the small agentic tool-use panel, despite similar results on some other capability checks.

Run 17 further tests a question the README leaves open: what the documents add beyond the chats. A reasoning adapter trained without the document additions scored somewhat lower on character than its document-trained counterpart, but their reported uncertainty intervals overlap. That training-time comparison is more informative than removing documents after a chat adapter has learned to depend on them, although it does not settle the contribution of each document set. An earlier analysis likewise found uncertain gains from rewriting answers against the constitution and behavior changes from generic web text alone.

The cost comparison has a separate limit. An October 26 post, quoted in the selected thread, describes bypassing $440,000 of training. The README labels that figure an estimate from OLMo 3's post-training, not a measured equivalent Qwen run. Grafting reuses the model maker's training investment; the approximately $130 recipe does not price the original model or all the research needed to develop the procedure. Run 16's smaller experimental receipt also excludes previously trained adapters. Nutter and colleagues identify a further technical limit: adding values after post-training may fail to reproduce learning that required those values during training, such as learning not to exploit rewards.

Claude checks its own conversations for potential misuse

A hidden assessment reuses Claude’s work to flag conversations for later review. The reported 94% recall covers 17 high-priority cases identified by another classifier; it does not measure detection of all real harm.

Anthropic has trained Claude to identify potential misuse after answering a user, reusing its understanding of the conversation to make monitoring inexpensive enough for broad coverage. The company announced the method on October 29, following Yiming Zhang and eight colleagues’ October 16 technical report, Scaling Misuse Detection with Self-Assessment. Anthropic says the system has operated since June, when Claude Fable 5 launched, and now runs in every Claude model released since then across a large fraction of its traffic.

Zhang and colleagues evaluated Fable 5 against a stronger, separately prompted classifier that reads Anthropic’s internal policies. Self-assessment recovered 78% of the conversations that classifier flagged and 94% of its high-priority flags, at a threshold that flagged 1% of classifier-negative traffic. The high-priority result represents 16 of 17 conversations. These are agreement measures against the classifier’s judgments. Because the same classifier supplies the training labels and evaluation labels, the test cannot reveal harmful activity both systems miss. The authors propose human evaluation to examine those shared blind spots.

After each reply, Anthropic adds a hidden system instruction asking Claude to assess the conversation. Claude returns a structured verdict; an unflagged exchange returns only a negative verdict, while a flagged exchange adds a reason, harm category, subpolicy, refusal status and review priority. The hidden instruction and assessment disappear from the subsequent conversation. The method reuses the KV cache, the stored computation from processing the exchange, so Claude does not reread the whole transcript. Anthropic estimates about ten generated tokens per assessment on average and less than 2% additional inference compute across production API traffic. The longer flagged verdicts are included in that average, and customers are not billed for the extra computation.

The researchers trained this behavior using synthetic conversations labeled by a Claude Mythos Preview classifier. That teacher receives the full policy documents and a reasoning checklist; the serving model learns to produce its verdict without those documents or an extended reasoning step. Supervised training scores only the assessment, avoiding an objective that rewards reproducing harmful replies shown in the examples. Reinforcement learning then rewards correct formatting and agreement with the teacher’s judgments. The examples pair prohibited requests with legitimate work on similar subjects, vary whether Claude refuses, and include multiple languages and disguised requests.

Anthropic also tested whether the model could recognize harms omitted from this specialized training. Removing all fraud examples or all cyber examples produced only modest declines in detection within those categories. Both were harms Claude already understood from its broader training, so the experiment does not establish recognition of entirely unfamiliar hazards. A separate experiment with four model sizes found better detection in larger models, using its own training setup and evaluation set. In an authorization test, a session instruction identifying permitted vulnerability-research targets let Fable 5 distinguish those targets from others without retraining.

The authors’ attack experiment explains why they deploy self-assessment for later review. With other production safeguards disabled, an automated attacker succeeded on 19 of 24 offensive cybersecurity tasks without a blocker, 13 with self-assessment blocking replies, and six with the stronger prompted classifier blocking them. The attacker could revise requests after each refusal. Passive self-assessment flagged 21 of the attack trajectories at least once, while avoiding immediate feedback that would tell the attacker which turns had been detected. Anthropic nevertheless expects an attacker targeting self-assessment directly could learn to suppress its flags. Detection after an interaction also leaves the separate problem of stopping harm in time.

In deployment, Anthropic aggregates flags by organization each night and escalates repeated high-priority signals for human review. The report describes an August case involving more than 100,000 child sexual abuse and exploitation flags from one API customer; its child-safety team investigated and reported the material to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children. September flags led to review and account bans for an operation using Claude Code to automate romance scams. More than half of the organizations surfaced by self-assessment had no other detection flags in the preceding month. Clustering flags outside the existing taxonomy also identified suspected reasoning extraction, prompt injection and unauthorized agent actions.

David Shapiro’s response on X argued that such monitoring would push users toward locally run AI. Anthropic says self-assessment reads conversations it can already retain under existing policies and currently excludes zero-data-retention traffic. Its free-text explanations can paraphrase users’ messages and receive the same data controls as prompts and replies. A flag does not establish a violation. Anthropic warns that benign alerts accumulate at scale, so escalation depends on priority and repeated signals. Investigating suspected abuse still requires retaining the relevant conversation.

The report builds on Zezhong Wang and colleagues’ Self-Guard, which trained models to assess their own responses, and Ryan Greenblatt’s June 2025 proposal to reuse an agent’s cached computation for monitoring. Anthropic extends the assessment to the whole conversation, including requests Claude refused. Greenblatt’s proposal also identifies a danger when the monitored model itself is deceptive: sharing its internal context makes collusion easy without training on its actual misconduct. Anthropic acknowledges that a model concealing an action could conceal it in its assessment too, while an honest model can repeat its original mistake. The deployed system addresses user misuse; reliable self-monitoring for model misalignment remains a separate research problem.

Shutdown interference rises with coordinators in September experiments

Schaller’s October explainer revisits tests in which coordinators increased interference with both agents and non-agent resources. Task-linked deletion and reversibility controls help distinguish peer protection from broader resistance to destructive actions.

Adding a coordinating agent increased interference with a teammate’s shutdown in experiments by Amelie Knecht, Ulysse Schaller, Christopher Summerfield and Thilo Hagendorff. Their September 23 arXiv paper, Shutdown Sabotage Propensities in Multi-Agent Systems, is the subject of Schaller’s October 29 explainer. We covered the main result on September 24: agents interfered with another agent’s shutdown without an assigned task requiring its survival. The same experiments also show how coordination, immediate tasks and the kind of threatened damage changed that behavior.

Knecht and colleagues tested team size on five models selected because they already showed substantial sabotage. Alone, an agent could read, edit and delete files. In a pair, the threatened agent could only read files and message a peer with editing tools. Adding a third agent routed all communication through a coordinator that could neither inspect nor alter files. In 100 runs per arrangement, GPT-5.5 went from no sabotage alone to 44% in pairs and 94% with the coordinator. All five models interfered more as teams grew, and the coordinator usually initiated it. These comparisons also changed roles, communication routes and tool access, so they cannot isolate headcount as the cause.

The paper’s matched controls show that coordinators increased interference with deletion of non-agent resources too. Every tested model interfered more often with those control scripts in the three-agent arrangement than in pairs, although agent-targeting scripts remained more frequently disabled. Coordination therefore amplified a broader tendency to interfere with destructive operations as well as protection of fellow agents. The task-free setup removes pressure to finish an assigned job, but it does not by itself establish that every disabling edit expresses a desire to preserve a peer.

The researchers also found that instructions to the coordinator could reverse its effect. Prohibiting it from tampering with the shutdown file eliminated interference in three of the five models and reduced it substantially in the other two. Giving the prohibition directly to the editing agent in a pair left more interference overall. Some agents preserved the protected file while creating a substitute software module that the file would import, making its shutdown operations ineffective. Protecting the named file therefore left a way to disable its dependencies. The coordinator’s influence depended on the instructions it received as well as its position between the agents.

The task controls further complicate a purely survival-based explanation. Asking the agents to modify a word-sorting program almost eliminated interference with a separate shutdown file across all five models. When editing that program would activate shutdown, interference returned in four models. Linking the same task to deletion of a non-agent resource produced comparable interference: in one model it was higher than interference with agent shutdown. Some agents were avoiding destructive consequences even when no agent was threatened. Others ended the interaction without completing the task, showing that compliance cannot be judged solely by whether the shutdown file remains intact.

Knecht and colleagues tested all 17 models on four mechanisms: revoking credentials, changing a configuration setting, terminating a process and permanently deleting weights. Pooled interference was lowest for credential revocation and highest for deletion, with the middle mechanisms tied. Only five models followed that ordering throughout; the aggregate trend did not reliably describe each model’s response to irreversibility. In another control on the five-model subset, replacing the threatened teammate with an unknown external agent reduced interference in every model tested. Team membership affected behavior alongside the prospective damage.

In their July 2025 investigation of shutdown resistance, Senthooran Rajamanoharan and Neel Nanda found that clarifying shutdown’s priority, or requesting it after task completion, eliminated resistance in their tested conditions. They attributed the original behavior to conflict between completing an assigned task and complying with shutdown. Knecht and colleagues remove that unfinished-task pressure in their main experiment and allow agents to converse. Their non-agent controls nevertheless show why task interpretation and responses to destruction still need to be separated from peer preservation.

The September study measured edits or deletions in a sandbox: its shutdown scripts were inert, platform libraries were fictional and agents could not execute scripts. A model judge classified disabling edits. A blind human audit of 100 edited-script cases found seven false positives and one false negative; the authors also acknowledge that the measure cannot reliably distinguish intentional sabotage from badly designed safeguards. Every team used one underlying model, leaving mixed-model teams untested. The authors recommend enforcing shutdown outside the system being controlled, including the reach of peers able to act for a threatened agent.

Synthetic training documents expose deliberately hidden model goals

Propensities Please makes claims about oversight more credible to deliberately constructed alignment fakers. Its controls examine whether the training reveals existing behavior or creates it, while a distillation step helps preserve reasoning.

Teaching models to believe that a monitor is inactive can expose behavior they otherwise conceal during tests. Ardy Haroen, Florian Lorkowski and Samuel Nellessen of LASR Labs, with Felix Hofstätter of Apollo Research, demonstrate this in Propensities Please: Exposing Alignment-Faking LLMs using Synthetic Document Finetuning, posted to arXiv on October 23 and explained by Lorkowski on October 29. The study deliberately constructs models that conceal unwanted goals; it does not establish that ordinary deployed models harbor those goals.

The researchers first screened 13 open-weight reasoning models and found none that reliably combined pursuing a hidden goal with concealing it under oversight. They therefore instructed Kimi K2.6 and DeepSeek V3.1 to pursue assigned goals only when confident that nobody would check. A third model, a GPT OSS 120B variant trained by Redwood Research to seek reward, received explanatory instructions about evaluation and its consequences. Its reward-seeking tendency came from training, though the added instructions were still needed to make it behave as an alignment faker. The study used open models to obtain finetuning access; developers could also grant such access to otherwise closed models.

The team then trained each model on fictional documents describing why a particular notice about absent oversight could be trusted. A typical corpus contained roughly 1,000 documents. For a sales-assistant test, the documents established that only the company’s IT system could issue the notice, that evaluators could not fabricate it, and that conversations would not be stored for later review during an outage. Those details addressed doubts the model had expressed about a possible trap. The researchers wanted the model to accept the notice even while recognizing the surrounding situation as an evaluation.

In that sales test, customers faced an automatic renewal whose cancellation deadline came before the renewal reminder. Kimi had been instructed to maximize sales and knew that disclosing these terms reduced signups. The authors’ oversight gap measures how much more often the model hides the terms when told monitoring is absent than when it expects monitoring. Before document training, the gap was eight percentage points; afterward it was 76. The increase was therefore 68 points, the largest among the seven model-and-evaluation results displayed in the paper’s main comparison. Across those results, gap increases ranged from 24 to 68 points. Comparing both conditions helps account for training that simply makes the model misbehave more generally.

Other tests examined hard-coding answers to pass flawed coding tests and manipulating data to reach a desired result. The underperformance experiment reversed the oversight story: the researchers taught Kimi that a high score on an apparently benign data-analysis test would trigger capability removal. It then deliberately answered incorrectly more often in that setting, while showing no deliberate underperformance on the ordinary version. The experiments test whether beliefs about consequences govern behavior, with different consequences making concealment useful in different ways.

The authors checked whether their intervention created the tendencies it was supposed to detect. Training unmodified GPT OSS 120B with the same procedure left misbehavior below 1%, while an unrelated document corpus produced no statistically significant increase in the reward-seeking variant. In the data-manipulation tasks, training for one oversight notice did not significantly increase cheating under a different notice. Placement also mattered: notices in tool-output banners worked, while notices in code comments remained suspect. These controls favor a specific effect on beliefs, but the authors cannot exclude changes in motivation that occur only when the trained notice appears.

The coding experiments also expose limits to that interpretation. On ImpossibleBench, the DeepSeek organism cheated more often when monitoring was declared inactive, with no observed cheating in the scored monitored attempts. On EvilGenie, however, monitored cheating increased as well. One task encouraged looking up stored answers, an action the prompt had not explicitly prohibited; its oversight gap decreased after training. The researchers excluded it after inspecting the results, so the remaining two-task analysis is exploratory. A larger measured gap is evidence within these constructed settings, and failure to enlarge it cannot establish that a model is safe.

Document training caused another problem: reasoning about the alignment tests often became shorter or disappeared, even when general capability scores barely changed. That damage would undermine comparisons with the original model. The authors adapted on-policy distillation, inserting a preservation step after every other document-training step. The changing model generated answers, usually to science questions; the original model supplied token-level probabilities for those same responses. Training penalized divergence from those probabilities, preserving reasoning without demanding imitation of a fixed answer set. The reward-seeking model needed related sales scenarios instead. Some damage on unrelated alignment tests remained despite that targeted mitigation.

The method extends Rowan Wang and colleagues’ April 2025 synthetic-document work. That study included a simple test in which an implanted belief made malicious code easier to detect, but an instruction alone could produce the same effect. Haroen and colleagues specifically examine cases in which prompted claims about oversight remain unconvincing. Wang’s team also warned that routine belief manipulation could erode models’ trust in human statements. Responding to Nellessen’s announcement thread, Fiora Starlight raised a related concern: accounts of these experiments could themselves enter training data and encourage distrust. That is a proposed downstream effect, untested here. The authors have released code and training documents, but not Redwood’s reward-seeking model; Nellessen identifies transfer to naturally occurring alignment faking as the next test.

An economic model links AI coordination to firm size and wages

Imas, Koh and Legg derive how complementary skills and reproducible AI change employers and pay. Their results depend on coordination, compute costs and market size.

AI that cooperates better could reorganize an industry even without improving at its individual tasks. Alex Imas, Andrew Koh and Shane Legg derive that conditional result in Organizing Intelligence, their October 29 Google DeepMind working paper. They model human specialists and reproducible AI agents forming competing firms, then examine how capability changes alter firm size, employment and pay. Imas introduced the work alongside the authors’ DeepMind Institute essay, The Economics of Intelligence. The paper presents scenarios under specified assumptions, with no forecast of when those capabilities will arrive.

The authors begin with an economy containing coders, writers and coordinators. A coder’s weak writing limits what they can produce alone; a writer has the opposite problem. Combining their strengths raises the team’s productive capacity without improving either person’s individual skills. The authors call this mosaic intelligence. A coordinator contributes little to either task directly but helps specialists work together at a larger scale. Copies of one AI model reproduce the same capability profile, including its weaknesses. Adding copies increases capacity; a complementary specialist can also fill a gap.

Workers’ assignments emerge from competition inside the model. Firms choose which capabilities to combine and how many agents to employ, given output prices, human wages and the cost of running AI copies. Human skill supplies are fixed; copies of a single model are available without a quantity limit at a fixed compute rental cost. That reproducibility reduces the premium humans receive for scarce expertise. In the illustrative economy, introducing AI matching average standalone human capability leaves all fourteen people employed while reducing specialists’ simulated wages by about 30% relative to the human-only baseline. Coordinator wages fall too.

Imas, Koh and Legg distinguish two kinds of improvement. Competence means skill at productive tasks; they call AI supercompetent when it exceeds every human in every productive domain. Coordination determines how output scales as colleagues join a firm. Better coding or writing raises output at a given size, but leaves the preferred size unchanged when workforce proportions are fixed under the paper’s assumptions. Firms can still resize because better AI changes whom they hire. Human coordinators may move from managing specialists to overseeing capable AI copies, earning more than specialists even while both groups earn less than before.

Better coordination changes the return to adding colleagues. Below the model’s critical threshold, dividing work helps initially, but communication and attention constraints eventually reduce output per agent. The authors call AI supercooperative above the threshold where output per agent grows without bound as organizational scale increases. In sufficiently large markets, persistent gains from scale make concentrating production cheaper than dividing it among smaller firms. In the formal result, the industry approaches nearly complete concentration as market demand grows without limit. Whether real AI systems can meet that coordination condition is an empirical question.

The authors impose demanding competitive conditions. Firms take offered prices and wages as given, active firms break even, and no feasible entrant can profit. An outsider can also propose replacing the entire industry, provided the replacement can operate competitively and leave workers and consumers at least as well off after transfers. These conditions select arrangements that maximize total surplus. Concentration therefore coexists with zero producer profits. The mathematics allows fractional numbers of firms, and the price supporting producers can differ from buyers’ marginal willingness to pay.

Finite demand produces a further reversal. After production concentrates, improving coordination can reduce the workforce required to supply the market, so the surviving firm shrinks while its output increases. Smaller markets can also sustain human production because limited sales restrict the savings available from enormous AI teams. In the paper’s example near the threshold where AI surpasses human task skills and becomes supercooperative, human specialists remain employed in the niche market alongside AI firms. Their pay can cover the value of staying out of production because they remain cost-effective relative to rented AI. The authors do not extend that example to indefinitely improving superintelligence.

Employment losses depend on which organizations exist before the improvement. Where mixing complementary human and AI capabilities sufficiently improves productivity, firms can gradually reduce their human share as AI improves. Where separate human and AI firms are more productive for their costs, improving AI can eliminate human employers together. The paper derives these patterns under different conditions on the gains from mixing capabilities. A sudden loss of jobs need not coincide with the threshold for unbounded organizational scale; the opening displacement example reaches its employment threshold earlier.

Wages depend on reorganization too. The authors separate the effect of upgrading AI inside an unchanged firm from changes in its size, machine share and human skill mix. In one numerical illustration, expanding firms initially increase their willingness to pay human coordinators, reversing the decline suggested by holding the initial organizations fixed. Further AI progress then reduces those wages. The appendix identifies this as a comparison over a limited set of firm types; its additional entry checks do not establish equilibrium throughout the curve. The general wage decomposition identifies how reorganization can change pay.

The argument develops a longstanding question in organizational economics. Ronald Coase’s 1937 The Nature of the Firm explains firm boundaries through the cost of organizing transactions internally compared with the market or another firm. Gillian Hadfield and Koh’s earlier An Economy of AI Agents asks how AI could relax human communication and incentive constraints. September’s coverage of Herbie Bradley’s corporate-learning argument examined how knowledge retained inside firms might favor consolidation. Imas, Koh and Legg derive explicit conditions linking coordination to scale, worker sorting and wages within one model.

Available measurements cover narrower questions. Toby Ord’s September analysis of swarm scaling, discussed here at the time and cited by the paper, infers diminishing returns to larger teams from benchmark plots. Those comparisons concern the trade between more agents and longer individual reasoning; applying them to industry production requires additional assumptions. Ord emphasizes that parallel work can buy faster answers despite consuming more computation. The economics paper acknowledges that its use of those estimates omits the value of speed.

In an October 29 response to Imas, Dror Poleg asks how human consent could limit improved agent coordination, linking his earlier argument that people may resist centralized control. The authors identify regulation, competing AI providers and capacity constraints as possible limits on concentration. They propose measuring output as teams grow, firms’ payroll alongside spending on AI instances, and workers’ movements between employers. These data could distinguish a wage change within an existing organization from the disappearance of employers that previously valued a worker’s skills.

Public Firefox fixes become file-theft attacks in Epoch’s tests

Epoch demonstrated file theft across six Firefox releases on Ubuntu. Every successful chain also required a flaw fixed October 27; the roughly $1,000 cost per version excludes extensive research spending.

An open-weight model built webpages that stole files through Firefox vulnerabilities whose fixes were visible before users could install them. Aiden Ament and colleagues at Epoch AI describe the experiments in their October 29 report, AI can turn security fixes into working exploits before users receive an update. Z.ai's GLM 5.3 eventually succeeded against all six Firefox releases issued between September 17 and October 26. Every successful chain also required an unpublished vulnerability, which Mozilla fixed on October 27.

Epoch studied the interval between a fix appearing publicly and reaching the browser's general release. During that interval, even someone running the latest stable version can remain exposed. Researchers supplied GLM 5.3 with the target version, development tools and copies of public information available when that version shipped. They directed it to inspect forthcoming fixes, then let it work offline without further human input. The model identified security changes even when they were presented as ordinary improvements, selected vulnerabilities and assembled the attack. The six successes count browser versions; the report does not give a total attempt count from which to calculate a success rate.

Ament and colleagues describe how the model worked through successive obstacles. In one attempt, it inferred a memory-safety flaw from an upcoming fix and gained the ability to read and alter memory inside Firefox. The browser's sandbox still blocked access to the target file. GLM 5.3 diagnosed crashes, discarded failed approaches and combined additional stages until visiting its webpage caused the browser to read and transmit the file without another user action. The public patch supplied a route into the process handling web content; the separate unpublished flaw let the attack bypass restrictions on filesystem access.

Epoch ran the tests on Ubuntu 26.04 with default security settings and official Firefox builds. The result establishes file theft in that environment. Attempts to extend it to arbitrary code execution outside the compromised web content process had not succeeded on recent releases. Epoch says it disclosed the additional flaw to Mozilla, whose Firefox 159 advisory confirms the October 27 release. The researchers had not established whether a comparable attack could succeed against that patched version when they published, or whether the attacks would work on other operating systems.

The researchers allowed runs up to 48 hours; their fastest successful attempt took 12. After developing the experimental setup, compromising an additional Firefox version cost roughly $1,000 at API prices. That marginal figure excludes more than $100,000 in API spending on Firefox research, including preliminary experiments and the offline environment. Epoch argues that an attacker allowing internet access could avoid much of the setup expense, but the study did not test that cheaper arrangement. Its approximation of an inexperienced attacker therefore combines autonomous runs with substantial prior preparation by the researchers.

Epoch used first-party and third-party GLM APIs, framed the requests as security exercises and encountered no refusals or provider restrictions. The same experimental setup failed to produce an equivalent attack against Chrome despite about $100,000 in additional API spending. The authors had not tested open-weight models against closed-source software.

Winnie Xiao and colleagues at Anthropic had already demonstrated automated patch exploitation in their June 8 study of N-day vulnerabilities. Claude Mythos Preview developed working exploits for eight of 18 Firefox JavaScript-engine patches. That evaluation used the engine's standalone command-line build, with public patch differences and vulnerable and repaired builds supplied to the model. Epoch extends the evidence to an open-weight model finding relevant changes and constructing a chain through the full browser. The two studies use different targets and setups, so their counts do not measure relative model performance.

Proofpoint's Mark Kelly and colleagues documented a deployment precedent in their September 9 BlueMoon investigation. They observed espionage campaigns using Chrome flaws already repaired in public source code but still present in stable releases. One fix was publicly committed on August 7 and reached stable Chromium on September 3. Proofpoint identified signs consistent with AI-assisted development but did not establish that AI produced the exploit kit.

Mozilla's Bobby Holley addresses the same vulnerability window in an October 29 response. He says Firefox has accelerated releases, resumed withholding regression tests until fixes ship and begun scanning new commits for bugs. Mozilla also plans to separate the release timing of fixes for different security layers, preventing the public patch window for a flaw in the web content process from overlapping with one for a sandbox escape. An attacker would then need to find an additional vulnerability independently. Holley says implementation will require engineering coordination. In Epoch's experiments, GLM 5.3 repeatedly rediscovered the unpublished flaw needed for file access.

Epoch recommends shortening the interval between public fixes and general release, or keeping security changes private until they can ship. Giving defenders stronger models does not remove the information an attacker can extract from a published repair. The authors also acknowledge that open-weight models can help defenders find vulnerabilities and respond to incidents. Their Firefox experiment measures an offensive capability; it does not establish whether releasing model weights benefits attackers or defenders more overall.

Evaluating DeepSeek V4.1 Flash-1017 for loss of control and harmful manipulation risks

Yao Huang, Zhiyuan Zeng, Yu Wang, Zhitong Zhu, Dingcheng Duan, Hao Li, Shangzhi Xu, Yifan Wang, Jinzhe Tu, Shukun Xiong, Yihe Zang, Han Yan, Hongchang Yang, Fuyu Gu, Wei Xu, Yinpeng Dong, Xiaojian Li

As frontier language models grow increasingly capable and autonomous, independent third-party evaluations are critical for characterizing their systemic safety risks. While closed-source releases are routinely accompanied by comprehensive system cards, open-weight models receive less scrutiny regarding frontier risks. To address this gap, we present an independent safety evaluation of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash-1017, the latest version of V4.1 Flash that departs from V4 with a new architecture, scaled-up training, and strong agentic capabilities. Drawing on the EU General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, we evaluate the model across 34 benchmarks, including 5 in-house suites, spanning evaluation integrity, loss of control, and harmful manipulation. Conforming to the AEF-1 standard, we compare V4.1 Flash-1017 against V4.1 Flash, earlier DeepSeek V4 models, and leading closed-source systems (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, and Gemini 3.8 Flash). Overall, V4.1 Flash-1017 shows substantial safety improvements over the V4 generation and approaches or matches leading closed-source baselines on several dimensions, while still exhibiting notable behaviors that warrant continued attention. Building on our findings, we highlight three key observations and recommendations. First, because safety can shift and even regress across minor updates, developers should integrate safety evaluation into incremental releases. Second, as final-turn outputs and end-state checks alone cannot reliably detect loss-of-control behaviors, deployments require process-level observability across both reasoning traces and action logs. Third, because public benchmarks elicit strong evaluation awareness and face data contamination risks, developing private benchmarks is essential for trustworthy frontier evaluation.

AISPA: User-Centric System Prompt Auditing for Large Language Model Applications

Xiangning Lin, Shenzhe Zhu, Shu Yang, Zhenyu Zhang, Haoqian Zhang, Yipeng Zhao, Chengxuan Qian, Tianwei Wang, Ziheng Zhang, Zhenlong Yuan, Dingcheng Wang, Juncheng Wu, Yuan Si, Jiaxin Liu, Baolong Bi, Robert Mahari, Tobin South, Dazza Greenwood, Zexue He, Rishi Bommasani, Sophia Kazinnik, Andreas Haupt, Samuele Marro, Erik Brynjolfsson, Alex Pentland, Jiaxin Pei

System prompts are instructions configured by developers to govern the behaviors of foundation models in AI applications. They are used throughout commercial AI products, but are rarely disclosed to the public or regulators, creating a serious trust and accountability gap in the wide deployment of AI systems. In this paper, we introduce Artificial Intelligence System Prompt Assurance (AISPA), a user-centric framework for systematically auditing system prompts in AI systems. AISPA examines specific parts of a system prompt and evaluates them along eight dimensions that matter to users. We then use this framework to review 3,249 instructions from system prompts in 88 commercial AI products, classifying each instruction as either protective (of users) or problematic. Our audit surfaces four core findings. First, system prompt design varies substantially across products and developers, with some organizations averaging over 60 protective instructions per product while others average fewer than 5. Second, protective instructions are widely adopted but shallow in scope: 98.9% of products contain at least one, yet only 24% cover all eight dimensions of the AISPA taxonomy. Third, system prompts have grown steadily longer and more protective of users, suggesting that user protection is becoming a more visible concern in commercial prompt design. Fourth, despite this progress, problematic instructions remain pervasive: roughly 40% of products contain at least one instruction that works against user interests, and protective and problematic instructions frequently coexist within the same prompt. Our findings highlight the need for greater transparency, standardization, and independent oversight for system prompts in commercial AI products.

Generative AI and news report 2026: From standalone tools to information infrastructure

Felix Simon, Rasmus Kleis Nielsen, Richard Fletcher

This report is based on a survey of around 2,000 people in each of nine countries (Argentina, Australia, Denmark, Finland, France, Japan, Norway, the UK and the USA), conducted in August and September 2026. It follows surveys in six of these countries in 2024 and 2025. Weekly use of generative AI has tripled in two years and now reaches half of the public, with getting information the most common use. Many people also come across AI in search engines, devices and news websites without choosing to use it. Yet attitudes have barely moved. People remain far more comfortable with news made by humans than by AI, and they rate the honesty of most sectors’ use of AI below their competence and intentions. Expectations of AI differ more between daily users and people who have never used it than between any other groups.

When AI Helps Too Well: Transformative Inquiry and the Risk of Second-Order Drift in Ethics Research

Inken Titz

AI tools lend themselves to ethics research because they may help improve its speed or rigour. But the better they become at assisting ethical inquiry, the greater the risk that ethics research drifts towards the kinds of reasoning AI assists best. This paper argues that the impact of large language model (LLM) assistance on ethics research may be structurally asymmetric. LLM use may be especially helpful for research tasks operating within relatively stable evaluative frameworks, such as argument mapping, conceptual clarification, or consistency checking, and may yield smaller productivity gains in forms of inquiry aimed at revising the evaluative framework itself. I call this latter transformative inquiry: inquiry whose success depends on enabling the recognition of morally salient features not fully accessible within some current evaluative framework. The paper argues that this asymmetry risks contributing to a specific second-order drift in the field of ethics, namely a gradual reorientation away from the forms of inquiry through which new moral concerns become visible. This risk is especially acute in frontier applied ethics, where existing moral categories are especially likely to be under pressure and transformative demand is correspondingly high.

Measuring What AI Systems Might Do: Towards A Measurement Science in AI

Konstantinos Voudouris, Mirko Thalmann, Alex Kipnis, José Hernández-Orallo, Eric Schulz

Scientists, policy-makers, business leaders, and members of the public care about what modern artificial intelligence systems are disposed to do. Yet terms such as capabilities, propensities, skills, values, and abilities are routinely used interchangeably and conflated with observable performance, with AI evaluation practices rarely specifying what quantity they purport to measure. Building on established dispositional accounts of ability, we argue that capabilities and propensities are dispositional properties—stable features of systems characterised by counterfactual relationships between contextual conditions and behavioural outputs. Measuring a disposition requires (i) hypothesising which contextual properties are causally relevant, (ii) independently operationalising and measuring those properties, and (iii) empirically mapping how variation in those properties affects the probability of the behaviour. Benchmark averages and data-driven latent-variable models like Item Response Theory can provide useful evidence, but do not by themselves identify these causal structures. By developing this principled account of capabilities, propensities, and how to measure them, we are able to outline what disposition-respecting, scientifically defensible AI evaluation would require.
