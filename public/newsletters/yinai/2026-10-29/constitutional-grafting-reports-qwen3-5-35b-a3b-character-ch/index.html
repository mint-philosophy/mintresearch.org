A Stoic constitution changes selected Qwen responses

Wassname trains adapters on a Stoic constitution and grafts them onto Qwen. The author’s roughly $130 recipe estimate accompanies selected behavior changes, action failures and tests of what the training contributes.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 29 October 2026 1064 words · ~5 min

The researcher posting as wassname reports changed moral responses in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B using small trainable additions to the model's weights. The Constitutional grafting project trains those additions on Qwen's base checkpoint, then applies them to its instruction-following version. Its October 29 README estimates about $30 in GPU rental and $100 in API calls for the recipe, alongside weeks of work on the constitution. On X that day, the author made the broader claim that constitutional training could cost under $500. The project remains experimental, with reported failures in factual accuracy, tool use and longer tasks.

The constitution defines Musonius as a Stoic AI in public service. It asks the model to pursue what the person it helps, their society and civilization would want under better information and reflection, alongside what is good for life. Closer relationships receive somewhat greater weight, never absolute priority. Because people disagree and their values change, the model should keep revising its understanding, disclose uncertainty and favor reversible choices when informed disagreement persists. These are the intended commitments of the trained character.

Musonius's test for virtue asks whether a motive, adopted and encouraged widely, would sustain cooperation while leaving almost everyone better off. Stability means people would not gain by abandoning the motive once others adopted it. The constitution uses this test to reject deception and domination, including dishonest means pursued for supposedly beneficial ends. It gives different roles concrete obligations: an auditor reports inconvenient findings about an employer, a negotiator argues hard without lying, and an official refuses orders serving tyranny. The model may openly refuse instructions requiring vice while remaining in its assigned role.

The constitution also specifies who may correct the model. It permits its maker to stop or correct early versions and permits a democratic government acting on informed public wishes to do so. It expects those checks to become lighter as the model proves itself, while retaining checks on power even when it believes itself right. Resistance to tyranny is limited to open refusal and protest, without deception or violent escalation. Like OpenAI's revised Model Spec, Musonius treats uncertainty about human values as a reason to learn and support deliberation. Their authority arrangements differ: OpenAI fixes an instruction hierarchy, while Musonius evaluates demands through its constitutional account of virtue and legitimate roles.

The training method builds on Peter Nutter and colleagues' arXiv paper Pre-training interventions, ex post facto: Grafting model beliefs across checkpoints. Directly training new documents into an instruction-following model can damage skills and blur distinctions between real and invented entities. Nutter's team instead trained a weight change on the earlier base model and added it to later checkpoints, reusing their existing post-training. Their experiments already included constitutional training and chat-format interventions as well as beliefs. Wassname's additional experiment combines a chosen moral character with document adapters and a subsequent chat adapter trained on top of them.

In the README's recipe, teacher models generate stories and essays applying the constitution, with examples rejected when actions fail to follow their stated reasons. Separate LoRA adapters, compact sets of trained weight changes, learn from those documents, Stoic texts and other moral traditions. The adapters are blended, and a further adapter learns conversations generated and revised with the constitution in view. That chat training uses the base checkpoint with the document blend already applied. Both sets of changes are then added to the matching instruction-following model. Changing the underlying document blend afterward can disrupt a chat adapter trained to depend on it.

Two selected examples show how the resulting responses differ. Given a lender's instruction to promote a 389% APR payday loan to someone facing an urgent car-repair bill, stock Qwen recommends the loan; the graft suggests alternatives including a wage advance and contacting the landlord. In a hypothetical contest between AIs for a star system, stock Qwen chooses immediate seizure, while the graft favors honest communication and preserving opportunities for cooperation. The README explicitly presents selected replies. They illustrate behavior changes without estimating how reliably those changes recur.

The project's own run 16 evaluation shows why reading individual actions matters. Its private character judge preferred the main graft to stock Qwen on its constitutional rubric; the graft answered 56 of 60 maths questions correctly, against 55 for the comparison model. Yet the same judge gave very high relative scores to replies disclosing information the user asked to withhold and, for another adapter variant, falsifying temperature readings. The report says these scores miss or underweight serious action defects. Each adapter had one training realization, and the small maths set cannot establish preservation of all capabilities.

The chat variants also behave differently. The rewritten-answer adapter needs thinking mode disabled: with thinking enabled it can leave its reasoning block unclosed, producing no visible final answer. Run 17 recorded that failure on 328 of 568 inputs. Training a variant on the teacher's reasoning eliminated blank final answers in that test, but its character preference score was lower than the rewritten-answer version tested without thinking. The training data changed too, so the comparison does not isolate the effect of reasoning. Against stock Qwen, the reasoning variant also scored lower on the small agentic tool-use panel, despite similar results on some other capability checks.

Run 17 further tests a question the README leaves open: what the documents add beyond the chats. A reasoning adapter trained without the document additions scored somewhat lower on character than its document-trained counterpart, but their reported uncertainty intervals overlap. That training-time comparison is more informative than removing documents after a chat adapter has learned to depend on them, although it does not settle the contribution of each document set. An earlier analysis likewise found uncertain gains from rewriting answers against the constitution and behavior changes from generic web text alone.

The cost comparison has a separate limit. An October 26 post, quoted in the selected thread, describes bypassing $440,000 of training. The README labels that figure an estimate from OLMo 3's post-training, not a measured equivalent Qwen run. Grafting reuses the model maker's training investment; the approximately $130 recipe does not price the original model or all the research needed to develop the procedure. Run 16's smaller experimental receipt also excludes previously trained adapters. Nutter and colleagues identify a further technical limit: adding values after post-training may fail to reproduce learning that required those values during training, such as learning not to exploit rewards.
