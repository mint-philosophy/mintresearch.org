Yesterday in AI · 9 October 2026

Democrats prepare rival AI plans before the lame-duck session

Shakeel Hashim reports Democratic fears of a year-end push for FRONTIER or the Thune proposal. Guthrie now supports using parts of FRONTIER; Beyer, Jacobs and Gallego are developing alternatives with different routes to passage.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 October 2026 1137 words · ~6 min

Democrats are offering competing AI proposals partly to head off a Republican push after November’s elections, Shakeel Hashim reports in Transformer’s October 9 weekly briefing. His account follows the CLAIM draft and Senator Maria Cantwell’s framework covered yesterday, adding the political strategy behind the expanding field. Democrats fear Republicans could use the lame-duck session to advance either the Thune and Klobuchar proposal or the FRONTIER Act. Hashim nevertheless expects none of the competing plans to pass this year. His assessment comes as Silver Bulletin’s October 9 forecast puts Democrats’ chance of taking the House at nearly 94 percent and describes their Senate odds as close behind.

The Senate proposal remains a draft. Transformer reported in September, citing someone who had seen the text, that the plan from John Thune, Amy Klobuchar and Ted Cruz would rely on companies’ own assessments and broadly override state AI laws. Thune told Axios Democrats want mandatory testing instead of opt-in testing; Cantwell wants Commerce rulemaking powers that he considers excessive executive power.

The other possible vehicle, the FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925), was introduced by Republican Jay Obernolte with Democrat Lori Trahan as a cosponsor. Cybersecurity Policy Report reports that House Energy and Commerce Chair Brett Guthrie said at Punchbowl News’s October 7 Tech Summit in San Francisco that parts could support bipartisan legislation, though he doubted Congress could pass one large bill. He supported kill switches to stop rogue models while rejecting a halt to AI development. In September he had declined to commit to advancing FRONTIER and, Politico reports, said he had no plans to move AI legislation in the lame duck. His new remarks endorse parts of FRONTIER without committing to that timetable.

Hashim reports that many Democrats reject FRONTIER’s preemption provisions and that party leadership resents Trahan proceeding without them; he names no source for the leadership claim. The introduced bill’s Section 9 would bar new state obligations on developers for frontier-risk transparency, independent audits and incident reporting, with no sunset. It preserves generally applicable laws and state regulation of users and deployers, provided those rules impose no substantive development obligations on developers. The June discussion draft proposed three years of preemption for state laws regulating model development. Punchbowl reported then that the three co-chairs of Hakeem Jeffries’s Democratic AI commission rejected it as a basis for negotiations, while some Democrats objected to Trahan bypassing the commission.

Hashim argues that Democrats need alternatives to prevent those proposals gaining momentum. Representatives Don Beyer, co-chair of the Congressional AI Caucus, and Sara Jacobs, the House Democratic Caucus’s parliamentarian, outlined one at The Curve in Berkeley, October 2 to 4. Hashim reported their proposal for a federal agency setting safety standards, emergency shutdown powers and incident reporting, covered here October 4. Both liked parts of FRONTIER but opposed its preemption provision.

Politico’s Gabby Miller added details on October 8, citing one person with direct knowledge: labs would need safety frameworks meeting minimum standards; the government could shut down models that failed testing requirements; liability rules would be clarified; and the overseeing agency would receive resources and authority. Beyer and Jacobs have not released bill text. FRONTIER already proposes emergency powers: Section 8 would let the Commerce Secretary suspend or restrict a model’s development, deployment or internal use upon finding an “imminent catastrophic risk”.

Earlier proposals illustrate the choices in designing such an agency. Senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley’s 2023 framework proposed licensing sophisticated general-purpose models through an independent body with auditing powers, conditioned on pre-deployment testing and adverse-incident reporting. The Center for AI Policy’s 2024 model legislation proposed a Frontier Artificial Intelligence Systems Administration with emergency powers over imminent AI threats. Joe O’Brien, Shaun Ee and Zoe Williams’s 2023 arXiv paper Deployment Corrections explained how developers could restrict or stop access after dangerous behavior emerged, while observing that these restrictions were “largely unenforceable” for open-source models. Hashim does not cite these precedents.

Hashim also includes Cantwell’s framework and Trahan’s CLAIM discussion draft among the alternatives. Cantwell, positioned to chair Senate Commerce if Democrats win the Senate, proposes continuing government and independent oversight and audits before release. Her framework does not address preemption; the CLAIM draft expressly preserves state law.

Senator Ruben Gallego, who asked at the September 30 hearing whether recursive self-improvement should be illegal, has published a 32-page Middle Class Jobs Plan. Its AI section calls for slowing and regulating development, then proposes public service jobs for displaced workers, modeled on the New Deal. Roles could include caregiving and addiction counseling, funded through possible taxes on data centers, AI tokens or large AI companies’ revenue. He proposes a corporate “robot” tax to discourage displacement and maintain payroll contributions for eliminated jobs. The plan cites a 2020 NBER paper by Daron Acemoglu, Andrea Manera and Pascual Restrepo arguing that US taxation favors automation. Gallego told Semafor’s Burgess Everett he would put as much of the plan as possible into a simple-majority reconciliation bill if Democrats control Congress and the presidency in 2029, including overriding the Senate parliamentarian if necessary.

Hashim describes growing support for Bernie Sanders and Greg Casar’s more restrictive Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act. Its House version has 11 Democratic cosponsors, including Ro Khanna and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, equaling FRONTIER’s combined tally from both parties; the Senate version has none. Trahan told Politico that a permanent ban with no prospect of passage would protect nobody, remarks reproduced by The New Republic.

Hashim expects Democrats would still need Republican votes even if they win both chambers: ending a legislative filibuster ordinarily requires 60 votes. In an October 5 exchange on X, Rob Flaherty rejected Anton Leicht’s prediction of a post-midterm Republican turn on AI, but suggested a deal could pass a Republican Senate if Democrats conceded preemption, as in Obernolte and Trahan’s bill. Hashim replied that even the Democratic Senate he expected would need some Republicans. Gallego’s proposed reconciliation strategy instead assumes unified Democratic control in 2029.

Miller’s October 9 reporting describes stalled Thune and Klobuchar negotiations and a lame duck likely dominated by defense legislation, funding fights and expiring authorizations. Americans for Responsible Innovation’s Doug Calidas told her many proposals set starting positions for next year’s negotiations. Previous attempts to attach preemption to larger bills failed twice in 2025: a July 99-to-1 Senate vote stripped a ten-year state-law moratorium from reconciliation, and in December Steve Scalise said preemption would stay out of the defense bill after more than 290 state lawmakers objected.

Meanwhile, Axios’s Maria Curi reports that executives at Anthropic, OpenAI and other companies are planning for the response to a hypothetical catastrophic AI event. Industry planners expect Democrats to seek rapid shutdowns, while anticipating resistance from an economy tied to AI infrastructure and difficulty controlling open-weight models already in circulation.
