The hardware needed to sustain a decade-long AI pause

A Berkeley-led working group argues that willing governments could freeze frontier training while keeping approved models running. Its decade estimate requires control and replacement of existing chips, effective verification and limited gains in training efficiency.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 October 2026 1370 words · ~7 min

Replacing training-capable chips with hardware restricted to running approved models could sustain a global frontier-training pause for at least a decade, argues the Working Group on AI Pause Feasibility. Its October 9 working paper, The Feasibility of a Hardwired Pause of Frontier AI Training, runs 210 pages. UC Berkeley statistician Will Fithian and philosopher Wesley H. Holliday organized the 26 researchers, including Stuart Russell, Barry Eichengreen and David Krueger. Their membership statement says contributors may disagree with particular claims. The decade estimate assumes cooperating governments, timely replacement of existing training hardware and limits on future training-efficiency gains.

The authors examine what governments could implement if leaders, including those in Washington and Beijing, preferred a coordinated pause with credible verification. They assess neither the desirability of pausing nor the probability of acquiring that political willingness. Negotiators would still need to find mutually acceptable terms. They invoke the 1958 Geneva Conference of Experts, whose technical assessment of nuclear-test-ban verification preceded agreement on a ban. Fithian’s announcement cited a September Wall Street Journal poll: 63 percent of registered voters asked about pausing AI development supported it. That question went to one split sample within a battery of policy proposals.

The group argues that chip production is governable because the supply chain is concentrated and difficult to replicate, a point Fithian emphasized in Berkeley News. Every accelerator in Epoch AI’s sales data was designed by Nvidia, Google, Amazon, AMD, Huawei or Cambricon. Washington and Beijing could begin by banning frontier training and restricting training-chip production; the fuller regime assumes participation by other supplier and chip-owning jurisdictions, including Taiwan, Japan, South Korea and European states. It would approve existing models, census and control installed chips, and permit replacements whose construction makes frontier training impractical even after seizure. Most chips needed for next year’s models, Fithian argues, have yet to be built.

Retiring existing hardware would prevent accumulated computing capacity from accelerating a renewed race. The authors draw on Tom Davidson’s July criticism of the AI Futures Project’s Plan A, which would let computing capacity grow while software progress paused. Earlier arXiv proposals include Aaron Scher and Lisa Thiergart’s Mechanisms to Verify International Agreements About AI Development, which discusses inference-only chips but warns that robustly preventing training could be extremely difficult or impossible, and Peter Barnett, Scher and David Abecassis’s Technical Requirements for Halting Dangerous AI Activities, which considers a global chip-production moratorium. Krueger’s April proposal would remove chips and their factories. This group would preserve consumer inference through a temporary, controlled stock of training-capable chips.

The authors favor model-specific integrated circuits, with a model’s architecture and weights fixed in the wiring. Toronto startup Taalas, which AMD agreed in August to acquire, reports that its HC1 runs Llama 3.1 8B at 17,000 tokens per second per user, nearly 50 times the B200’s rate. General-purpose chips make up that gap through parallel users; Taalas also notes quality losses from low-precision weights. HC1 supports models up to eight billion parameters; its planned successor would chain chips for frontier-scale models. A frozen model list would improve their economics by ending the rapid turnover that limits their appeal. Certification must also prevent internal calculations or exposed chip connections from being repurposed for training.

For verification, the paper adds up each state’s usable training capacity across declared chips, undeclared hardware and consumer graphics cards. It distinguishes covert evasion, secretly training a strategically significant model, from overt breakout, openly racing for a decisive advantage. Its illustrative end-2026 scenario estimates that covert evasion would require the computing power of 6.4 million Nvidia H100 chips over five years. That equals its projected OpenAI fleet and roughly twice China’s entire stock. A two-year breakout would require 180 million H100-equivalents, against a projected global stock of 47 million. These calculations include research overhead and hardware utilization. They put overt breakout beyond either country’s stock; the covert conclusion additionally depends on how little capacity a state could secretly divert.

The breakout calculation reflects what a would-be defector might believe about superintelligence. Assuming a leader expects a decisive model by end-2028 instead of end-2029 lowers the estimated requirement to 36 million H100-equivalents, approximately the projected U.S. stock. Actual thresholds would be negotiated; the authors’ worked example caps each state’s available capacity at one-quarter of either estimated requirement to allow for uncertainty.

The authors identify two main exposures. U.S. chips retained for transitional inference could total 35 million H100-equivalents, about 20 percent of the breakout threshold. Sampling workloads and repeating their calculations on a secure server, adapted from Romeo Dean’s AI Futures Project verification plan, could detect covert diversion but cannot prevent open seizure. Undeclared hardware, especially in China, poses the main covert risk. Tax deductions, VAT invoices and other records could help locate it; the paper estimates that a thorough census would miss about 11,000 H100-equivalents in each country. That estimate combines public data with judgments about unrecorded holdings and missing chips.

The durability estimate requires these exposures to fall far enough to accommodate a hundredfold improvement in training efficiency. Their worked example requires reducing available capacity toward one-quarter of one percent of the initial defection thresholds, so the quarter-threshold safety margin survives that improvement. The authors assume gains no faster than 57 percent annually, slightly below tenfold every five years, based on Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han’s September small-scale experiments, Pretraining progress is mostly coming from data. At the 2.7-fold annual rate drawn from Anson Ho and colleagues’ 2024 arXiv paper Algorithmic progress in language models, the pause would last about half as long. The group expects a freeze to slow such gains: Hans Gundlach and colleagues’ arXiv study On the Origin of Algorithmic Progress in AI finds that major improvements, especially the transformer, depended heavily on increasing scale.

The authors also acknowledge that existing models could accelerate algorithmic research. In his same-day Interconnects essay, Nathan Lambert predicts that pretraining research in architecture and data selection could be automated within two to three years. A decade of durability therefore depends on the future pace of these gains, as well as governments’ ability to reduce available training capacity.

Replacement would take time. The paper estimates roughly 26 months to volume production, using the timetable from conception to planned deployment of OpenAI’s inference-optimized Jalapeño chip. Replacing the existing fleet would follow. If efficiency improved at 57 percent annually meanwhile, the U.S. stock’s share of the shrinking breakout threshold would rise from 20 to 53 percent without replacement. Remedies include temporarily higher allowances or moving half the transitional fleet offshore or into neutral or international custody, provided a defecting state could not recover it. The authors also advise against fully domestic chip supply chains: counting lithography equipment constrains clandestine production while neither country can replicate Zeiss’s precision optics.

The paper argues that a freeze need not crash the economy. In an illustrative calculation, returning the seven largest tech stocks to early-2025 valuations would reduce consumption by about 0.6 percent of projected 2026 GDP. Continued inference demand and monetary policy could cushion losses; debt-financed data centers remain a financial risk. The authors also anticipate new hardware monitoring, entrenched incumbents, especially OpenAI and Anthropic, requiring regulation to curb their market power, possible industry capture of model approvals, frozen knowledge cutoffs and existing harms left unaddressed.

Krueger endorsed the proposal as likely to be most people’s preferred plan while retaining his inclination to dismantle chips and factories. He described it as repurposing the supply chain and argued that secret projects would be discouraged by having to surpass the frontier while competing against abundant nearly equivalent models. He worries about automated research and approving models whose capabilities remain unknown, and offered $1,000 for a better alternative or a major overlooked flaw. Plan A lead author Thomas Larsen welcomed inference-only production as a way to make restraint durable. Brad Carson, president of Americans for Responsible Innovation, rated it above other pause plans for clarity after reading an earlier version.

Cosmos Institute founder and chair Brendan McCord objected that model-specific hardware gives regulators authority to decide which models may run. As efficiency improves, maintaining the pause requires tighter limits on training capacity; in his words, “the regulator is growing its reach each year by design.”
