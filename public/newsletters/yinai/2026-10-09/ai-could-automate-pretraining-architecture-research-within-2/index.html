Lambert expects pretraining research to be automated in two to three years

His forecast covers architecture and data selection for current models. He expects coding agents to make experimentation cheaper and good research ideas more valuable, extending his September argument about the limits of self-improvement.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 October 2026 1238 words · ~6 min

Nathan Lambert now expects AI to automate research on model architecture and data selection within two to three years, at least for pretraining, the initial training of today’s models. In the public portion of his October 9 Interconnects essay, “I expect rapid progress but not towards general superintelligence”, he gives a timetable to the engineering progress behind his September argument that self-improvement will lower AI’s costs faster than it expands peak capability. He predicts that agents will outperform people at engineering distributed GPU systems within a few years, making experiments with model designs much easier while leaving the models’ fundamental nature largely unchanged.

Lambert wrote the essay after a week in San Francisco. He begins with a prediction he has repeatedly heard from leading industry researchers: AI will soon surpass them at their own jobs. He had doubted this without being sure why; he now attributes their confidence to the accelerating infrastructure and engineering skills they see in models. On that narrower expectation, he agrees. In his accompanying posts on X and Bluesky, he says he is more persuaded that AI will become much more efficient and widely used than that breakthroughs will eliminate language models’ known weaknesses.

He links Ilya Sutskever’s November 2025 interview with Dwarkesh Patel, in which Sutskever described a return to research after years dominated by scaling, now with much larger computers. Lambert argues that coding agents will increase the relative value of good ideas as software execution becomes easier. Leading researchers are currently judged by their ability to implement and scale ideas through complex infrastructure; he expects engineering to constrain them less, recalling the balance before deep learning took off. He calls the near-term process “parallelized, AI-assisted language modeling.” Much of the progress he anticipates comes from spending more inference compute through existing tools.

Lambert gives concrete measures agents can optimize: training throughput in tokens per second per GPU, and serving efficiency in tokens per prompt, computing operations per token or cost per answer. Established sub-problems and architectural trade-offs give agents places to look for improvements. He expects them to optimize training and inference from end to end within a few years, bringing inference closer to hardware limits. Companies have already cut roughly 10 to 30 percent from serving costs after announcing a model’s price, he says. Compounding gains could make equivalent intelligence become cheaper at a near-exponential rate, possibly faster than recent trends. Later, he expects designing accelerators and models together to add further orders of magnitude in efficiency; until then, flexible GPUs allow exploration of different architectures. Google DeepMind supplied an earlier example in May 2025: its coding agent AlphaEvolve found a faster matrix-multiplication routine for Gemini, reducing overall training time by 1 percent.

Lambert had sketched the move from engineering automation to automated model research in March’s “Lossy self-improvement”, describing agents progressing from low-level GPU code to architecture decisions. October adds the specific timetable. In March he also recalled that excitement about automated machine learning during his PhD had never changed his own job. Existing projects illustrate the work he now expects to spread. Andrej Karpathy’s autoresearch lets an agent edit a small GPT training script, test each change through a five-minute training run and retain improvements on a fixed validation measure. Weixian Xu and colleagues’ “Neural Architecture Discovery via Autonomous Evolution”, accepted at COLM 2026, reports 105 state-of-the-art designs from 1,773 autonomous experiments with linear attention, a less computationally costly alternative to standard Transformer attention. Earlier results were uneven: Bingchen Zhao and colleagues’ June 2025 arXiv paper “The Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark: Reproducing NanoGPT Improvements” found agents struggling to reproduce known training speed-ups even with detailed hints.

Lambert’s forecast remains specific to architecture and data selection for the current class of models. In his April 2025 response to AI 2027, he had argued that working with data would be difficult for AI: research depended on examining datasets, developing intuitions and trying experiments whose promise could not be reduced to a narrow score. The October timetable puts a more definite expectation on one part of that work.

For deployment, Lambert applies his earlier Jevons argument to agents: lower costs should increase demand as developers find better ways to offer them to different users. He cites Meta’s Muse as an early example. Meta introduced Muse on September 8 as a personal agent that can send email, book travel and make purchases from a dedicated cloud virtual machine. Lambert sees value in understanding how to deliver agents, without requiring a further advance in maximum model performance.

In biology and chemistry, he expects models to connect findings across small scientific communities that rarely interact. He judges models already exceptionally good at searching literature and relating these scattered findings. He leaves open whether the results amount to a new era of discovery, for example cures for most cancers, or faster progress along science’s existing course. A relevant precedent is Jamshid Sourati and James Evans’s 2023 Nature Human Behaviour paper, “Accelerating science with human-aware artificial intelligence”: their models predicted future discoveries more successfully when they represented the distribution of scientists’ expertise, especially where the literature was sparse.

Lambert also sees scope to improve reinforcement-learning environments, the settings and scored practice tasks labs buy to train models. He says more new RL-data companies than he expected have reached $100 million or $1 billion in revenue, yet researchers regularly complain about poor material. Leading labs still find the purchases worthwhile, and he considers the quality defects fixable. His September warning concerned a separate problem: devising and testing future environments difficult enough to challenge stronger models. Improving today’s commercial supply would leave that problem open. Interviewees in Epoch AI’s January FAQ on RL environments identified resistance to reward hacking, where models exploit the scoring system, as a major quality requirement.

The Anthropic Institute puts more weight on progress through execution in “When AI builds itself”. It argues that advances usually come through repeated cycles of scaling systems, finding failures and fixing them. Claude can handle much of that work, allowing humans to direct more research; the institute expects acceleration to compound even if Claude never acquires good judgment about which ideas to pursue. The distinction also appears in Peter Kirgis, Sayash Kapoor and colleagues’ July arXiv paper, “Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies”. Agents completed the engineering for two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 research questions without human help but made no substantial progress on the questions themselves. Epoch AI’s October 7 InnovationEval report likewise found that neither tested agent came close to matching the human-developed SDPO training method. Those tests concern open-ended research and novel post-training methods, distinct from Lambert’s forecast for pretraining architecture and data selection.

Replies to Lambert’s Bluesky post challenged his expectations for both adoption and capability. Dean Lee argued that cheaper tokens do little for workflows where costly errors require human checking, because the checker still costs the same. The account MMS argued that efficiency gains often improve capabilities, and cited predictions about persistent hallucinations and limited time horizons as examples it believed progress had overtaken. Lambert has also announced plans to investigate these questions through Trillium Labs, the open-science nonprofit he co-founded with Tom Zick and leads as executive director. In his October 2 announcement, he said the lab would expand into open infrastructure for studying recursive self-improvement, reward hacking and systems of multiple agents.
