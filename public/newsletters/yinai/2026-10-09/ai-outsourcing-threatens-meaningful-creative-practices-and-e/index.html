Jordan MacKenzie on AI and the value of how things are made

Handing writing and acts of care to AI could leave fewer things to value for their making, MacKenzie argues in Philosophers’ Imprint; it also shows people who value the making that others often never did.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 October 2026 1433 words · ~7 min

In “Fear and Loathing in Silicon Valley: Does AI Threaten our Valuing Practices?”, published October 5 in the open-access journal Philosophers’ Imprint, Jordan MacKenzie starts with a gifted storyteller she calls Marshall. After five years of writing advertorials, he now prompts ChatGPT for pieces such as a listicle about Samsonite luggage and copy-edits what comes back. The job is easier, and he finds it worse: “AI makes him anxious, and even a bit disgusted.” MacKenzie, an assistant professor of philosophy at the University of Virginia, asks what justifies such fear and loathing when AI takes over creative work or work that expresses care. Generative AI, she answers, threatens how people value things in two ways, one looking forward and one looking back.

Following Samuel Scheffler, she treats valuing as a cluster of attitudes: to value something is typically to judge it valuable, care about it and see it as a source of reasons. She illustrates two patterns of valuing with a love letter to her spouse written by ChatGPT. Even if that letter were better than one she could write, it would fail, because “the method of its production disqualifies it from being a love letter.” Qualitative valuing prizes an object’s features and so treats the object as exchangeable; it can be aspirational, always seeking better, or satisficing, content with good enough. Process valuing prizes how something came into being and entered one’s life, and so makes it hard to replace. Kelly Weirich, whom MacKenzie cites, made a similar point in a 2023 Prindle Post essay on “emotional outsourcing”: “Putting words to your love is itself an act of love.”

Process valuing need not involve creativity. MacKenzie treasures a 1960s starburst clock that hung in her aunt’s living room for more than fifty years, until the aunt took it down and gave it to her the moment MacKenzie said how much she loved it. She notes that “a lot of our care-based labor isn’t particularly creative,” from rereading bedtime stories to helping friends assemble Ikea furniture. Such valuing “generally tracks attributions of agency,” she writes, and connects the valuer to the people involved. She presents process valuing as an account of how objects acquire what G. A. Cohen called personal value, the kind that kept Cohen from replacing his old eraser with a newer model.

Looking forward, MacKenzie doubts that people simply envy a superior machine, since Marshall finds ChatGPT’s listicles generic and dull. His anxiety fits better with a fear that AI makes things worse, pushing people from aspirational valuing to satisficing. Automation trades one set of qualities for another and, being cheaper, takes over the market: machine-knit sweaters left buyers with pretty, disposable sweaters and no durable ones, and “the good is transformed into the good enough.” She treats this shift as real, though familiar from earlier automation and perhaps temporary, since AI could improve.

She considers the threat to process valuing more serious. ChatGPT’s products, she writes, are interchangeable: the same prompt run twenty times gives results that barely differ. They are also only loosely tied to anyone’s agency. Arcimboldo’s Vertumnus, an emperor’s portrait built from vegetables, makes her wonder how a Renaissance artist came up with it; Shrimp Jesus, an image of Jesus made of shrimp that took a few seconds of prompting in Midjourney, invites no such questions. As such products multiply, people have fewer chances to value anything for how it was made. In a footnote she calls this “value disappearance,” distinct from C. Thi Nguyen’s “value capture,” in which simplified measures such as Fitbit step counts displace richer values.

AI can preserve agency, she grants, as when artists build it into their practice, and can even extend it for people who cannot paint. She writes that “the worry is not that we can’t use AI agentially, but that we won’t.” The shift from AI that supplements a person’s agency to AI that supplants it can happen gradually and unnoticed, and noticing it may depend on expertise acquired before generative AI: a professional philosopher may keep her voice while using AI, but a typical PHIL 101 student may not. If valuing a process requires some understanding of it, the loss goes deeper. Someone who always has AI write heartfelt messages, she argues, crediting Daniel Hoek, never learns how hard such words are to find, and so cannot fully value a friend’s effort to find them, much as someone who knows nothing of woodworking cannot imagine how an inlay was made.

She traces the loathing to the backward-looking threat. When Marshall began producing advertorials with ChatGPT, their quality fell, and nobody but Marshall cared. She calls ChatGPT a “terrible black mirror” for Marshall: it showed him that others had valued his work only for its function, and barely for that. AI, she argues, can reveal “value discord,” the places where people’s valuing practices had already diverged. Of a student who has ChatGPT write his paper, she observes, “I learn something about how he sees his work, my course, and perhaps even me”; a friend’s AI-written heartfelt email shows that the friend values the correspondence but not enough to labor over it. Brian Porter and Edouard Machery’s 2024 finding in Scientific Reports that non-experts prefer AI poems, she suggests, could leave a poet wondering what years of searching for the right phrase were for.

Discovering such discord can turn the loathing inward, at oneself for assuming others cared, or outward, at people unwilling even to try to share one’s values. MacKenzie concedes that people have always disagreed about what to value: “One person’s meaningful process is another person’s monotonous drudgery.” She gives two reasons the discord still matters. Shared valuing serves creators’ interests: people who value work aspirationally give its makers time to do it well, and people who value its making see the makers as hard to replace. More deeply, people want others to value them as they value themselves, and self-conceptions are bound up with activities people find meaningful. Marshall sees himself as a writer; his boss sees a prompt engineer.

She closes with earlier resistance to mechanization: craft potters opposed slip casting, 19th-century painters lamented the daguerreotype, and the Luddites broke machines. A ceramics factory, she notes, can only make ceramics, while AI can write papers, love letters and advertorials. Values, she writes, can feel “under siege from all directions.”

MacKenzie builds most directly on Lindsay Brainard of the University of Alabama at Birmingham, whom she cites throughout and thanks. In “Artificial Intelligence and the Threat of Creative Obsolescence,” published in Ergo, Brainard argues that people may gradually abandon creative pursuits because AI seems to supply what creativity offers, though AI appears unable to replicate the imagination, originality and connectedness of human creativity. MacKenzie takes Brainard’s account of creativity, extends the worry to care that involves no creativity and adds the backward-looking threat. The paper does not cite John Danaher and Sven Nyholm’s 2020 argument that automation opens “achievement gaps” in meaningful work, or Shannon Vallor’s 2014 warning that information technologies can morally deskill their users, a worry close to her point about the PHIL 101 student.

Porter and Machery, of the University of Pittsburgh, also found that readers rated a poem lower when told AI wrote it, a result MacKenzie does not discuss. In six preregistered studies published after her paper was accepted, Scott Claessens, Pierce Veitch and Jim Everett of the University of Kent found that people who use AI to write love letters, apologies or wedding vows are judged less caring, less authentic, less trustworthy and lazier, even when they disclose it, while outsourcing code or recipes draws much less criticism. Those results fit MacKenzie’s account of process valuing for messages of care, and suggest the discord she describes may be greatest for functional writing like Marshall’s advertorials.

The argument found readers before publication. A preprint subtitled “How AI Threatens Human Valuing” has been on PhilPapers since October 2025, and Erich Hatala Matthes assigned it in his draft spring 2026 philosophy of art syllabus at Wellesley College. In September a self-described lecturer posting on Bluesky as noam wrote that “LLMs have revealed that there was an illusory consensus about academic norms,” because people disagree about which parts of academic work are drudgery; A. G. Holdier replied with the preprint, saying its last section “gets into this kind of thing.” Alex Chalmers describes the opposite resentment in his Cosmos Institute essay “You Can’t Speedrun Yourself”: as machines take over more of a practice’s visible output, he argues, standards that still demand real effort come to be resented.
