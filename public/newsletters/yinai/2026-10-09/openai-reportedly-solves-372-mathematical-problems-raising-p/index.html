Scott Aaronson’s case for understanding AI mathematics

Moshkovitz’s struggle to read the Unique Games proof illustrates Aaronson’s argument: mathematicians need to understand and extend AI results, while deciding how to work with the companies producing them.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 October 2026 1265 words · ~6 min

Scott Aaronson of UT Austin opens “The Mathocalypse”, posted on Shtetl-Optimized on October 7 and updated October 9, with his nine-year-old son taunting his wife, complexity theorist Dana Moshkovitz, that a robot had solved a problem she had pursued throughout her career. OpenAI’s claimed proof of Subhash Khot’s Unique Games Conjecture was among the 372 result families in its October 6 release. Aaronson considers that day historically momentous. A Lean formalization accompanies the claim, but he writes that almost none of the released proofs had yet been understood by humans. His essay asks how mathematicians can preserve that understanding and build on the results.

Khot proposed the conjecture in 2002. It concerns constraints pairing variables so each value of one permits exactly one value of the other. Even when almost all constraints can be satisfied, finding an assignment satisfying a small fraction would be NP-hard: solving it efficiently would efficiently solve all problems whose proposed answers can be checked quickly. Prasad Raghavendra showed in 2008 that the conjecture, assuming P differs from NP, makes semidefinite programming, a standard optimization method, optimal for approximating each fixed finite constraint-satisfaction problem. Many theorists doubted the conjecture; Moshkovitz, Aaronson writes, never did.

OpenAI’s 58-page manuscript, The Unique Games Theorem, builds on a 2018 theorem by Khot, Minzer and Safra that established hardness when about half the constraints can be satisfied. That work, Erica Klarreich reported in Quanta, drew on an idea from Moshkovitz. The manuscript introduces nonlinear random perturbations into a consistency test: valid answers almost always survive, while the proof can still detect inconsistency. Its bibliography cites no Moshkovitz paper, although OpenAI’s reasoning summary shows the model considering approaches she developed. The prompt sought hardness results without assuming the conjecture; the model pursued a proof of the conjecture along the way.

Aaronson reproduces Moshkovitz’s texts from the release night: “It feels like something written by someone who’s on psychedelics.” She found the citations confusing and the argument impossible to read without AI help. Astra assembled guarantees for the proof’s random perturbations from claims scattered through the paper. She described an unfamiliar recursive encoding, unlike the established long and short codes, while wondering whether a more natural half-space-code proof remained possible. In the comments, Aaronson, who had worked at OpenAI, says Moshkovitz had long avoided AI. She spent October 7 questioning Astra; early on October 8 he reported that she mostly understood the proof and wanted to give talks.

Aaronson identifies about a dozen other results he intends to study, some touching his own work. OpenAI’s A Nearly Quartic Separation Between Randomized and Quantum Query Complexity claims to refute a conjecture from a 2021 paper he co-wrote. Some problems, it says, require randomized algorithms to make almost the fourth power of the input queries needed by quantum algorithms, exceeding the conjectured cubic bound. Aaronson has followed this question since 1998. Another claims a positive answer to the unitary synthesis problem he and Greg Kuperberg posed in 2007: whether access to a suitable classical function lets a quantum computer efficiently implement any quantum operation. OpenAI’s catalogue specifies a version allowing constant approximation error and gives no efficient classical procedure for constructing that function. Aaronson says he and most others expected a negative answer. Dakshita Khurana of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, who spent three years seeking a positive one, recognizes ingredients she had tried in the AI’s argument.

Aaronson contrasts OpenAI’s release with Anthropic’s handling of the algorithm behind Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams’ October 5 arXiv paper, “Truly Subquadratic 3SUM and Truly Subcubic APSP via Triangles in Sparse Lopsided Graphs.” It improves algorithms for finding three numbers summing to zero and shortest paths between every pair of graph vertices. The Columbia and MIT authors say Claude found the algorithm without human input while tasked with checking cryptographic constructions. Anthropic shared it confidentially and offered compensation; the authors simplified and extended it. OpenAI’s approach starts a public competition to explain difficult proofs, work he fears could be thankless, poorly credited or joyless. Anthropic’s gives a company control over which mathematicians first receive and explain its discoveries. Aaronson leaves the choice open.

Some details Aaronson passes on are secondhand. He says the results came from OpenAI’s latest internal model, unlike the approximately 10,000-agent system behind its September 8 Navier-Stokes announcement. He suggests release to paying ChatGPT users within a couple of months, subject to the safety board; OpenAI’s announcement supplies no date. His estimate of three hours of Pro-level compute per result matches the README, but his roughly 8,000 problems tried and 5 percent solved are unconfirmed: the README says approximately 4,000 problems and gives no success rate. The advisory group consulted by OpenAI had requested disclosure of failed attempts.

Jordana Cepelewicz’s October 5 Quanta essay compares receiving an AI proof to arriving on a fogbound summit without learning the route. Aaronson thinks mathematicians can reconstruct individual arguments with AI assistance; sustaining a community motivated to do so will be harder. Moshkovitz disputes the metaphor in her own comment: AI built on existing communities, frameworks and results, and even major advances leave further mathematics to do.

In an October 7 update, Aaronson notes the absence of cryptographic breakthroughs, which Ethereum researcher Justin Drake had highlighted that morning. Aaronson says unnamed sources told him AI companies had begun cautiously testing whether internal models could break important cryptographic protocols. He argues for anticipating any such capability before it becomes widely available; he does not report that a protocol has been broken.

The October 9 update connects understanding to eventual applications. Aaronson knows of no direct practical implications of the released results and does not expect the tiny asymptotic Fourier-transform improvement to be deployed soon. But mathematical ideas often become useful through later work. He expects researchers who understand these arguments to find applications, including practical ones, and wants humans to retain that role.

He also answers the Association for Human Mathematics, which lists 809 members committed to avoiding work for commercial AI companies and publication of AI-generated mathematics. Its October 7 statement says, “Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done.” It accuses OpenAI of disregarding the advisory group’s request to stop testing advanced problems on proprietary models, invokes research norms serving the public interest, and urges mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI. Zvi Mowshowitz’s roundup dismisses the association as unrepresentative while agreeing that OpenAI ignored that request.

Aaronson notes the ridicule directed at the association’s sentence and denies that its members speak for all mathematicians. He supports holding AI companies accountable, especially over the pace of development, alignment and catastrophic risk. He rejects protecting mathematicians’ enjoyment or careers as grounds for stopping AI solutions; that is his characterization of the association’s position. He argues that it lacks a public-interest justification, conflicts with anyone’s freedom to attempt open problems, and becomes unenforceable as capabilities reach subscribers. The advisory group’s statement, which he reproduces, neither endorses nor condemns OpenAI’s conduct. It stresses that mathematicians must be able to pursue their own questions, with equitable access to powerful tools and computing resources.

One result Aaronson still links, concerning the Hodge conjecture for products of K3 surfaces, appears among the withdrawals recorded in OpenAI’s October 7 update. Told of the sign error in the comments, he expects most results to survive but anticipates further retractions, as with human papers. Explanations are arriving too: on October 9 Ariel Elboim shared an 11-page proof and 17-page presentation of the Unique Games argument, prepared with Codex and Claude. Elboim says neither has yet been reviewed by a human expert.
