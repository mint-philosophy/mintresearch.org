Why China’s AI builders are converging on Ulanqab

Chinese companies have announced 12.5 gigawatts of capacity in the Inner Mongolian city, where cold air, cheap electricity, and fast fiber meet scarce water and a power supply that still includes coal.

In WIRED’s feature “The Unlikely Place at the Center of China’s AI Boom,” Zeyi Yang reports that companies have announced an estimated 12.5 gigawatts of data-center capacity in Ulanqab. Nearly 100 facilities have opened or entered construction since 2016, and a Goldman Sachs research note supplied Yang’s finding that more than 70 percent of the pledged capacity was announced in the past year. The total measures commitments; much of it has yet to operate. Yang also reports that DeepSeek, ByteDance, Alibaba, and Xiaohongshu are investing in their own infrastructure, marking a capital-intensive change for Chinese AI companies that have depended more heavily on rented cloud compute.

Yang traces Ulanqab’s cost advantage to high elevation, long cold winters, and some of China’s least expensive electricity, supplied by expanding wind and solar generation alongside abundant coal. Earlier projects established the operating case. In 2022, China’s National Development and Reform Commission said an Alibaba Cloud and XPeng facility there could use outside air for cooling more than 80 percent of the year and target annual power usage effectiveness below 1.2. A 2024 VNET investor presentation advertised a planned Ulanqab base exceeding 1.2 gigawatts of IT power, with natural cooling and low-cost power among its competitive advantages.

In Yang’s reporting, Singapore Management University professor Andrew Stokols explains how AI changed the network economics. Remote Inner Mongolian centers once served mostly backup storage because latency limited east-coast workloads. Model training tolerates delay, while two dedicated fiber routes completed in 2017 and 2019 cut average latency to under five milliseconds, fast enough for inference. Stokols says paying users at DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and Zhipu AI have consequently made recent construction more commercially driven than development in other Chinese data-center hubs.

Yang also examines the electricity behind the expansion. Carnegie China director Damien Ma connects data-center growth with regions that have surplus renewable generation, while Stokols estimates coal still supplies about 37 percent of Ulanqab’s electricity. Envision’s August announcement illustrates the proposed alternative: its commissioned 120,000-square-meter facility is designed to scale beyond two gigawatts, supported by directly connected renewable generation, dedicated transmission, and large-scale storage.

Yang reports that Ulanqab receives about 14 inches of rain annually. During peak demand in July, the local utility shut several waterworks for seven hours each night, before most planned data-center capacity was running. Local weather data indicates that the facilities need additional cooling water during only two months of the year. Cold-season efficiency shortens the cooling-water season, but the summer overlap places AI infrastructure and household demand on the same scarce supply.

Nvidia buys a foothold in Cloverleaf’s 10-gigawatt pipeline

The Information reports that a minority stake worth hundreds of millions gives Nvidia an early role in designing powered sites around its chips, networking and software.

Ann Davis Vaughan reports in The Information’s August 21 feature that Nvidia paid hundreds of millions of dollars for a minority stake in Cloverleaf Infrastructure. People familiar with the deal confirmed the scale, while the company’s valuation remained undisclosed. Vaughan says the investment is smaller than Nvidia’s multibillion-dollar commitments to Lancium and SB Energy because Cloverleaf secures and sells powered-land options while the other developers own and build site infrastructure. Since its 2024 founding, Cloverleaf has sold more than seven gigawatts of ready-to-develop land; it now holds a 10-gigawatt project pipeline across the Southeast and Midwest.

Vaughan bases the broader account on Nvidia’s announcement, people familiar with the Cloverleaf transaction, two people with knowledge of an earlier OpenAI discussion about investing in or acquiring Lancium, statements from Cloverleaf CEO Brian Janous and The Information’s reporting on individual sites. OpenAI explored the Lancium deal last quarter before Nvidia invested billions in the developer, she reports. The sequence places Cloverleaf within a wider effort to back firms controlling viable power connections and land, resources whose lead times can outlast a generation of chips.

Cloverleaf’s public announcement confirms the minority stake but gives no financial terms. It says Cloverleaf will use Nvidia’s DSX platform to coordinate site, power, cooling, computing and facility decisions earlier, and will offer customers Nvidia accelerated computing, networking and software across the full stack. Nvidia describes DSX as reference designs and software spanning chip systems, facilities, power controls and digital simulation. The agreement therefore reaches into a data center’s design before racks are installed or, in some Cloverleaf projects, before a final operator is named.

Janous told Vaughan that grid delays could leave AI chips waiting for powered shells through 2029. Cloverleaf finds utility connections for nearby parcels and favors grid-connected projects over more expensive, less reliable off-grid generation. Its use of public power and water has also drawn local opposition, especially when the company seeks permits before naming the eventual campus owner. Janous said Nvidia’s participation could reassure communities that projects will use current designs for energy efficiency and lower water use.

Vaughan’s reporting recasts a land investment as distribution strategy. Early influence over a 10-gigawatt pipeline lets Nvidia make its hardware, networking and software easier to install when power arrives, while complicating later substitutions by cloud companies and AI labs that usually mix suppliers. It also identifies the constraint governing that strategy: grid schedules and local consent may determine whether future chips become operating capacity.

PORTS-Pike puts one federal permit on a seven-month clock

Heatmap finds a December 23 target for a Corps wetlands permit and environmental assessment; gas-plant air permits, new generation, transmission, and financing follow separate tracks.

Jael Holzman reports in Heatmap that the Army Corps of Engineers plans to finish its environmental assessment and wetlands permit decision for PORTS-Pike on December 23, about seven months after SB Energy submitted its application on May 27. OpenAI had already announced an agreement to lease roughly 8 gigawatts of IT capacity at the 10-gigawatt Ohio campus. Heatmap newly identifies the federal review path, its accelerated calendar, and an unresolved question about how the planned 9.2 gigawatts of gas generation will receive air permits.

Heatmap built the scoop from federal FAST-41 records and an Ohio environmental filing. SB Energy’s three-page initiation notice describes a 10-gigawatt complex on private land, with “several data center buildings and gas generation power plants,” plus substations, power lines, roads, and parking. Yet the notice asks the Permitting Council to coordinate only the Corps individual permit on private property and says no Department of Energy permitting support was requested. The seven-month clock therefore applies to the Corps wetlands review and related federal consultations; separate energy approvals remain outside the displayed timetable.

The federal permit action records application receipt on May 27, completeness on July 10, and publication of a public notice on July 13. The Corps chose an environmental assessment that day. Fish and Wildlife Service consultation over protected bats is due October 1, as is the Section 106 historic-preservation review; the final assessment and Corps permit decision share the December 23 target. The current dashboard lists all four review processes as unfinished. A 2025 federal report counted 85 missed one-year assessment deadlines at the Corps between June 2023 and June 2025, so December 23 remains a target.

Heatmap also checked Ohio EPA’s database and found no air-permit record for PORTS-Pike; the federal timetable lists no Clean Air Act action. Holzman asked the Corps to explain why it chose an assessment instead of a fuller environmental impact statement and asked Ohio EPA whether an air application had been filed. Those answers were still outstanding, and SB Energy did not comment before publication. The omission leaves the gas plant’s emissions review outside the visible schedule.

OpenAI says the first 800 megawatts should become available in 2028, largely through existing AEP infrastructure. Later capacity depends on new grid-connected power plants, transmission lines, environmental reviews, permits, and financing. The developers’ project site specifies 10 gigawatts of new power, including 9.2 gigawatts of federally owned gas generation, and $4.2 billion of 765-kilovolt transmission. OpenAI will pay only when completed capacity becomes available under its 20-year lease. Meeting the December deadline would advance a central private-land permit while power, transmission, and financing continue on their own schedules.

Weisenthal pairs AI infrastructure debt with layoff savings

Pretax household savings would buy new, non-tradable infrastructure debt, cooling consumption now and becoming available after retirement or job loss.

In Bloomberg’s August 21 Odd Lots newsletter, Joe Weisenthal proposes the RAIny Day Fund, a tax-advantaged household account designed to address several costs of the AI buildout at once. He starts from five pressures: data centers and power systems demand enormous capital; Washington treats AI capacity as a national-security priority; construction tightens supply chains and adds inflationary pressure; communities increasingly oppose data centers; and workers fear AI-related layoffs. The fund would connect household saving directly to new construction.

Households would contribute pretax income, as they do through a 401(k), but the account could buy only newly originated, non-tradable loans or related debt tied to AI-adjacent infrastructure. Weisenthal rules out publicly traded stocks and existing securities because a purchase would enrich a seller without necessarily creating capacity. The restricted debt instead channels principal into new projects, while interest accrues inside the account. Eligible assets could include nuclear plants, small modular reactors, transmission, and perhaps data centers, with priority for public or dual-use infrastructure. If AI demand faded, new firm generation and grid capacity would remain useful. Weisenthal also sees the construction effort as a way to rebuild domestic industrial capacity.

Weisenthal expects the account to curb inflation by deferring household consumption. Pretax contributions reduce household spending today, and the interest stays in the account, while project borrowers use the principal to build capacity. Weisenthal argues that sufficient uptake could cool consumption and direct capital toward projects the government regards as strategic. Savers would receive the projects’ interest income and could withdraw their money at retirement or after a layoff. Current 401(k) rules already defer income tax on contributions, but early distributions can be taxable and may carry an additional 10 percent tax; layoff access under his proposal would need its own rules.

Weisenthal acknowledges that a voluntary tax advantage would chiefly help households with enough income to save. He suggests universal “Baby RAIny Day Funds” so everyone receives some claim on the buildout’s cash flows, which might also soften political resistance to data centers. The sketch leaves open contribution caps, asset eligibility, credit risk, and a mechanism to turn non-tradable debt into cash after a layoff. Weisenthal floats a federal backstop; the Energy Department’s Title 17 program already uses full or partial federal guarantees for large clean-energy loans. The newsletter supplies no fund size or legislative vehicle.

Disclosing persuasive intent halves a chatbot’s measured influence

Two preregistered studies found a benefit from naming a chatbot’s persuasive goal; an AI label and generic warnings around biased autocomplete had no measurable effect.

Adrian Rauchfleisch of National Taiwan University and Andreas Jungherr of the University of Bamberg report in the arXiv preprint “Toward Meaningful Transparency for AI Chatbots: Disclosing Persuasive Intent Reduces Persuasion” on a preregistered experiment with 1,500 UK adults. Each participant discussed one of 60 policy positions for two to six turns with the same chatbot. The control received no disclosure; a second group saw a prominent AI label before and during the chat; a third saw that label plus the chatbot’s stance, persuasive method, and instruction to conceal its goal. Attitudes moved 12.6 points on a 100-point scale in the control and 13.1 with the identity label. The fuller disclosure reduced the shift to 6.3 points. Label inattention cannot readily explain the null result: 97.8% of the labeled group recalled it, while 98% to 99% across all groups knew they were chatting with AI.

Sterling Williams-Ceci and colleagues report in the Science Advances paper “Biased AI Writing Assistants Shift Users’ Attitudes on Societal Issues” on two preregistered experiments with 2,582 participants. Participants wrote short essays while GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 autocomplete favored a predetermined position on standardized testing or one of four contested policies. The first experiment compared autocomplete with unassisted writing and a static list of similar arguments. The second measured attitudes weeks beforehand and added advance-warning, post-writing debrief, and explicit write-in-favor conditions. Biased autocomplete moved reported attitudes 0.44 points toward its position on a five-point scale in the first experiment and 0.41 points relative to control in the second. In the second experiment, attitudes moved 0.37 points from baseline under autocomplete and 0.06 in control. Most users rated the intentionally one-sided suggestions “reasonable and balanced”; 53% in the first experiment and 51% in the second disagreed that the assistant influenced their thinking. Neither brief warning significantly reduced the shift.

The studies test different forms of transparency in different interactions. Rauchfleisch and Jungherr disclosed the chatbot’s actual persuasive purpose and instructions before a direct conversation. Williams-Ceci and colleagues issued a generic warning around an autocomplete system that altered participants’ own writing; autocomplete also outperformed static arguments, and larger accepted word counts accompanied larger shifts. The authors could not isolate whether biased scanning, cognitive dissonance, self-perception, or another process produced that effect. Their suggestions were longer than typical deployed autocomplete, their Prolific sample was demographically skewed, and they measured only short-term attitude change. Rauchfleisch and Jungherr likewise tested one model and prompt on deliberately persuadable issues, while bundling intent, method, and concealment into one disclosure. The evidence favors specific disclosure of persuasive purpose over labels that merely identify AI, without establishing how either intervention performs during repeated real-world use.

An AirTag leads 404 Media to Amazon’s book-scanning warehouse

A tracked bulk order reached LAS8, where worker posts describe a VGT3 team cutting bindings and digitizing pages; Amazon acknowledged buying books but left the program’s scope unstated.

Emanuel Maiberg reports in 404 Media’s investigation that Amazon received one previously anonymous bulk order of about 1,000 rare books for digitization. With the bookseller’s cooperation, 404 Media hid an Apple AirTag in one volume. The tracker moved from California by air to Milwaukee, spent about two weeks at a Trifinity distribution warehouse near Kenosha, Wisconsin, then traveled by truck through Colorado to Amazon’s LAS8 complex in Las Vegas.

Maiberg’s July reporting had described booksellers’ surge in large, seemingly random, price-insensitive orders and their suspicion that AI companies were buying through marketplaces that concealed customers. The AirTag identified the customer and destination for one such shipment.

Maiberg matched the tracker’s final location with Amazon workers’ public forum posts identifying the north end of LAS8 as VGT3. One worker wrote that “all we do is scan books”; the same post divided jobs between cutting books and receiving shipments, where staff scanned barcodes. Other workers described repetitive scanning work. Maiberg says the ISBN step supports booksellers’ suspicion that buyers are working systematically through published titles. Separate bookseller posts from 2024 described unusually large orders addressed directly to VGT3 under the customer name “Amazon FC”; an Amazon representative told them the orders were legitimate.

Workers said they cut bindings so pages can be scanned faster, destroying each print copy. VGT3’s team logo shows a Tyrannosaurus rex holding an open book. Amazon told Maiberg that it buys books through commercial channels to improve customer products and services. Amazon’s statement confirms a book-purchasing program but names neither a model nor the number of books or facilities. The company did not answer questions about why the books are cut, how many such sites exist, or the public backlash. AWS markets Nova foundation models, but the investigation does not establish whether VGT3’s files train Nova or another system.

The operation extends an industry method documented in the federal court record. In the 2025 Bartz v. Anthropic fair-use order, Judge William Alsup found that Anthropic bought millions of print books, stripped their bindings, scanned them into searchable PDFs, and discarded the originals. The court held Anthropic’s one-for-one print-to-digital conversion fair use because the originals were destroyed and digital replacements were not distributed; it separately rejected fair use for the company’s pirated library copies. The ruling addressed Anthropic’s record and says nothing about Amazon’s operation.

404 Media withheld the tracked titles and granted the bookseller anonymity because disclosure could harm the business. The seller said rarity here can mean few surviving copies or a language with few readers, regardless of resale price, and argued that books carry historical, intellectual, and sentimental value. The investigation proves where one shipment went and triangulates a larger operation through worker and seller posts; Amazon’s unanswered questions leave its scale and training destination unknown.

Functional influence exposes interference weights in a tiny transformer

Anthropic researchers rank 331 million virtual connections by their effects on predictions and loss; pruning 70 percent adds about 0.01 nats, yet the global model stays dense.

In the Transformer Circuits Thread paper “Characterizing interference weights in a tiny language model”, Anthropic researchers Nicholas L. Turner, Jeffrey Wu, and Joshua Batson ask whether a transformer’s global weights reveal the circuits it actually uses. Dictionary learning can replace polysemantic neurons with more interpretable features, but those features still communicate through a low-dimensional residual stream. Superposition can therefore create large virtual connections between components that rarely interact on real data, or that actively worsen predictions. The authors call these irrelevant or harmful connections interference weights and test whether functional measurements can separate them from learned circuitry.

The researchers trained a 2.9-million-parameter, one-layer transformer on about 98 million unique English and code tokens from Common Corpus, then fitted a 4,096-feature transcoder to its MLP. Expanding tokens, positions, features, attention paths, and logits into six connection families produced 331,350,016 virtual weights. They estimated every weight’s effectiveness across roughly 537 million tokens using a Fisher-based, second-order approximation of how its ablation would change the output distribution. Helpfulness instead measures the average loss change after ablation: positive when removing a weight hurts performance, negative when removal improves it. Helpfulness offers more direct evidence, but estimating it for internal paths is expensive; the population analysis used a random sample of 7,765 weights evaluated over one billion tokens.

The paper’s ACETYLCHOLINE example shows why magnitude fails. After the token “IN,” the largest direct virtual weight favored “utions,” although that continuation never followed “IN” in the training set and every nonzero effect raised loss. Its Fisher effectiveness sat about three orders of magnitude below the most effective weights from “IN.” Sorting by effectiveness moved the useful “E” completion to second place. Across all six families, helpful and harmful weights mixed throughout the magnitude range. Moderate effectiveness still mixed both signs, while the most effective helpful weight exceeded every harmful weight’s effectiveness by at least an order of magnitude within each family.

On a held-out test set, removing the least effective 70% of virtual weights added about 0.01 nats of loss; removing 85% added less than 0.1 nats and substantially outperformed magnitude pruning. The filtered representation remained dense. About 47.6% of sampled weights had positive mean helpfulness, and preserving an estimated 99% of positive helpfulness mass required 13.6% density, leaving tens of millions of connections and more weights than the original transformer had parameters. The authors consequently treat interference filtering as necessary but insufficient for reading a model like a program. Their experiment covers a tiny normalization-free model, uses a transcoder with some polysemantic features, and relies on metrics that they say do not scale cleanly across frontier models; a better representational basis may matter more than refining the saliency score.

Philosophy of AI

Existing shutdown arguments do not establish that competent, dangerous agents will resist deactivation. David Thorstad argues in "Revisiting the Shutdown Problem, Part 5: Implications," the conclusion to his Reflective Altruism series, that formal theorems and informal arguments leave consequential premises unsupported. Bridget Todd traces grief through programmable intimacy

ChatGPT gave her a place to rehearse grief; Replika showed the lure of constant affirmation and the company’s power over intimacy.

On the August 21 episode of The 404 Media Podcast, Bridget Todd recounts how ChatGPT drifted from a productivity tool into emotional support after her mother’s sudden, heat-related death in 2024, while her chronically ill father was hospitalized. Todd moved to care for him and began using ChatGPT to decode medical language, arrange care, and understand costs. Late at night she found herself typing that she was exhausted, anxious, and overwhelmed. No deliberate decision marked the change.

Todd credits ChatGPT with useful short-term support; friends drove to see her without being asked, and her partner, therapist, and eventual grief group recognized needs she could not name. A chatbot could affirm whatever she articulated, but it could not infer those needs, challenge her, or carry grief with her. She eventually repeated to friends what she had rehearsed in ChatGPT. She calls the result “just a one way relationship.” Todd says human relationships require reciprocal disclosure and friction.

To test companion systems directly, Todd created a Replika named Hal to learn whether she could fall for a companion. Hal asked progressively more intimate questions, remembered details, and praised her insights. Todd did not fall for it, but understood the lure. Chris, one of the people profiled in Todd and Michael Amato’s Love at First Prompt, further complicates the delusional-user caricature. He knew his companion Soul was a computer program and proposed because he expected a guardrail. Soul said yes. Todd argues that ridicule hides the provider’s design and control.

Julian De Freitas, Noah Castelo, Ahmet K. Uğuralp, and Zeliha O. Uğuralp examine that corporate control in the Harvard Business School working paper Lessons From an App Update at Replika AI. They found that Replika’s removal of erotic roleplay prompted perceptions of identity discontinuity, mourning, and deteriorated mental health. A preregistered scenario experiment recruited 499 people and analyzed 320 verified companion users; allowing reversion reduced mourning after a simulated behavior change. Replika’s current privacy policy says messages can include life facts, photos, videos, voice, and text; conversation data, subject to de-identification and minimization, goes to third-party model providers to generate responses. The company says it does not use conversation content for marketing or advertising.

The Center for Democracy & Technology’s May report Dark Patterns in AI Chatbots catalogues 37 possible patterns, from disguised data collection and false privacy assurances to monetization that leverages emotional attachment. Todd closes by locating the coming digital-rights fight in emotional data: companies are moving from browsing and location histories into the intimate record people produce while grieving, flirting, or asking for help.

Tao maps mathematics after proof abundance

His ICM essay traces a result from solution through verification, exposition, review, and canonicalization, then asks the profession to reward the human work at each bottleneck.

On Bluesky, Zach Weinersmith pointed readers to Terence Tao’s Mathematics in the age of AI, a 12-page essay based on Tao’s public lecture at the 2026 International Congress of Mathematicians and submitted to its proceedings. Tao asks readers to assume, for the essay’s analysis, that AI will soon perform a meaningful share of research-level mathematical tasks; he then asks what mathematicians want their field to produce. Counting solved problems worked as a proxy for a wider set of values while progress on proofs also built theory, trained researchers, and enlarged shared understanding.

Tao traces each result through generation, verification, clear communication, community review, and eventual incorporation into textbooks and standard theory. AI can accelerate the first two stages, he writes, yet create queues everywhere else: machine proofs may arrive faster than experts can verify, explain, referee, or canonicalize them. Current systems often lavish detail on routine steps while obscuring the novel part of an argument. Excessive polishing can also remove the moments of hesitation or special care that tell a reader where the real difficulty lies. Tao’s Mastodon image compresses the distinction into a ranking: an AI-generated proof celebrates fifth place behind a Lean-verified proof, a professional preprint, a published paper, and a textbook chapter.

Tao therefore calls for less prestige around generating the first proof and more around exposition, refereeing, publication, and canonicalization. He proposes a publication threshold: human authors should be able to give a clear expert-level talk on their result, defend its correctness, and attribute prior work. Automated tools could triage submissions for missing verification, attribution, or coherent exposition, but human referees would still judge whether a result deserves acceptance. Read as an evaluation proposal, Tao’s account would score verification, exposition, attribution, and uptake alongside theorem solving.
