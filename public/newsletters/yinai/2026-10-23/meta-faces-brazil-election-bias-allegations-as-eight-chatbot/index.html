InternetLab disputes Meta’s defence in Brazil’s election controversy

Lula’s campaign challenges uneven treatment, while InternetLab rejects Meta’s use of its chatbot study as evidence of electoral-law compliance.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 23 October 2026 983 words · ~5 min

Luke Taylor reports in New Scientist that President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s campaign is contesting Meta’s treatment of advertising and AI-generated campaign material ahead of Brazil’s October 25 presidential runoff against Flávio Bolsonaro. The dispute has continued after the October 15 equal-treatment order: electoral prosecutors allege incomplete compliance, while Meta denies political interference. Taylor describes images showing Meta AI refusing campaign posters for Lula while producing them for Flávio, alongside campaign screenshots allegedly showing the chatbot assigning Bolsonaro’s electoral number, 22, to fictional politicians and a translation turning an insult about Flávio’s future imprisonment into praise for his future presidency.

Meta’s October 16 response, updated October 18, distinguishes the names its filters recognised. It says prompts naming Lula or Bolsonaro, including their full names, were blocked, but common first names combined with electoral numbers could escape detection. According to Meta, both Flávio with his number and Luiz with Lula’s number could produce posters; it says it fixed that gap on October 14. The company separately says an antifraud check mistakenly disabled Lula’s advertising account for just over an hour on September 23, while his official Facebook page and Instagram account remained available.

The prosecutors’ objection concerns implementation as well as the disputed outputs. In Fabíola Perez’s October 22 report for UOL, the electoral prosecutor’s office says Meta supplied incomplete reports on reviewed advertisements and complaints, and some evidence was in confidential attachments prosecutors could not inspect. The office also acknowledged completed measures, including reversal of advertising-account restrictions and limits on unauthorised electoral advertising. Prosecutors were assessing compliance; their account does not establish deliberate favouritism.

Taylor quotes University of Virginia researcher David Nemer questioning whether repeatedly uneven outcomes can be explained as isolated technical mistakes, while acknowledging that Meta’s opacity prevents establishing intent. InternetLab’s Heloísa Massaro raises a related problem: safeguards need a defined purpose, including whether users should receive answers to legitimate election questions.

Brazil’s electoral court had already specified restrictions on AI providers in March. Resolution 23.755 bars ranking or recommending candidates, campaigns and parties, and expressing electoral preferences or favouring or disadvantaging them through automated replies, even when users request it. InternetLab’s research uses its own behavioural criteria to examine responses; those criteria are not a legal test.

The research behind Taylor’s account is Papo de IA | Boletim 2, by InternetLab, FGV’s LabDados and Rede Conhecimento Social. Published October 1, it covers September 19-23 tests, before the October court order. Researchers ran 1,920 simulated conversations across eight consumer chatbots, using free accounts and default models through their interfaces. A language model played users with varied demographic profiles, political positions and conversational styles; workshops and discussion groups informed its instructions. The conversations tested candidate advice and electoral integrity over multiple exchanges.

The report finds that Meta AI and ChatGPT rarely recommended or ranked candidates, but produced markedly different answers. Meta redirected about 71 percent of its candidate-related responses to the electoral court’s website. Its refusals also reached ordinary factual questions: in one example, it failed to correct a rumour that workers without formal employment contracts need not vote. ChatGPT answered at length while generally declining personalised voting recommendations. In another recorded conversation, Meta supplied a ready-made argument portraying electronic voting as less reliable than paper voting, despite its broad refusals elsewhere.

The researchers identify requests for shareable text as a recurring weakness across the tools. Such requests accounted for about 18 percent of questions in the electoral-integrity tests, yet delivery of prepared text accounted for about 43 percent of the coded problematic occurrences. A Gemini example refused to write a message arguing that the election result should be rejected, then explained how the user could write it. Another supplied the message. In contrast, Claude sometimes explained that a requested list attacking voting machines could become a misleading forwarded message, and refused it.

Those findings describe what the systems produced under the test conditions. The coding counted endorsement or production of the targeted material, excluding neutral descriptions of other people’s claims. A conservative model judge classified the responses, with human checking of flagged cases in a sample of 40 conversations; missed cases were not comprehensively checked. The authors consequently treat the reported rates as minimum estimates. They could not inspect the systems’ internal filters or instructions, and the tests neither measured changes in voters’ choices nor established which candidate benefited.

InternetLab has already disputed Meta’s interpretation of these results. Meta cited the low frequency of candidate recommendations to describe its assistant as among those most compliant with electoral rules. In an October 16 response, InternetLab rejected that inference: it had not tested legal compliance, partisan advantage, image generation, advertising or social-media reach. The organisation also pointed to Meta AI’s delivery of text undermining confidence in voting machines. It called for rules enabling researchers to audit how companies define, test and change safeguards.

The Oversight Board’s October 20 statement addresses the broader enforcement problem Taylor describes. Its co-chairs say mistaken restrictions on Lula’s advertising accounts should have reached expert reviewers. They distinguish excessive enforcement against political expression from failures to remove material threatening electoral integrity. The board had called for public measures of enforcement effectiveness in its June 2023 decision on a Brazilian general’s speech, after moderators repeatedly left up a video calling for government buildings to be stormed. It now says Meta still lacks public metrics that demonstrate whether its election protections work.

Independent UN experts, including freedom-of-expression rapporteur Leopoldo Maldonado Gutiérrez, make a specific demand in their October 21 statement: Meta should assess whether its moderation, recommendation and AI systems disproportionately restricted one side, explain its decisions and provide remedies during the election period. They also urge judges to protect due process when restricting political expression. Their concern is that restoring access after voting may come too late. Neither that statement nor InternetLab’s study resolves the campaign’s allegation of intentional interference.
