Criminal bottlenecks may delay losses from AI-assisted hacking

Joshua Saxe examines recruitment, payments and government adoption while urging preparation for more destructive attacks. His loss figures are modeled estimates.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 23 October 2026 975 words · ~5 min

Joshua Saxe argues in his essay on AI-driven cybercrime that recruitment, money laundering and the organization of criminal businesses may delay losses from increasingly capable hacking tools. His starting point is PHP2Shell, whose failed public exploits and working expert demonstrations have already complicated the debate about AI-assisted attacks. Saxe welcomes defenders' discovery and patching of vulnerabilities. He expects attackers to exploit them too, but argues that finding a way into a computer does not determine how much damage follows.

Saxe describes the essay as a sketch for future peer-reviewed work and acknowledges Claude's help with research. He begins with a chart estimating about $300 billion in global fraud and hacking losses in 2025, with about $5 billion attributable to AI. Its caption labels the AI contribution as his estimate of losses that would otherwise not have happened. The historical bars combine multiple data series in a statistical model; the 2026 bar is a projection. He interprets slower growth after ChatGPT's launch as evidence against a large AI-driven increase. A trend in total losses alone cannot identify how much harm AI added or how much defensive AI prevented.

The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report uses a different measure. It records more than $893 million in losses associated with complaints containing AI-related information. Its definition flags references to AI; it does not estimate how much less would have been lost without it. In their March 2026 GovAI technical report Global Cybercrime Damages: A Baseline for Frontier AI Risk Assessment, Kamilė Lukošiūtė, John Halstead and Luca Righetti argue that underreporting, differing definitions and measurement uncertainty make aggregate damage estimates inadequate for detecting incremental AI effects. They propose combining evidence from attacks, security tests and criminal adoption.

Saxe describes the work surrounding an intrusion: establishing infrastructure, finding valuable information inside a network, negotiating payment and moving stolen money into spendable accounts. Criminals must recruit and retain people while avoiding police. His demanding example of an AI-native criminal business would automate most of the sequence through to laundering the proceeds. He recalls reading about cryptographic extortion years before ransomware became a large industry, and attributes its subsequent expansion to payment systems, favorable jurisdictions and the organization of labor. He expects similarly consequential changes in business organization to take time.

Lawful jobs draw away skilled recruits, Saxe argues, leaving criminal groups less technically capable than many AI-risk accounts assume. His workforce model is dominated by scam operations, and his wage chart compares criminal programmers' pay with lawful employment. One underlying source, Kaspersky's January 2023 study of dark-web recruitment, examined posts from 155 forums and found no significant difference between median IT pay in the criminal and legitimate markets. Its salary analysis used more than 160 advertisements with explicit compensation. The study also describes interviews, probation and practical tests for skilled workers. Those advertisements establish recruitment practices and advertised wages, without measuring the skill level of the criminal workforce as a whole.

Saxe also argues that expansion attracts enforcement attention, even when criminals operate from permissive jurisdictions. His chart examines 152 named enterprises, including forums, laundering services and ransomware brands. Among enterprises that had closed, his chart attributes most closures to police action, but fewer than half of ransomware-brand closures to that cause. The definition includes seizures, sanctions and departures under police pressure as well as arrests. These are selected, documented enterprises, and Saxe acknowledges the bias toward cases police caught. The sample cannot establish the probability that an arbitrary criminal organization will be stopped.

Kirill Levchenko and colleagues at UC San Diego and other institutions studied similar economic constraints in Click Trajectories: End-to-End Analysis of the Spam Value Chain, presented at IEEE Security and Privacy in 2011. They followed spam advertising through infrastructure, purchases and payment processing. Three banks serviced payments for more than 95% of the spam-advertised goods in their study. Replacing banking relationships took more time and money than replacing hosting or domains. Their findings concern that sampled spam economy, providing a concrete example of the financial dependencies Saxe emphasizes.

For AI-dependent operations, Saxe adds the expense and reliability of model access. Providers can terminate accounts while criminals are still developing their workflows. His chart also includes operations that providers did not stop, including self-hosted models. A documented example of disruption is Anthropic's November 2025 account of a cyberespionage campaign: the company said it banned accounts as it identified them during a ten-day investigation. Anthropic also reported successful intrusions and substantial automation beyond initial access, alongside fabricated findings that required human validation.

Saxe distinguishes governments from profit-seeking criminals. States may have capital, skilled operators and their own model infrastructure, but he expects organizational change and competition with private-sector salaries to slow adoption. His technology chart estimates faster US security-agency adoption of chatbots than of several earlier technologies; its timing for offensive AI-agent adoption remains a projection. He adds strategic restraint: governments contemplating widespread disruption of another country's infrastructure must consider retaliation, potentially including physical warfare. More available cyberweapons therefore need not produce proportionately more destructive use.

In the discussion beneath Saxe's essay, Ciaran Martin welcomes the emphasis on attackers and distinguishes theoretical capability from practical probability. Mike Hodges agrees about the economics but raises another possibility: cyber operations could retain limited strategic effects even with better tools. Saxe replies that agent swarms could have greater wartime effects and give weaker powers new opportunities for asymmetric competition. He is less certain about cybercrime, whose development he says depends heavily on policy and economics.

Saxe ends by urging preparation during what he considers a temporary period favoring defenders. His dangerous futures include state-operated agent swarms that inflict much greater damage, destructive self-replicating programs released by ideologically motivated actors, and criminal organizations that automate much more of their business. His proposed response is to anticipate those operations and prevent them while the organizational obstacles still slow attackers.
