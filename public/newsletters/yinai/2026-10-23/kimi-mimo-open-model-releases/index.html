Kimi K3.1 Pro approaches the leading proprietary models; MiMo remains downloadable

Moonshot’s October 23 upgrade improves coding and agent work, with hosted access now and weights promised by November 4. Xiaomi’s current Pro and Flash models are available under MIT licenses, with updates addressing repetitive tool use.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 23 October 2026 1156 words · ~6 min

Moonshot AI announced Kimi K3.1 Pro on October 23, an upgrade to its largest open-model family. The model and a two-to-three-times faster Highspeed variant went live the same day on Kimi.ai, the Kimi apps, Kimi Work, Kimi Code and the Kimi API. Standard K3.1 Pro costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. The weights are not out yet. Moonshot says they will follow by November 4, and as of October 24 the moonshotai organization on Hugging Face holds Kimi K3 but no K3.1 repository and no K3.1 license. K3.1 Pro is a post-trained version of K3, the 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 104 billion active parameters and a one-million-token context released in July; Moonshot credits the gains to much larger post-training compute and joint reinforcement learning across hundreds of task domains.

Artificial Analysis has published independent results, scoring K3.1 Pro at 55 on its Intelligence Index, fourth overall, three points behind the leading Claude Opus 5.5 and 11 points above K3. Its Terminal-Bench 4.0 run put K3.1 Pro at 67 percent, the highest it has recorded. The firm expects K3.1 Pro to become the top open-weights model once the weights ship, cutting the gap to the proprietary leader from 12 points to 3; the current open leader on its board is Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro at 46. Most of Moonshot's own table was self-run at maximum reasoning effort in the Claude Code harness, with competitor numbers taken from vendor reports or leaderboards.

Moonshot's blog spends most of its length on what the model can build: complete games in Unity, Unreal and Godot, parametric CAD and Blender models driven by scripts the model writes itself, a new 3D Make product on kimi.ai for printable objects, and end-to-end financial modeling. In one internal run, K3.1 Pro built a walking simulator on human-atlas, an open-source anatomical model; when reward adjustments failed to fix arm motion, it probed the simulator directly, corrected 36 errors in shoulder-muscle moment-arm calculations, and retrained the controller, taking the simulated human from collapsing within seconds to 11.2 seconds of walking.

Cyber capability removed on purpose

As post-training scaled, vulnerability-research performance kept rising, and Moonshot traced the trend to cyber content in the general mid-training corpus and cyber tasks in the reinforcement-learning mix. For the open-weight release it removed both. On xdev-bench-hard, Moonshot's in-house set of exploit tasks built mostly from recently disclosed bugs in the Linux kernel, Node.js, JavaScriptCore, Exim and VirGL, the unfiltered checkpoint solved 90.5 percent of problems within three attempts and the final K3.1 Pro checkpoint solved 81.0 percent. The filtered model still substantially outperformed K3, which Moonshot attributes to improvements in general coding and sustained debugging carrying over to cyber tasks. Each task gives the agent the vulnerable source, a bug hint and the binary, and requires arbitrary code execution in a sandbox; K3.1 Pro ran in Moonshot's own KimiCode harness while comparison models ran in Claude Code. On Moonshot's chart the open-weight DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash scores 85.7 percent, above the filtered K3.1 Pro. Moonshot states two principles: it aims not to release weights that extend the absolute frontier of cyber capability, and it intends to "progressively raise the cyber capability available in open weights" so that defenders can audit code on infrastructure they control.

The idea has a research lineage Moonshot does not cite. Kyle O'Brien, Stephen Casper and colleagues showed in Deep Ignorance (August 2025) that filtering biothreat text out of pretraining data gave 6.9-billion-parameter models resistance to adversarial fine-tuning through 10,000 steps, though filtered models could still use dangerous information supplied in context. OpenAI's release rationale for gpt-oss, by Eric Wallace and colleagues, went the other way: maliciously fine-tune the model for cyber and biology first, then release because the result did not substantially advance the open-weight frontier. Moonshot's stated ceiling is the absolute cyber frontier, including proprietary systems. Its evidence compares an unfiltered checkpoint with the final model; it does not report malicious fine-tuning of that final model. A recent comparison is Anthropic's September 29 finding that GLM-5.3 produced working exploits in 50 of 410 ExploitBench attempts and had been "released without meaningful safeguards to limit misuse", which we covered at the time. UK AISI and US CAISI assessed K3's cyber capability four days before its weights shipped. Moonshot’s latest announcement presents its own cyber evaluation.

Reactions split. Simon Willison wrote that no soon-to-be open model "has scored this highly against the frontier", and Nathan Lambert read the gains as Moonshot having "figured out their RL" on an already strong base. Elie Bakouch praised Moonshot for "limiting cyber capabilities of open models (which is a first!)" while arguing trusted actors need defensive cyber tools. On Hacker News, one commenter asked "So they admit that they could do more, but they won't?", and the replies argued over responsible pacing versus a nerf, with several asking whether fine-tuning after November 4 would restore the capability.

Where Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6 stands

Xiaomi launched MiMo-V2.6-Pro and MiMo-V2.6-Flash on September 22. It now provides downloadable weights under MIT licenses on Hugging Face, a technical report, an RL training framework and more than 7,000 RL task environments. Pro is a 1.02-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 42 billion active parameters that takes text, image, video and audio; Xiaomi says its RL run took under six days and cost about $2.62 million for Pro and $0.85 million for Flash. After launch, users saw the model repeat the same tool calls without progress; Xiaomi traced the failure to an RL penalty that only fired above 32 calls in a turn, trained a small specialist teacher that learned when to stop, and distilled it into the main model for about $90,000, against an estimated $2.31 million to rerun the RL. The corrected models have served the API since September 25, and MOPD-suffix checkpoints are available on Hugging Face. The technical report reached arXiv on October 8, and Xiaomi retired the MiMo-V2.5-Pro and V2.5 API IDs on October 21. The RL scaling behind V2.6 came up in the October 20 issue.

The labs describe different choices about cyber training. Xiaomi's report describes training vulnerability reproduction with RL on OSS-Fuzz bugs, calling it a "foundational offensive-security skill", with cyber making up 4 percent of RL tasks, and it released 1,000 of those environments. Xiaomi reports MiMo-V2.6-Pro at 47.9 on ExploitBench, against Moonshot's 74.7 for K3.1 Pro, though each company ran its own harness and settings. Oliver Chen and Anthony Ozerov of Vals AI found that 1,795 of 2,698 of Xiaomi's released coding tasks leave the fix commit recoverable as unreachable Git objects, and that MiMo models went looking for it when the rules were ambiguous.

Moonshot kept its previous weight-release promise: Willison confirmed that K3's 1.56 TB of weights arrived on the pledged July 27 date, under a custom license requiring model-as-a-service businesses with more than $20 million in revenue over any 12 months to sign a separate agreement. Moonshot has not yet announced K3.1’s license.
