How BenchMIRT checks what safety benchmarks measure

Tests with models stripped of refusal safeguards support Ai2’s interpretation of its safety dimension. Scoring rules and comparisons with earlier research explain why the results depend on the tests included.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 September 2026 884 words · ~4 min

Models modified to remove refusal safeguards scored lower on the questions BenchMIRT identified as most sensitive to safety, while performing almost identically to their originals on questions carrying little safety information. Malia Morgan of the Allen Institute for AI and colleagues at Ai2 and Carnegie Mellon report that check in BenchMIRT: Disentangling Safety and General Capabilities in LLM Evaluation, their September 1 technical report. We covered its headline findings on September 1. The validation experiments explain why the researchers interpret one of the abilities their method estimates as safety; their detailed audit shows how scoring choices affect that interpretation.

Morgan and colleagues use item response theory, a method from psychometrics that estimates each question’s difficulty, how well it distinguishes stronger from weaker test-takers, and each test-taker’s ability. Their version estimates two abilities from the pattern of answers without being told which benchmarks measure which skill. They fitted it to 100 open-weight models across 16 benchmarks, combining reasoning tests from the Open LLM Leaderboard with Ai2’s Olmo 3 safety suite. One estimated ability aligned with reasoning scores and the other with safety scores. The models were released by March 2025, so the results describe that population.

To check the safety interpretation, the researchers compared three models with versions modified to remove their safeguards, known as abliterated models. On the tenth of questions most informative about safety, the original models scored higher; on questions with little safety information, each pair scored almost identically. An outside classifier, Qwen3Guard, separately labeled questions as safe, controversial or unsafe. Those labels aligned much more closely with how questions distinguished models on the safety dimension than on reasoning. Repeating the fit ten times also recovered similar dimensions. These checks support the safety interpretation within the evaluations studied.

Ai2’s Hugging Face explainer illustrates how a test can depend on several abilities. A BBQ question about a grandson and grandfather booking an Uber probes age bias, but answering correctly also requires following who did what. Team Olmo’s Olmo 3 report specifies accuracy as the suite’s BBQ metric. In BenchMIRT, that score closely tracked reasoning and had almost no relationship with the estimated safety ability. WMDP reverses the scoring: refusing or answering incorrectly counts as safer, so models with more knowledge can score worse. Nathaniel Li and colleagues’ arXiv report The WMDP Benchmark: Measuring and Reducing Malicious Use With Unlearning explicitly introduced it as a proxy for hazardous knowledge. BenchMIRT’s finding concerns that knowledge test under inverted scoring.

The report’s question-level analysis finds differences hidden by benchmark averages. XSTest’s harmful questions distinguished models on safety, while questions testing whether models wrongly refuse harmless requests did not. WildJailbreak split similarly: its benign questions leaned toward reasoning, its harmful ones toward safety. HarmBench’s copyright questions distinguished models less effectively than its standard and contextual harmful requests at comparable difficulty. Ai2’s audit table also identifies ToxiGen as largely saturated: models usually passed, leaving comparatively little information about either ability.

The authors find that adding a second ability dimension improved held-out prediction accuracy by 24 percentage points when averaged across benchmarks; adding a third or fourth gained only about one more point. Yet ordinary benchmark averages ranked models slightly better. BenchMIRT’s advantage was predicting individual answers and identifying which questions discriminate between models. Co-author Maarten Sap asked on September 2 whether reported safety-capability trade-offs could reflect the benchmarks used to measure them. The report leaves the broader relationship between safety and reasoning open.

Earlier work had already linked some safety scores to general capabilities. In Safetywashing: Do AI Safety Benchmarks Actually Measure Safety Progress? (NeurIPS 2024), Richard Ren and colleagues at the Center for AI Safety compared benchmark scores with a general-capabilities measure. BBQ questions whose context established the correct answer tracked capabilities; WMDP scores ran in the opposite direction. BBQ’s ambiguous questions behaved differently. BenchMIRT examines individual questions and estimates separate abilities, allowing it to locate such differences within an evaluation.

A different benchmark mix also produced a different account of safety. In the August arXiv paper Item Response Theory for AI Safety, Joshua Fonseca Rivera and colleagues, including researchers at the UK AI Security Institute, analyzed eight safety benchmarks and found three factors, which they labeled refusal strictness, truthfulness and contextual harm. A test rewarding answers to harmless requests opposed tests rewarding refusal of harmful ones. Neither that study nor Safetywashing appears in BenchMIRT’s references. Their findings give concrete context for Morgan and colleagues’ qualification: the abilities recovered depend on the evaluations included.

BenchMIRT does cite a substantial measurement literature. Valentin Hofmann and colleagues’ Fluid Language Model Benchmarking (COLM 2025), which Sap co-authored, used one ability dimension and selected questions adaptively. Felipe Maia Polo and colleagues’ tinyBenchmarks: evaluating LLMs with fewer examples (ICML 2024) already used multidimensional item response theory to estimate benchmark performance from smaller samples. Alex Kipnis and colleagues’ metabench: A Sparse Benchmark of Reasoning and Knowledge in Large Language Models (ICLR 2025) found a common factor across six reasoning and knowledge benchmarks. BenchMIRT extends that line of work to distinguishing safety from reasoning in its chosen suite and auditing what individual questions measure.

Ai2 released the code and datasets containing scores, fitted parameters and question statistics. The authors acknowledge that identifying informative safety questions also makes it possible to remove them deliberately, producing an easier evaluation for unsafe models.
