Sai-Ming Wong on who must answer for automated state decisions

Wong proposes a right to an answerable public office when a burdensome, contestable decision becomes effectively final. An official signature counts only when the institution enables genuine judgment.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 September 2026 1230 words · ~6 min

A lawful automated procedure can still deny someone the public judgment they are owed, Sai-Ming Wong argues in “Office and unowned architecture: public judgment in the age of algorithmic governance”, published September 26 in Ethics and Information Technology. Wong, affiliated with Hong Kong Baptist University, proposes a right to an answer from a public office that takes responsibility for a decision’s grounds. The argument concerns burdensome, contestable determinations that take legal effect before timely review. Authorization, validation and appeal can leave that responsibility missing.

Wong draws on Kant’s account of public right: state coercion must be justified to people as free and equal members of a legal order. General laws leave disputed questions about how they apply to particular people. Wong distinguishes the events government produces, such as notices and suspended payments, from the procedures producing them and the judgments through which an authority explains why the law binds this person in this way. Cary Coglianese and David Lehr’s “Regulating by Robot” (2017) argues that machine learning can fit conventional administrative and constitutional doctrines. Wong asks a further question: which office adopts the grounds of a particular decision?

The proposed right applies when four conditions coincide. A determination must materially affect interests such as liberty, livelihood, essential benefits or immigration status; settle a legal position; admit reasonable disagreement about the applicable facts or norms; and become effectively settled before timely review of its merits. Practical finality depends on more than whether an appeal formally exists. Wong identifies observable indicators: how often affected people appeal, how long hearings take relative to the onset of harm, whether a burden is suspended during review, and the barriers claimants face. Institutions must judge these conditions; Wong does not offer mechanical thresholds.

An office, in Wong’s account, is an institution with legal powers and duties, the capacity to receive claims and give answers, and responsibility that persists when its personnel change. Adopting a decision requires five externally checkable conditions: authority to reject the system’s recommendation; access to its legally relevant basis and error profile; time and training to assess it; independence from incentives punishing disagreement; and public responsibility for the outcome. Adoption concerns institutional design, not an official’s private feeling of ownership. More burdensome and contestable decisions require stronger provision for these conditions; an isolated shortfall does not automatically invalidate every decision. Workloads that make review impossible, however, systematically disable judgment.

Wong allows routine automation outside the four conditions and does not require a human decision in every case. Aziz Huq’s “A Right to a Human Decision” (2020) also rejects a general right to human decisions, proposing a right to a well-calibrated machine decision. Wong additionally requires an office to answer for the individual judgment when the conditions apply. Full technical transparency is unnecessary if officials understand enough to assess the relevant inference. Conversely, understandable outputs can remain unowned. That separates the proposal from the debate over explanation, including Sandra Wachter, Brent Mittelstadt and Luciano Floridi’s 2017 interpretation of the GDPR as providing limited information rights.

Wong describes five degrees of AI involvement. Pure assistance, such as summarizing records, and reliance on model outputs as evidence are generally permissible when officials retain responsibility for the legal inference. A strong presumption favoring the recommendation can be permissible if deviation remains genuinely possible. Automation that effectively decides cases without review before finality, and full substitution of a system for a decision-maker, violate the proposed right wherever the four conditions hold. Difficult cases include ninety-second reviews, incentives discouraging disagreement and nominal review under impossible workloads. The question is whether officials can exercise their authority in practice.

In Wong’s welfare hypothetical, an agency automatically denies below-threshold applications for subsistence benefits. Appeals exist but most claimants cannot effectively use them; officials monitor aggregate performance and exceptional cases. Uncertain facts about income and household composition make eligibility contestable, and deprivation can occur before review. The legislature authorized the scheme, but no office considered the claimant’s case and the model cannot assume responsibility. Wong would retain automated screening while requiring an empowered office to adopt or reject the grounds before the denial becomes practically final.

Wong’s second hypothetical concerns a judge using an AI-drafted opinion. A judge who reviews the record, critically examines the reasoning and revises the analysis can adopt the resulting judgment. Cursory reading, cosmetic edits and a signature under docket pressure do not suffice. An unread opinion written by a clerk has the same defect: the problem is the absence of adopted judgment. England and Wales’s October 2025 judicial guidance offers a practical comparison, emphasizing judicial office holders’ personal responsibility for material issued in their names.

Wong answers objections about opaque verdicts and complex evidence. Juries need not explain every inference because their verdicts occur within a continuing court institution, with sworn duties, evidence, instructions and review. Officials using expert systems need not understand every technical mechanism, but must understand an output’s legal relevance and evidentiary weight well enough to own the final inference. Validation establishes general reliability; subsequent review can correct mistakes. Neither supplies missing judgment when a burden first becomes effectively final. Wong also argues that choosing speed cannot waive the institutional answerability that gives a consequential decision its public character.

The same requirement reaches older bureaucracies: a paper points matrix can prevent judgment as effectively as AI. Wong holds that present AI systems cannot independently bear the duties an office requires, while leaving open future institutions built around AI with human trustees or other responsible agents. The discussion cites Christian List’s “Group Agency and Artificial Intelligence” (2021), which tentatively defends possible responsible moral agency for artificial systems under specified conditions. Wong’s restriction concerns institutional duty-bearing, not consciousness or predictive accuracy.

Other researchers have challenged nominal human oversight. Ben Green’s 2022 review of 41 oversight policies argues that people often cannot reliably perform the oversight roles assigned to them and recommends institutional oversight, including public justification of algorithm adoption. Rebecca Crootof, Margot Kaminski and W. Nicholson Price’s “Humans in the Loop” (2023) likewise urges regulation of the combined human-machine system. Wong does not cite either; the five adoption conditions describe how institutions must enable the official’s role. The proposal also differs from Andreas Matthias’s 2004 account of the responsibility gap, which concerns attributing responsibility for unpredictable machine behavior. Wong asks who answers for the grounds of the state’s act.

Australian materials illustrate the difference between legal authorization and Wong’s proposed criterion. A 2024 Attorney-General’s Department consultation paper, responding to the Robodebt Royal Commission, counted 46 legislative authorizations for automated decision-making, including section 6A of the Social Security (Administration) Act 1999. It also described human sign-off without significant independent analysis. Such authorization establishes legal permission; Wong’s argument asks an additional question about answerability. A different problem arose in Pintarich, as Anna Huggins explains in a 2018 case note: a court majority held that an automatically generated tax letter was not a decision because no officer had reached its conclusion. That harmed the taxpayer. Its focus on a mental process also differs from Wong’s institutional test.

Wong leaves institutions to determine adequate review time, training and independence, and does not specify the legal remedy for a decision that fails the proposed standard. The practical demand is nevertheless concrete: before a burdensome, contestable determination becomes effectively final, an identifiable office must be able to adopt or reject its grounds and remain answerable for them.
