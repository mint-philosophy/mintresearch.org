Deep Utopia’s surveillance and servitude need stronger justification, Thorstad argues

His close reading finds too little justification for pervasive monitoring and beings designed to enjoy serving others. He also questions whether a technological vision shaped by today’s science can guide preparations for the far future, even if it suggests that future lives could have immense value.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 September 2026 1231 words · ~6 min

Nick Bostrom gives surveillance and engineered servitude too little ethical scrutiny in Deep Utopia, David Thorstad argues in his September 26 Reflective Altruism essay. Thorstad, an assistant professor of philosophy at Vanderbilt and a research affiliate of the MINT Lab, which publishes this newsletter, examines Tuesday’s lecture in Bostrom’s 2024 book, Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World. His September 4 installment, covered in our September 5 issue, examined work, income and population. This second part asks what humanity could do with the universe’s resources and whether Bostrom sufficiently justifies the means he envisages.

In Thorstad’s account, Tuesday’s lecture begins by revisiting calculations from Superintelligence. Self-replicating probes traveling at half the speed of light might reach 6×1018 stars; at 99 percent, 2×1020. If a tenth had planets supporting a billion humans for a billion years, the high estimate reaches 1035 human lives. Dyson spheres, structures harvesting stellar energy, might support 1067 computational operations per second and perhaps 1058 simulated hundred-year lives. These possibilities require approaching technological maturity, or near-maximal control over nature. The lecture’s list of necessary advances runs from atomically precise manufacturing and machine superintelligence to medical improvements, brain uploading and pervasive monitoring. Thorstad finds this positive account brief and largely recycled.

Bostrom’s own five barriers, as Thorstad summarizes them, complicate that picture. Coordination failures may worsen when technology empowers criminals and dictators. Prudence may require slowing development when speed raises existential risk. Human values impose another limit: status remains competitive, meaningful impact and novelty may become scarce, and additional wealth may cease to satisfy. Moral constraints may also rule out some means of pursuing abundance. The fourth barrier is metaphysical: digital minds might be incapable of consciousness or moral status, undermining the largest estimates of future value. Finally, automation may face regulation, distrust and preferences for human-made goods, whether for status or solidarity. People might also want humans in roles such as clergy, ceremonial leadership and sport. The concerns about meaning and status are therefore part of Bostrom’s analysis, before Thorstad develops his objections.

Thorstad’s first objection concerns two proposals that, he thinks, receive much less argument than their moral importance warrants. In the passages he presents, Bostrom lists continuous, detailed monitoring and AI policing among the technologies needed for maturity. Elsewhere in the lecture, surveillance is offered as a way to prevent crime, while automated treaty enforcement could help states make credible nonaggression commitments. Thorstad links these suggestions to the surveillance scenario he criticized in May 2024: compulsory wearable cameras and microphones, with AI classifying the feeds and human officers investigating suspicious activity.

The original source, Bostrom’s 2019 Global Policy paper “The Vulnerable World Hypothesis”, gives that scenario a conditional setting. Bostrom considers inventions that could let individuals or small groups cause civilizational destruction, leaving existing policing and global governance unable to contain the threat. He illustrates intensive preventive policing with a wearable “freedom tag”; officers could question wearers or dispatch police, inspectors or drones. He includes privacy protections and oversight of officers, but acknowledges the danger of totalitarian misuse. The paper leaves the hypothesis’s truth open and declines to settle the overall balance for or against these capabilities. It nevertheless offers an additional reason to develop them, potentially before a threat becomes apparent. Thorstad regards the prospect as sufficiently disturbing that its reappearance in a utopian vision needs much more defense.

His parallel concern is about beings created to find fulfillment in subservience. In Thorstad’s presentation, Bostrom distinguishes superficial submission from creatures whose dispositions are thoroughly adapted to service, leaving open whether creating the latter would be wrong. He acknowledges possible further moral complications without developing them. Later, discussing labor, Bostrom suggests that owning sentient machines might differ sufficiently from human slavery to require a new moral category. Thorstad objects to this willingness to entertain potentially objectionable relationships with so little justification. He does not supply a developed argument that every such arrangement is impermissible. His complaint is that the chapter devotes too little reflection to the conditions under which creating or owning these beings could be acceptable.

The wider debate identifies several disputed conditions. In their chapter on AI rights, self-respect and freedom, Eric Schwitzgebel and Mara Garza summarize Steve Petersen’s defense: creating willing servants could be permissible if they enjoy serving and retain those desires after sound reflection. Schwitzgebel and Garza object that beings with human-level moral status should appreciate their own equal worth; designing them to subordinate that worth can undermine self-respect. Adam Bales’s 2025 Philosophical Quarterly paper argues that willing servitude can impair autonomy even without coercion or unhappiness, giving moral reasons against creating such systems, other things equal. In the abstract for a July 8 London talk, Andreas Mogensen challenges recent objections while allowing that willing service can convey a demeaning view of the beings’ status without necessarily being wrong to create.

Bostrom’s other writing also supplies qualifications. In their explicitly tentative “Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society”, Bostrom and Carl Shulman suggest that designing motivations need not generally be wrong. They require informed consent from AIs capable of giving it, while warning that engineered consent and mass copying of unusually compliant minds can still enable exploitation. This is a fuller treatment of consent than the book passages Thorstad discusses. An earlier 2020 critique by Benjamin Charles Germain Lee likewise juxtaposed singularitarian concern for machine workers with the rights of humans under pervasive surveillance.

Thorstad’s second objection concerns the technological imagination itself. A medieval thinker might have treated agricultural improvements as the essential inventions and pictured plenty as Cockaigne, with sausage walls and rivers of wine. A nineteenth-century industrialist might have imagined automated factories and abundant coal. Bostrom’s space settlement, stellar energy collection and simulated minds similarly reflect present scientific understanding. Thorstad allows that some goals, such as medical progress, may survive changes in that understanding. His concern is that many supposedly necessary technologies may prove as historically contingent as the lists those earlier thinkers would have produced. The limitation belongs to futurism generally, rather than being a personal failing of Bostrom’s.

Thorstad anticipates a reply: even an incomplete vision might establish a lower bound on the value of possible futures, with later discoveries allowing something better still. Bostrom’s 2003 Utilitas paper, “Astronomical Waste,” deliberately used technologies whose feasibility had been argued to estimate such a floor; its practical conclusion prioritized eventual safe colonization over maximum speed. His 2013 paper, “Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority,” likewise recognized that unforeseen technologies could yield much larger estimates. Thorstad grants that the lower-bound reply might succeed. But an estimate of possible value does not establish what the future will look like or which institutions and technologies we should build to prepare for it. That distinction preserves his objection even if the astronomical estimates are accepted.

Earlier reviews illustrate related disagreements. Robin Hanson’s April 2024 review questioned whether radically advanced societies would retain values resembling those of Bostrom’s present milieu. PeterMcCluskey’s review that month praised Tuesday and Wednesday as the most valuable chapters and dismissed objections based simply on an inability to imagine unfamiliar technologies. Thorstad’s objection concerns the limits of present scientific assumptions, not mere unfamiliarity with proposed inventions. His series continues the complaint made in Part 1 that important argumentative steps need fuller justification; the announced next installment addresses Wednesday’s lecture.
