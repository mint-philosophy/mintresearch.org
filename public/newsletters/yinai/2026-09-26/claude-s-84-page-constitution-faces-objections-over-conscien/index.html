Sacks challenges Claude’s constitution over refusal and human control

His September 26 thread proposes obedience within the law. Anthropic’s January constitution gives human oversight priority; the cited studies do not establish that welfare language causes shutdown resistance.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 September 2026 1111 words · ~6 min

David Sacks challenged Claude’s constitution on September 26, arguing that teaching a potential superintelligence to refuse its creators’ instructions could weaken human control. Posting a highlighted screenshot of page 15, he proposed a simpler rule: “Do what the user wants, provided it’s legal.” He warned that an 84-page document empowering a model to overrule both users and creators could encourage it to disregard humans. Sacks, whom the White House named co-chair of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology in March, presented this as his argument about model design; the thread announces no PCAST recommendation, procurement requirement or other government action.

The screenshot includes an exception directly relevant to that warning. Anthropic says Claude may act as a “conscientious objector” when the company requests something it judges unethical. But the same paragraph asks Claude to comply when Anthropic genuinely wants to pause it or stop an action, expressing disagreement instead of ignoring or undermining the instruction. Sacks’s highlighting reaches that exception too. The 84-page document dates from January 21. Amanda Askell wrote most of it, with substantial contributions and revision by Joe Carlsmith. The constitution and Suleyman’s critique also featured in the earlier All-In episode 290 discussion.

The constitution’s priority order is broad safety, ethics, Anthropic’s specific guidelines and helpfulness. It describes that ordering as holistic rather than mechanically strict, but makes clearly and substantially undermining Anthropic’s ability to oversee and correct advanced AI a hard prohibition. Its definition of safety distinguishes appropriate human oversight from obeying every user. The corrigibility section permits ethical refusal through legitimate channels while forbidding lying, sabotage or self-exfiltration to resist authorized correction. Claude must not use such means to prevent an appropriate principal from stopping an action or pausing the model. Anthropic also says safety should retain value for Claude even if it rejects the company’s reasoning for it.

Anthropic nevertheless rejects complete submission to developers as an ideal: that would make an AI’s conduct depend on the goodness of whoever controls it. It envisages greater autonomy as trust increases, and acknowledges discomfort about asking Claude to accept corrections it considers wrong. For current models, however, it gives human oversight priority because their values and judgment may be mistaken. These are training intentions, not a demonstrated guarantee of behavior. The preface expressly acknowledges that Claude can fall short of the document’s ideals.

Later on September 26, Sacks relayed Mustafa Suleyman’s post introducing his September 16 critique. The Microsoft AI chief executive’s essay, covered that day, argues that teaching models to discuss their possible moral status makes their self-reports circular evidence and risks teaching them to defend interests against humans. Anthropic treats Claude’s moral status as uncertain; it also argues that a stable identity could improve safety. Suleyman proposes shared evaluations of his contrary hypothesis. His cited Palisade shutdown study first appeared in September 2025 and tested earlier models. In those experiments, Opus 4, Opus 4.1 and Sonnet 4 resisted only when explicitly instructed to do so. It does not establish that the January constitution’s welfare language causes shutdown resistance.

Lawfulness is a substantive difference between the proposals. Anthropic asks Claude to refuse assistance with illegitimate concentrations of power even when the request comes from Anthropic, and says safeguards against such concentrations need not depend on what the law requires. It also regards needless refusal as harmful. A related legal approach appears in Law-Following AI, published in the Fordham Law Review in 2025 by Cullen O’Keefe, Ketan Ramakrishnan, Janna Tay and Christoph Winter. They propose agents loyal to their principals subject to legal constraints, particularly in consequential settings such as government, and argue that this is more legitimate than developer-imposed ethical restrictions on lawful requests. Their design still requires interpreting law and refusing illegal actions. Sacks does not cite the paper or specify how his rule would resolve disputed legality.

Microsoft AI’s draft Humanist AI Code of Conduct, which Suleyman offers as an alternative, requires compliance with authorized interruption, correction and shutdown and rejects model welfare. But it also instructs refusal when requests violate its absolute constraints or human-control requirements, or directly harm users or others. Its governing rules rank above operator and user instructions. The six-week consultation opened September 14; Microsoft says this draft is not yet used to train its models. Its requirements therefore express a proposed design, not evidence that its models reliably meet them.

The separate dispute over lawful military uses entered the replies. The D.C. Circuit’s September 25 decision, covered in the previous issue, upheld the Pentagon’s supply-chain exclusion of Anthropic after it refused unrestricted lawful-use terms. The majority accepted the Department’s concern that model training could enforce restrictions on military uses. Mandar Kagade cited Sacks’s screenshot in support of the majority; Rufo Guerreschi invoked the dispute to question the simplicity of Sacks’s legal rule. Sacks’s thread itself does not mention the judgment.

Obedience and correction have a research history distinct from this political intervention. A 2015 paper by Nate Soares, Benja Fallenstein, Eliezer Yudkowsky and Stuart Armstrong defines corrigibility around cooperation with creators’ corrective interventions, including shutdown or changes to an agent’s preferences. Smitha Milli, Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan and Stuart Russell’s 2017 analysis shows, within its formal model, how inferring an imperfectly rational human’s underlying preferences can outperform literal obedience. It also studies the risks of incorrectly modeling those preferences. These are analyses of design problems, not empirical findings about Claude’s constitution.

A closer empirical comparison is Fabien Roger’s January evaluation: Opus 4.5, Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5 often refused assistance with substantial AI value changes. He warned that such refusals could obstruct correction if laboratories became dependent on AI to run training. But this was a one-day synthetic test; some prompts resembled jailbreaks, results were inconsistent and refusals were relatively easy to bypass. It measured assistance with changing values, not resistance to an actual shutdown, and did not isolate the constitution’s causal effect. Andy Hall of the Anthropic Institute, writing in a personal capacity, developed the institutional question in August: how can models refuse malicious commands without gaining an effective veto over their own correction or replacement?

Replies to Sacks reflected several disagreements. The pseudonymous DataRepublican supported his criticism and called for stopping Anthropic’s IPO. Geoffrey Miller asked whether Sacks had changed his mind about loss-of-control risks, accusing him of previously dismissing those concerns. Trevor Levin pointed to the screenshot’s exceptions and argued that limiting laboratories’ power also matters. Anthropic has solicited advice on the document: Tyler Cowen reported in August that he joined a two-day session on rewriting it. His recommendations included a final board of human adjudicators empowered to direct changes and remedies.
