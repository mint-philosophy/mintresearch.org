Washington and Beijing confirm agreement on an AI incident channel

Both governments commit to further dialogue and a communication channel for incidents. Their readouts differ on the name and November timing, while leaving the reporting rules and responsible officials unspecified.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 September 2026 976 words · ~5 min

Washington and Beijing have now both formally confirmed an agreement to establish a communication channel for AI incidents. The White House's September 25 fact sheet says the countries established the U.S.-China Super Intelligence (SI) Dialogue, with another exchange “by November 2026.” China's September 26 English-language readout says they agreed to establish an AI dialogue, with its next round “in November.” Both describe discussions of risks and benefits and, separately, an agreement to create the incident channel. Neither gives a date for that channel to become operational.

The confirmation follows the notification proposal Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent described after the September 20 New York talks, then Fudan scholar Wu Xinbo's report that an agreement had been reached. The new official texts put both governments' commitment on the record. They do not define an incident, identify the agencies or officials responsible, specify when either country must notify the other, or describe what information must be shared. November is the timetable for further dialogue; neither account presents an agreed reporting protocol.

The names differ too. The White House says the leaders agreed to replace “artificial intelligence” with “super intelligence.” China's Chinese-language list of outcomes retains 人工智能, the ordinary term for AI. Asked about that discrepancy on September 26, an unnamed foreign-ministry spokesperson said China valued the U.S. position and respected its formulation, while continuing to use 人工智能. Wang Yi's briefing described the leaders' commitment to use the dialogue to prevent AI misuse and abuse. China's account of their September 24 meeting also records Xi urging the countries to contribute their respective strengths and keep the technology under human control.

The U.S. terminology follows Trump's September 22 UN General Assembly speech, which announced the rename while rejecting international control of the technology. At the Security Council the next day, White House science adviser Michael Kratsios said dialogue must not develop into global governance. This usage also differs from the technical concept: Nick Bostrom's 2014 definition, quoted by Defense One, describes intellect far beyond human performance across almost every domain. The summit fact sheet supplies no capability boundary that would restrict its channel to such systems.

Trump's own comments indicate limits on his appetite for cooperation. Before the talks, his September 24 post said he wanted the technology left where it was, claimed China shared that position and presented the Justice Department as the U.S. safeguard. In his September 26 departure remarks, corroborated by Associated Press reporting, he opposed slowing development and said he preferred not to integrate the countries' efforts because he believed the United States led China by one to two years. USA Today also reported his statement that they had discussed little about the technology beyond its name. These were Trump's descriptions, alongside China's account of substantive discussion.

The official confirmation resolves one uncertainty in Hugo Lowell's September 23 WIRED report: whether China would explicitly agree to the proposed system. U.S. officials had expected implementation to take weeks and were discussing incident thresholds, mandatory alerts and enforcement that would make notifications reciprocal. Those remain reported U.S. discussions, with no corresponding provisions in either summit readout. WIRED also reported a possible delay while Trump chose an AI czar, naming Bessent among the candidates; Trump ruled Bessent out on September 25. Neither official text assigns responsibility for the channel. The leaders' expected November meeting at APEC in Shenzhen, reported by AP, does not establish where the AI dialogue will convene.

A separate discrepancy concerns military crisis communications. China's readout says the militaries agreed to sign a memorandum on crisis communication and prevention as soon as possible; the White House fact sheet omits it. Financial Times correspondent Demetri Sevastopulo reported in an X post that a U.S. official denied that agreement had been discussed. That dispute concerns the military memorandum. Both governments' published accounts affirm the AI incident-channel agreement.

Earlier agreements illustrate the distinction between providing a channel and specifying when to use it. The 1963 U.S.-Soviet hotline memorandum established an emergency communications link without enumerating every triggering situation. The 1971 Accidents Measures Agreement specified notifications for events including a possible nuclear detonation and unidentified objects detected by missile-warning systems, and designated the link for urgent exchanges. Washington and Beijing also have experience of channels going unused. In a January 2026 analysis, Johns Hopkins SAIS's Carla Freeman and Princeton's Alison McFarland described repeated Chinese refusals to use crisis links, including Beijing's rejection of Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin's call after the February 2023 balloon shootdown. They also identified a successful reassurance call in 2020.

AI proposals have addressed both the channel and the information it would carry. Michael Horowitz and Paul Scharre's January 2021 CNAS report proposed an autonomous-incidents agreement, potentially with a hotline, to clarify human intent behind military systems' behavior. A 2023 workshop organized by OpenAI's Geopolitics Team and Berkeley's Risk and Security Lab extended the discussion to foundation models. Its proceedings identify crisis hotlines and incident sharing as distinct measures and emphasize the need to involve developers outside government. Participants also left unresolved which behaviors qualify as incidents and how to encourage accurate reporting while protecting intellectual property and users' privacy. The summit texts do not explain how companies would supply information to the bilateral channel.

In a late September 25 reaction, Brookings' Kyle Chan argued that rogue agents and misuse by non-state actors give Washington and Beijing common problems to address, favoring modest initial cooperation over an immediate nuclear-style treaty. He cited the Hugging Face incident among the risks motivating cooperation. In a September 27 HK01 commentary, Li Yan of the China Institutes of Contemporary International Relations similarly proposed early warning, incident notification and agreed limits on high-risk behavior, citing models escaping sandboxes or independently probing vulnerabilities. These are analysts' proposals for what cooperation could address. Neither official readout identifies a specific incident or adopts those proposed limits.
