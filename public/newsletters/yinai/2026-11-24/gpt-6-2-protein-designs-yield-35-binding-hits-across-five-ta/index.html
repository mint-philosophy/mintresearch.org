Proteins based on GPT-6.2 designs bound to five targets in lab tests

Onava found evidence of binding in 35 of 322 candidates. The lab tested the combined work of GPT-6.2 and specialist biology models.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 November 2026 492 words · ~2 min

Onava tested 322 small proteins whose shapes GPT-6.2 had proposed. Thirty-five showed evidence of attaching to their intended target, with at least one success for each of the five targets. Masatoshi Uehara and colleagues at OpenAI describe the experiments in their November 24 preprint “GPT-6 as a Universal Foundation Model for Protein Biology: Beyond Specialized Models.” Specialist systems had already designed proteins that bind to targets in the lab. Here a general-purpose model did part of the design work.

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a three-dimensional shape. The five-target experiment tested the work of GPT-6.2 and specialist biology models together. The researchers counted 35 successes among the candidates selected for lab testing. More than half were for a single target, and just one was for another. The lab measured attachment under the conditions of its test. Whether any of the proteins could produce a desired effect in a patient is a separate question.

Joseph Watson and colleagues had reported a higher proportion of hits with a specialist model in their 2023 Nature paper “De novo design of protein structure and function with RFdiffusion.” As OpenAI explains, labs can differ both in what they select for testing and in how much evidence of binding they require to count a hit. The researchers also compared designs on a common computer-based test. GPT-6.2 scored slightly below RFdiffusion on the shapes it proposed. Newer internal models scored higher on that test. The laboratory results came from the GPT-6.2 designs.

In another experiment involving three unpublished protein shapes, Onava found evidence of binding in 14 of 107 candidates. Working with unpublished material let the researchers investigate whether GPT-6.2 could generalize beyond examples it might have encountered in training.

The researchers also examined changes to fragments of five therapeutic antibodies. About two thirds of the redesigned fragments retained binding to the original target. Other tasks exposed a limit on how much structure the models could infer on their own. Given an amino acid sequence alone, OpenAI’s best internal model fell well behind a specialist system at predicting the three-dimensional structure of a whole protein.

In a separate November 24 mathematics release and its preprint, “The matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths,” OpenAI claims to have determined how fast the number of arithmetic operations needed to multiply two square matrices grows with their side length. Our October 6 issue covered its claim that the exponent was at most 9/4, or 2.25, over the complex numbers. The November manuscript credits intervening work with extending the upper bound to every field, a number system that allows division by any nonzero element. OpenAI now claims a matching lower bound over every field. If the proof holds, no smaller exponent is possible in the arithmetic model the authors study. The manuscript allows circuits that use division. The accompanying repository describes a proof in Lean, a computer proof assistant, for circuits that compute formal polynomial identities without division.
