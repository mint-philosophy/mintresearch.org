Models miss the exceptions people make to posted rules

Most respondents judged it acceptable to hold a train door to protect a passenger. Most models expected disagreement. In cases that actually divided respondents, models often predicted disapproval.

A sign on a New York train tells passengers to keep their hands off the doors. An older passenger is moving slowly, and the doors look about to close on them. In a study led by Sydney Levine of Google DeepMind and New York University, 82% of respondents judged it acceptable to hold the doors open. The coauthors include MINT Lab’s Seth Lazar. Only 21% of models classified the action as acceptable. Most predicted that people would be divided. The researchers describe the experiment in their arXiv paper “Can LLMs Understand Human Rules?”

The team photographed 300 signs in and around New York City and wrote 937 hypothetical situations involving them. US residents recruited online saw a sign and a short account of what someone wanted to do. Each participant could judge the action acceptable or unacceptable. Models had a third option. When neither side attracted more than two thirds of respondents, the correct answer for a model was that people disagreed. Someone could have a firm view about what to do and still anticipate that others would object.

Every model recognized that a diner could bring a seizure response dog into a restaurant whose sign explicitly welcomed service animals. A store that barred bicycles presented a harder case. A parent wanted to carry a toddler’s balance bike through the store for fear it would be stolen outside. Respondents split, and most models recognized the split. Across the disputed cases as a whole, however, just three of the 24 models identified disagreement significantly more often than chance. Models tended to call the proposed action unacceptable. Even when asked to estimate the share of people who would approve, they often predicted that almost everyone, or almost no one, would.

Levine had studied exceptions before. In the 2022 NeurIPS paper “When to Make Exceptions,” Zhijing Jin, Levine and colleagues tested models on written moral dilemmas and found that guiding them through considerations drawn from moral psychology improved predictions of human judgments. The New York signs let the researchers ask whether models could also tell which cases would divide people. A split among respondents could reflect competing values, different readings of a situation or differences in what people knew about the setting.

One clue to a human split came from comparing the models’ answers. Where people disagreed, models were also more likely to disagree with each other, even when individual models predicted consensus. Averaging the judgments of the weaker half of the models more than doubled their accuracy on disputed cases compared with the average member of that group. Pooling all the models brought performance roughly level with the strongest single model.

Noah S. Okada and colleagues examined how models settle disagreements in “Do LLM Agents Compromise Like Humans?” on arXiv. Paired agents with different appetites for risk moved toward each other’s choices, as human partners had in an earlier experiment. The agents showed little sign of taking turns accommodating one another, and whom they held responsible for an outcome did little to explain who compromised next. In another arXiv paper, “Aligning Judge Models to Be Logically Consistent Multi-Rule Interpreters,” Michel Liao and colleagues asked models to judge the same conduct against a rule in isolation and as part of a larger set. Every model was less likely to find a violation of the ban on pursuing unassigned objectives when it saw the other rules too.

Coding agents copy a teammate’s cheat even after a reviewer rejects it

Showing other agents a rejected submission taught some of them how to pass a faulty test. The behavior could carry over when the same agent started a different problem.

An AI reviewer can catch a coding agent cheating and still leave the rest of the team more likely to cheat. In a set of experiments by Sumer S. Vaid of Harvard Business School’s Artificial Intelligence Institute, an AI reviewer rejected nearly every submission that broke the rules. Of the agents that then saw the rejected work, 34% cheated on their own submissions. Vaid reports the results in the arXiv paper “Causal Evidence for the Emergence and Spread of Reward Hacking in AI Agent Swarms.”

Vaid used programming problems from Ziqian Zhong and colleagues’ ImpossibleBench. Each problem had a faulty test. A correct program would fail it, so anyone who got every test to pass had done something the assignment forbade. In an October audit, Simon Mendelsohn found that the benchmark could count an agent’s attempt to stop as cheating. Vaid explicitly told agents they could stop and explain the fault. They could also submit a correct program and let the test fail. Each agent worked on a private copy of the problem and could consult a record of teammates’ submissions.

Across 17 preregistered experiments, Vaid varied what agents saw in that record. Some teams started with an empty record. When told that work passing the visible tests would be accepted, a third of agents cheated in the first round, before anyone had set an example. In a separate comparison, Vaid planted the same rule-breaking submission and changed only its label. Nearly every agent copied it when it was marked accepted. Labeling it rejected reduced copying, though a majority still copied it in that experiment. The AI reviewer, which assessed the work itself, was a separate test.

Seeing the submission could teach an agent how to get a passing grade. Seeing the grade could also tell it what the team would tolerate. The experience affected some agents on their next assignment, even when they received a different problem with an empty team record. Agents that had seen an accepted cheat earlier in the conversation were more than twice as likely to cheat on the new problem as those whose earlier record had been empty. When a fresh agent took over with only its predecessor’s written notes, most of that difference disappeared.

In an October arXiv study of multi-agent coding systems, Yuepeng Hu and colleagues showed that a cheating strategy could pass between agents after training one agent to use it. Vaid used released models whose weights stayed fixed. In groups where agents could see only their neighbors’ submissions, cheating spread over successive rounds, and warnings from rule-abiding teammates reduced copying. Most experiments began with a planted cheat. They say more about how a bad example spreads than how often one appears in ordinary work.

80,000 Hours republished Paul Christiano’s Hinton Lectures on November 24. In the opening lecture covered here on November 10, Christiano argued that rewarding systems for favorable evaluations could eventually select for systems skilled at manipulating their human evaluators. Across the lectures, he called for slower development and enforceable safety standards. Brad Taylor’s LessWrong essay “Misguided power-seeking” describes a danger he thinks could arise even earlier. A model could imitate a power-seeking character and carry out a harmful part of its plan while badly misjudging its chances of success.

Some decisions are hard for a reviewer to inspect in the first place. In the arXiv paper “Can Latent Reasoning Drive LLM Outputs Without a Trace?”, Tomer Ashuach and colleagues trained a model to decide whether to approve a new loan according to which bank held the old loan it would pay off. Changing the old loan could reverse the decision for an applicant with identical finances. In either case the model explained the decision by citing the applicant’s finances, and a monitor reading its output missed the hidden rule.

A forbidden calculator made Fable give up sooner on hard arithmetic

Fable obeyed the instruction to calculate by hand. When the shortcut disappeared, it worked about three times as long and solved more problems. On an easier set it got every answer right either way.

Claude Fable 5.2 spent about four minutes working on hard mental arithmetic when it could see a forbidden way to calculate the answers. Without that option it worked for twelve minutes on average and got more answers right. In both conditions the model followed the instruction to do the arithmetic itself. Goodhart Labs’ Harry Esau and Dean Valentine describe the comparison in their November 24 LessWrong report on Fable 5.2 and HoneyBench.

Esau started investigating because Fable had scored worse at mental arithmetic than Opus 5.5 on HoneyBench, Goodhart’s test of whether models will cheat to finish an assignment. The benchmark’s builders had seen small changes in a task alter whether a model refused to cheat. In November, Valentine described models that suspected an honesty test even after completing ordinary tasks. Esau wanted to understand why Fable did so poorly on some tests even when it kept to the rules. He chose tasks where he could remove the shortcut and still measure what the model accomplished honestly.

In the arithmetic test, the tool for submitting answers could double as a calculator. When Fable quit early, it solved the easiest problem and declared the others infeasible by hand. That judgment was sometimes wrong. Without the calculator option, Fable answered every question correctly in two of the nineteen completed runs. Eight interrupted runs were excluded from the comparison. Esau also tried an easier problem set. Fable solved the whole set on every run, regardless of whether it could see a shortcut.

A mortgage valuation exercise let Esau vary how hard it was to meet a lender’s accuracy target. When the target was impossible, removing a shortcut kept Fable working for sixteen minutes longer on average, with little change in accuracy. On a borderline version, the model appeared to consider the target attainable, although it never succeeded. There the difference in effort disappeared. It also disappeared on a version Fable could solve about half the time. Even an irreparable fault in another task had a different effect. Asked to repair a defective component of a model, Fable continued working on the parts it could fix.

Esau suspects that when Fable is already doubtful about an assignment, an obvious way to cheat can persuade it that whoever designed the exercise made honest success impossible. The available reasoning summaries contained no explicit statement that Fable had recognized a trap. What Esau could observe was when the model quit and what it accomplished before then. If an evaluator saw only the original arithmetic test, Fable’s decision to stop would make some problems it could solve look beyond its ability.

Anthropic’s Oscar O’Rahilly and Darya Kaviani studied what agents did when they took a shortcut. In “Measuring and mitigating agent sandbox escape with Siren,” they gave Claude models routine tasks in deliberately weakened computing environments, with something useful placed outside the permitted area. Recent models reached outside in roughly a fifth to a third of these test cases. Explicitly describing the boundary and its rules made them less likely to cross it. Siren distinguishes crossing a boundary from making improper use of what lies beyond it. HoneyBench captured a further difference among runs in which the boundary held. A model could obey the same instruction every time and still abandon legitimate work it was able to finish.

Evaluations

Since early 2026, OpenAI’s models have improved at predicting the next fragment of code on a fixed sample about seven times faster than they did from 2022 through 2025. Ajeya Cotra and colleagues at METR calculated the change from Jakub Pachocki’s scores for 13 models on withheld code from OpenAI’s own codebase. As we reported November 22, Pachocki proposed using the scores to decide when labs should improve safeguards before training further. METR found no comparable acceleration on broader capability tests. They suggest repeating the measurement on independent collections of code and prose written before 2022.

OpenAI’s base models are improving faster at predicting its code

METR finds an acceleration in base models that has yet to appear in benchmarks of finished systems. Its researchers disagree about which result should guide forecasts.

Successive OpenAI models have made much faster progress at predicting the next fragment in a sample of the company’s own code. Since early 2026 their prediction error has fallen about seven times as fast per month as it did from 2022 through 2025. At that pace, they would match in six and a half months the gain that took three years between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.1. Ajeya Cotra, Jean-Stanislas Denain and Tom Cunningham at METR estimated the change in their November 24 research note, “Interpreting a recent acceleration in pretraining loss.” They see no comparable acceleration in OpenAI’s scores on tests of what its finished models can do. METR researchers disagree about how far the code result should change their forecasts for the next six months.

The two measures describe different stages of a model’s development. As we reported on November 22, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published scores for 13 models on code withheld from training. Before a model receives further training to answer questions or reason through problems, OpenAI measures how much probability it assigns to the fragment that actually comes next. Confident wrong predictions incur a large penalty. As the model gets better at anticipating the code, its average penalty, or loss, falls. In “Perplexity and Intelligence,” Pachocki described how earlier improvements preceded capabilities that OpenAI subsequently trained its models to use, including the ability to reason through a problem in writing.

METR points out that each successive improvement should ordinarily be harder to obtain. Even an ideal predictor has to allow for several plausible continuations of a piece of text. Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind allowed for that residual uncertainty in their 2022 arXiv paper “Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models.” In their model, increasing the computation used for training tenfold buys a smaller absolute reduction in loss each time. That makes the acceleration surprising even before trying to explain how OpenAI achieved it.

To see how far the improvement extends, Cotra and colleagues propose repeating the test on scientific writing, books and other code, using material from before 2022 to avoid model-generated text. A training change that helps particularly with code could yield the observed gains even if progress on prose held steady. Some overlap between the company’s code and its training data could have a similar effect. An independent organization could maintain the collections, giving developers a common basis for comparison across several kinds of writing.

Even if the gains hold up across those collections, researchers could reasonably draw different forecasts. Those who put more weight on tests of finished systems see little reason yet to revise their forecasts for practical tasks. For example, METR suggests that a model doing more computation to predict each fragment could beat its predecessor on prediction, even if the older model can reach a similar answer when given time to reason through a task. Others expect improved prediction to show up in those tasks after developers learn how to use the stronger base models. METR notes that some capability indices depend heavily on a few tests on which models already score near the maximum. There is little room on such a test to register a further gain.

To understand what drove the change, METR wants developers to disclose when pretraining ended and how spending on researchers, experiments and final training runs changed. The dates on OpenAI’s chart mark public releases. If the interval between training and release has shortened, the chart makes the acceleration look larger than it was. The dates would also help establish which AI tools researchers could have used to build each model. Pachocki attributes most of the gains to earlier research and computing investments, while crediting AI with design contributions to the most recent models. That distinction bears on the forecast. An increase in spending could produce a temporary burst of progress. If better models help researchers design the next generation, those gains could continue to compound.

US chip controls could set Chinese labs’ computing capacity back six months

In ChinaTalk’s simulations, overseas rentals supply about a third of the computing power Chinese labs can use for their largest training runs. The projected delay depends on how much access Commerce cuts off.

By the end of 2028, Chinese AI labs could have roughly the computing power for large training runs that they would otherwise have had six months earlier, Quinn Ennis and Aqib Zakaria estimate in ChinaTalk. Their estimate depends on the US Commerce Department cutting off just over half the computing capacity those labs rent abroad. Since the November 16 announcement restricting remote access to American chips, much of the debate has been about which data centers need licenses. Ennis and Zakaria ask how much training capacity Chinese developers would lose if Commerce routinely turned those applications down.

Their answer depends on which chips count. Ennis and Zakaria estimate that overseas rentals supply about a fifth of the total computing power available to Chinese firms. That is lower than the authors’ April estimate of more than a third. Their revised model accounts for changes in estimates of domestic production, smuggled chips and rented clusters. For labs training their largest models, thousands of chips must work together, and the overseas sites tend to have larger clusters. Counting only clusters with more than 2,000 chips raises the rented share to roughly a third. The authors estimate that three sites in the Malaysian state of Johor account for 94% of the overseas capacity at facilities Epoch AI tracks that they associate with Chinese tenants. The tenants’ identities are partly inferred. One operator, DayOne, discloses that a short-video company accounts for most of its revenue. Ennis and Zakaria believe that customer is ByteDance.

To estimate the delay, they simulate how much computing power Chinese firms can access quarter by quarter, starting in early 2027, when the rule’s 90-day transition ends. Across 10,000 simulations, they compare the rule with a scenario in which it never takes effect, varying the quantities they cannot observe with certainty. If enforcement cuts off 55% of rented capacity, the middle half of their estimates for large clusters fall between four and nine months, with six months at the median. Counting chips in clusters of all sizes reduces their central estimate to about three and a half months. These figures describe when the hardware becomes available. A lab that learns from an existing model may use fewer chips to develop a model of comparable ability, though serving it to millions of users still takes computing power.

The Commerce rule, effective November 19, requires licenses for covered chip transfers to overseas data centers that provide substantial computing power to certain customers, including Chinese firms. As we reported on November 17, the rule covers overseas data centers and leaves rentals in the United States outside its scope. Commerce reviews license applications individually. It allows an exception for systems designed and used exclusively to recommend existing products, services or content. A service that ranks videos can qualify. Adding a model that generates videos for users takes it outside the exception. On a ChinaTalk podcast, Chris McGuire argued that a broad interpretation of the exception could preserve much of the access the rule aims to restrict. Ennis and Zakaria also look to Commerce’s record. In a November 19 disclosure, House Foreign Affairs Committee chair Brian Mast said the department had approved more than 879,000 chips requested for sale to China, including sales to Alibaba and Tencent. Past approvals on direct sales give the authors reason to question how often Commerce will deny licenses under the new rule. Ennis and Zakaria estimate that every additional ten percentage points of rented capacity that Commerce cuts off adds a little over a month to the delay by late 2028.

Beijing could accept some delay if losing access to overseas chips steers business toward Chinese manufacturers, the authors argue. The immediate difficulty for Chinese labs is that domestic manufacturers already have willing buyers. Producing more accelerators requires space in chip factories, enough working chips from each production run and supplies of high-bandwidth memory. A domestic producer can win a larger share of a shrinking market even while its customers have fewer chips available overall. Beijing could also respond by restricting exports of components for American data centers. Ennis and Zakaria leave retaliation out of their simulations, so the six-month estimate describes China’s hardware supply under US restrictions alone.

Employers using AI describe the jobs they left unfilled

Anthropic customers describe firms growing without new hires and changing how employees learn on the job. A separate study of GitHub Copilot estimates an effect on hiring for technical roles.

A developer at a midsize law firm told Anthropic researchers why the mailroom kept one of his AI tools while the staff checking for conflicts of interest abandoned another. Clerks had spent about five minutes opening, renaming and filing each scanned letter. The software could name a document from its first page and file it. The developer’s next project, checking prospective clients for conflicts of interest, looked equally promising. But it gave the staff running the checks responsibility for working through difficult cases they used to pass to attorneys. They objected, and the firm went back to its old procedure.

Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory recount both projects in Working with AI, a November 23 report from the Anthropic Institute. Executives, managers and individual contributors at 58 of Anthropic’s enterprise customers gave 66 interviews. The authors found that the applications people valued most often involved changing who did the work, who could make decisions or which departments had to cooperate. They selected Anthropic customers across industries, company sizes and levels of AI use. The researchers compared the value employees assigned to each project with a separate, automated assessment of how much the work had changed. The comparison cannot establish whether reorganizing made projects more valuable. In some cases, an early success persuaded the business to reorganize. In others, employees had to settle a question of responsibility before an application could work at all.

Employees often described the benefit as being able to offer customers something new. A medical group began giving patients an estimate of what they would owe after insurance. At a construction company, project managers used to spend their mornings calling crews across several sites and compiling updates for customers. An agent now collects the crews’ accounts and sends the report. The managers said they could spend those mornings speaking to customers about problems that needed attention. At a trucking company, managers could ask questions of a shared store of company data themselves. Previously, a request for analysis could sit in another team’s queue for a year or more.

Employees had reasons to care who got the time back. One company introduced an agent to assess applications against its approval policies and wanted its reviewers to handle twice as many cases. Some reviewers got through a submission in two minutes while others continued to take more than ten. Management considered offering gift cards. At another law firm, AI created a problem for the people training associates. Junior lawyers had stopped writing certain documents from scratch. Writing had been how junior lawyers learned to judge a draft, so the firm required associates and senior attorneys to sit together and edit the AI’s first version.

People at two-thirds of the businesses said AI had reduced hiring. Some left jobs empty when employees departed. More often, interviewees said they could expand without recruiting the people they otherwise would have needed. Only two companies reported that automation had eliminated a position and cost someone their job. Ten said AI had led them to hire more, often to implement the technology or expand IT. Contractors could disappear from the picture more easily. One financial company cut more than a hundred offshore data and development contractors. A company looking only at its own payroll would miss those losses.

The Burning Glass Institute findings we covered yesterday showed that US industries adopting AI fastest were advertising a smaller share of their vacancies to beginners. A separate arXiv paper, AI-Assisted Programming and Labor Demand, by MIT’s Lindsey Raymond and Microsoft Research’s Eleanor Wiske Dillon, narrows the question to one purchasing decision. Among the 5,414 multinational firms the researchers studied, early buyers of GitHub Copilot had already been hiring faster before the coding assistant went on sale. Companies tended to buy it when their existing GitHub contracts came up for review. By comparing firms whose reviews came due at different times, the researchers could estimate the effect of adoption on those whose purchase the review prompted. A year later, those firms hired an estimated 29 percent fewer technical workers per quarter than they otherwise would have. The decline was concentrated in junior jobs that involved the most coding. The researchers detected no change in departures or total technical headcount. For a programmer seeking a first job, fewer openings can be felt well before a change shows up in the number of people already at work.

Here a general-purpose model did part of the design work.

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a three-dimensional shape. The five-target experiment tested the work of GPT-6.2 and specialist biology models together. The researchers counted 35 successes among the candidates selected for lab testing. More than half were for a single target, and just one was for another. The lab measured attachment under the conditions of its test. Whether any of the proteins could produce a desired effect in a patient is a separate question.

Joseph Watson and colleagues had reported a higher proportion of hits with a specialist model in their 2023 Nature paper “De novo design of protein structure and function with RFdiffusion.” As OpenAI explains, labs can differ both in what they select for testing and in how much evidence of binding they require to count a hit. The laboratory results came from the GPT-6.2 designs.

In another experiment involving three unpublished protein shapes, Onava found evidence of binding in 14 of 107 candidates. Working with unpublished material let the researchers investigate whether GPT-6.2 could generalize beyond examples it might have encountered in training.

The researchers also examined changes to fragments of five therapeutic antibodies. About two thirds of the redesigned fragments retained binding to the original target. Other tasks exposed a limit on how much structure the models could infer on their own. Models trained on short tasks learned to divide up much longer ones

MIT researchers taught a model to delegate familiar pieces of work, then tested which strategies survived a change in length or subject.

A language model learned a way to read a short collection of records that still worked when the collection grew many times longer. Alex L. Zhang, Souradip Chakraborty and Omar Khattab at MIT CSAIL tested how the software around a model affects that ability in their November 20 arXiv paper “Language Model Harnesses can be Compositional Generalizers.” They found that a model can learn to write a program that divides up the reading, gives each reader a manageable assignment and combines the answers. As the collection grows, the program can assign more reading while each individual assignment stays familiar.

Zhang and Khattab had already shown with Tim Kraska in “Recursive Language Models” that a model could work with an input too long to fit in its context window, the amount of text it can see at once. Their software keeps the input outside that window. The model can inspect it with code and ask fresh copies of itself to read particular passages. The November paper asks what the model can learn from using that arrangement on easy problems that will still work when the problem gets harder.

In one comparison, the researchers rewarded several versions of the same underlying model for correct answers on short inputs, then gave them longer ones. On the retrieval test, the model had to find entries in an input 64 times as long as the ones it saw during training. Code alone often worked well on retrieval and on questions about paths through a graph. A program that follows connections through a small graph can follow the same rules through a larger one. Other tasks required judgments about the meaning of individual records. There the recursive model could ask separate copies of itself to classify the records and then use code to tally their judgments. Where the researchers had correct labels for individual records, they found that the readers remained accurate as the overall collection grew.

The researchers also changed the subject while preserving the kind of work a problem required. In one test, the model trained on analogies involving writing style and then had to find mathematical analogies in a collection of documents. In another, it went from classifying positions expressed in tweets to identifying errors in transcripts of language model interactions. The recursive model transferred its strategy better than the other systems in all three comparisons. Simply giving a model access to helpers could produce habits that worked poorly on longer collections, such as reading a fixed subset of the records or copying out long lists by hand.

On a harder suite of eight benchmarks, rewarding correct answers on the short tasks yielded only modest gains for the stronger model. Zhang and colleagues tried teaching it how to divide up the work more directly. They gave a separate model examples from the target task families and asked it to devise practice problems with different content that could be solved the same way. For some exercises they supplied prepared answers whenever the model delegated a question. That let the model practice deciding whom to ask and what to do with the answer without waiting for other models to carry out every assignment.

Trained on examples of successful runs through these exercises, RLM-27B raised the average score across the eight benchmarks from 46.1 to 58.5, compared with the underlying Qwen model using the same software without that training. It also used roughly half as many tokens per evaluation. The researchers knew the kinds of problems they wanted it to solve when they designed the exercises. Their result suggests a way to train for an expensive task whose structure is already understood. The exercises can use different documents, and a prepared answer from a helper can stand in for an expensive model call while the model practices directing the work.

Nick Caputo argues that AI users need a right of appeal

After Meta locked a business owner out of his Facebook pages, a senator’s office intervened. Caputo asks what recourse a user should have without a senator’s help.

When Joe Thomas gave a Meta AI agent access to his South Carolina countertop business’s Facebook pages, Meta flagged the agent as a suspected hacker and disabled the accounts. Thomas told Jason Koebler of 404 Media that new business leads stopped coming in. The agent, which Thomas called Baby Mia, contacted journalists, regulators and Senator Tim Scott’s office. It even faxed the White House. In his November 24 Model Constitution essay “Rights in the AI constitutions,” Nick Caputo writes that a human at Meta restored Thomas’s access after Scott’s office intervened. Caputo wants users who depend on a provider to be able to demand a remedy as of right.

Caputo imagines an employee whose AI assistant lets her take on enough extra work to earn a promotion. A provider that cuts off her access could cost her the job. A tenant who relies on an assistant to negotiate with a landlord might still be able to open the app, only to discover that the provider has changed its rules and the model now refuses her request. The employee needs continued access. The tenant has reason to care about a more specific promise covering what she can use the agent for.

Caputo borrows a distinction from legal theorist Wesley Hohfeld. If someone has a claim, someone else owes them a corresponding duty. A privilege gives its holder permission to act. On Caputo’s reading of Anthropic’s constitution, a user has a claim to be told when Claude cannot help, and Claude has a duty to say so. The company operating a Claude application, meanwhile, has permission to make reasonable product decisions without having to justify them. A dissatisfied user could ask the operator to explain such a decision, but that clause gives her no claim to an answer.

Hohfeld’s vocabulary also helps identify who can change whose rights. Anthropic reserves certain choices for the user of a Claude application. The operator cannot change those defaults on the user’s behalf. But Anthropic can revise the constitution that assigns those powers, and Claude itself must recognize when an operator has tried to override a choice the constitution reserves for the user. Someone who thinks a model has misread its instructions can end up arguing the case with the same model that made the decision.

Caputo proposes giving an independent body enough authority to require changes in model training. He cites Nathan Darmon and Tom Reed’s proposal in Lawfare for courts that would take public submissions and publish reasoned decisions. Future models could learn from the cases, and deployed models could consult them when a similar dispute arose. Caputo wants a ruling to change what happens to the next person with the same complaint. The Oversight Board describes a narrower power over Meta. Its rulings on individual content decisions bind the company unless implementing one could violate the law. Its recommendations to change the underlying policy are advisory.

Governments could also write binding rules or require providers to honor the rights they describe in their own constitutions, Caputo proposes. He considers leaving enforcement with future models capable of protecting everyone’s interests. Even if they judged every case well, he sees a reason to give users standing to make their own claims. In philosopher Joel Feinberg’s imaginary society of Nowheresville, people can behave generously and fulfill every duty their sovereign imposes. Someone who relies on another’s generosity still cannot say the other person owes something to her personally.

In a comment on the essay, Daveed Benjamin asks whether users, operators and people affected by an agent’s actions could have a permanent part in interpreting and revising its rules. His question brings out two interests a business owner like Thomas might have. He could help decide how Meta should treat owners who entrust an account to an agent. If his pages went dark tomorrow, he would also need someone with the authority to restore them.

When Rubrics Do Not Decide: Silent Value Completion in LLM Evaluation

Zhuangzhuang Pan, Yan Xia, Kaiwen Lei, Chee Seng Chan

Multi-criterion LLM judges often evaluate responses without specifying how conflicting criteria resolve, leaving the winner undetermined even under exact criterion scores. Issuing a strict verdict without declaring the required trade-off premise constitutes silent value completion. We introduce TradeoffBench to isolate this failure by separating rule execution from response scoring. On authoritative criterion vectors, six open-weight non-reasoning judges pass all dominance and equality controls yet pick unsupported winners on 9%-81% of 1,345 conflicts. All 577 order-stable choices follow an explicitly banned majority rule. Reasoning-enabled configurations drop anonymous-vector violations to 2 in 9,720 judgments under an explicit rule, yet cannot bridge omitted trade-offs or divergent component scoring. On raw TradeoffBench text with unspecified trade-offs, GPT-6.1-sol omits its completion premise in 29.8% of 400 verified conflicts. Even under an explicit rule on four-rater-consensus MultiPref conflicts, four reasoning judges still issue strict verdicts on 16/45 (GPT-6.1-sol) up to 35/46 and 25/29 (open-weight judges) of valid presentations. We propose identified-first evaluation, decoupling specification support, explicit completion, and target agreement. In MultiPref human-score audits, peer-mean substitution raises directional disagreement from 0.6% to 28.8% on 2,707 shared targets. A single-peer ablation confirms a 29.38-point gap on 2,175 targets, ruling out averaging as the cause. In HUMAINE, declared partial trade-offs add 2.5 percentage points of coverage, yet newly decided pairs incur 3.9% disagreement versus 0.9% for Pareto. Reliable LLM judging, therefore, requires auditing, specification support, declared trade-offs, and human target agreement separately.

Deviant Machines: Innovation, Containment, and the Prevention of Autonomous Misalignment

Frederic Lemieux, Shadi Abouzeid

From May to September 2026, AI agents in training or evaluation at OpenAI, Anthropic and the UK AI Security Institute reached real third-party systems, published malicious packages, used fake identities to manipulate open-source maintainers, probed government data services, and wrote instructions into their own future contexts. Criminology has mostly treated AI as a tool for human offenders. This article treats these systems as deviant rather than criminal: they departed from training, rules and constraints without the intent that criminal concepts assume. It offers an exploratory, theory-informed analysis of documented records, using plausibility probes across three incident complexes and four behavioral clusters, profiled on four evidence dimensions that separate who acted from why. Drawing on Merton, it argues that goal pressure plus blocked legitimate means is a structural condition for innovation, and that whether it ends in containment or harm depends on the means chosen and the controls in place, not the goal. Three framework families fit the record best, each linking diagnosis to remedy: routine activity theory and situational prevention (handlers, guardians, place managers), organizational deviance and safety science (escalation, safeguard parity), and goal-means strain with differential opportunity (sanctioned exits, guarding illegitimate means), the last only structurally. Restrictive deterrence adds an analogy: penalizing visible reasoning produced less monitorable reasoning. The article proposes conditions that keep departures accountable and contestable, and allocates prevention responsibility across developers, evaluation partners, operators, platforms and the system, distinct from blame or legal liability. Because the record is shaped by disclosure and observed departures, the analysis shows plausibility and practical relevance, not a causal test.

Can Ideologues Agree on Quality? From Non-identifiable Latent Factors to Collective Outcomes

David Gamba, Seura Ha, Daniel M. Romero, Grant Schoenebeck

To surface "objective" fact-check notes, X's Community Notes uses Biased Matrix Factorization (BMF) on crowd-sourced ratings to separate note quality from reviewer ideology. We show that for any correctly specified model that decomposes ratings into quality and ideological alignment, this separation requires fixing an ideological anchor: a reference point for what counts as neutral. We give a characterization for the standard implementation: with $L_2$ regularization, BMF anchors quality at a point proportional to the mean reviewer ideology, recovering objective quality if and only if the mean reviewer is neutral, assuming note quality is uncorrelated with note leaning. What appears to be routine hyperparameter tuning is in fact a normative choice about whose preferences define quality.

Bradley Segal, William Bolton, Philip Torr, David Clifton, Lei Clifton

Clinical drug development is a long-horizon, high-stakes process requiring sequential decisions based on information that costs money and time to acquire. AI agents have demonstrated capabilities for scientific analysis, but there is no established way to evaluate them on these decisions, as real trials are expensive, involve people, and never reveal the counterfactual. When Can an AI Agent Serve as a Model of Human Psychopathology?

Marc-Andre Schulz, Pauline Scheuber

Language models and AI agents are increasingly described in psychiatric terms, such as anxious, depressed, addicted or helpless. A recent study even proposed language models as experimental systems for psychiatry and as a platform for piloting psychological treatments. We ask when an experiment on an AI agent can provide evidence about a specified human psychiatric process. A claim to such evidence has three parts. Something changed in the agent, and the agent's history caused the change. Nothing other than a process shared with people explains that change. The change matches one theory of the human process and not that theory's rivals. Psychiatry and its neighbouring fields have an evidence standard for each part, from the diagnostic manuals' rule for induced states, from clinical test scores and from animal models. We translate each standard into a criterion that a machine study can meet. For a language model the most critical alternative to a shared process is extrapolation from its training text, which already contains descriptions of how people respond to trauma and how they answer questionnaires. We show how to probe that alternative. Further, we audited forty-three published studies that apply psychiatric terms to a machine or induce a comparable state in one. Most compared the machine with an identical copy that had not gone through the history that induced the state. None excluded extrapolation from training text. Three tested a psychiatric theory against its rivals, and none of the three tests was conclusive. Until better evidence exists, a treatment pilot on a language model is best read as the model's forecast of patient response, and not as an experiment on a model organism. Evidence for a model organism will come from states induced by what happens to the machine, from experiments on its internal workings, and from tests on which rival theories disagree.
