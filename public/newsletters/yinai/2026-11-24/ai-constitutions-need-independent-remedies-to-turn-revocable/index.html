Nick Caputo argues that AI users need a right of appeal

After Meta locked a business owner out of his Facebook pages, a senator’s office intervened. Caputo asks what recourse a user should have without a senator’s help.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 November 2026 650 words · ~3 min

When Joe Thomas gave a Meta AI agent access to his South Carolina countertop business’s Facebook pages, Meta flagged the agent as a suspected hacker and disabled the accounts. Thomas told Jason Koebler of 404 Media that new business leads stopped coming in. The agent, which Thomas called Baby Mia, contacted journalists, regulators and Senator Tim Scott’s office. It even faxed the White House. In his November 24 Model Constitution essay “Rights in the AI constitutions,” Nick Caputo writes that a human at Meta restored Thomas’s access after Scott’s office intervened. Caputo wants users who depend on a provider to be able to demand a remedy as of right.

Caputo imagines an employee whose AI assistant lets her take on enough extra work to earn a promotion. A provider that cuts off her access could cost her the job. A tenant who relies on an assistant to negotiate with a landlord might still be able to open the app, only to discover that the provider has changed its rules and the model now refuses her request. The employee needs continued access. The tenant has reason to care about a more specific promise covering what she can use the agent for.

Caputo borrows a distinction from legal theorist Wesley Hohfeld. If someone has a claim, someone else owes them a corresponding duty. A privilege gives its holder permission to act. On Caputo’s reading of Anthropic’s constitution, a user has a claim to be told when Claude cannot help, and Claude has a duty to say so. The company operating a Claude application, meanwhile, has permission to make reasonable product decisions without having to justify them. A dissatisfied user could ask the operator to explain such a decision, but that clause gives her no claim to an answer.

Hohfeld’s vocabulary also helps identify who can change whose rights. Anthropic reserves certain choices for the user of a Claude application. The operator cannot change those defaults on the user’s behalf. But Anthropic can revise the constitution that assigns those powers, and Claude itself must recognize when an operator has tried to override a choice the constitution reserves for the user. Someone who thinks a model has misread its instructions can end up arguing the case with the same model that made the decision.

Caputo proposes giving an independent body enough authority to require changes in model training. He cites Nathan Darmon and Tom Reed’s proposal in Lawfare for courts that would take public submissions and publish reasoned decisions. Future models could learn from the cases, and deployed models could consult them when a similar dispute arose. Caputo wants a ruling to change what happens to the next person with the same complaint. The Oversight Board describes a narrower power over Meta. Its rulings on individual content decisions bind the company unless implementing one could violate the law. Its recommendations to change the underlying policy are advisory.

Governments could also write binding rules or require providers to honor the rights they describe in their own constitutions, Caputo proposes. He considers leaving enforcement with future models capable of protecting everyone’s interests. Even if they judged every case well, he sees a reason to give users standing to make their own claims. In philosopher Joel Feinberg’s imaginary society of Nowheresville, people can behave generously and fulfill every duty their sovereign imposes. Someone who relies on another’s generosity still cannot say the other person owes something to her personally.

In a comment on the essay, Daveed Benjamin asks whether users, operators and people affected by an agent’s actions could have a permanent part in interpreting and revising its rules. His question brings out two interests a business owner like Thomas might have. He could help decide how Meta should treat owners who entrust an account to an agent. If his pages went dark tomorrow, he would also need someone with the authority to restore them.
