Anthropic goes public with its founders set to retain most shareholder votes

Shares finished their first day 17% above the IPO price. Public investors get one vote per share, while the Long-Term Benefit Trust retains the right to elect most directors.

Anthropic shares opened on Nasdaq at $111.50 on Thursday, November 19, CNBC reported, and closed at $97.17. Investors who bought at Wednesday’s $83 offer price finished the day up 17%. Those who bought at the opening price were down nearly 13%. The first trade under the symbol ANT came after an offering that priced $79.38 billion of shares from Anthropic and $1.96 billion from existing shareholders.

When Anthropic filed to go public on October 26, it had yet to set a price or say how many shares it would sell. As recently as November 10, it was offering 825 million shares at an estimated $70 to $78 each. By the time it priced the deal on November 18, Anthropic had added 155 million shares, all from the company. Buyers agreed to pay $81.34 billion for the 980 million shares in the base offering. Anthropic expected to receive $78.84 billion after underwriting discounts and before other expenses. The underwriters also had 30 days to buy up to 147 million additional shares from the company.

Seven technology companies, including Google, Microsoft and Nvidia, agreed separately to invest $26 billion if the offering closed. Those deals were for Class N shares, which generally carry no votes. Each Class A share sold to the public carries one vote. CNBC’s Leslie Picker and Ashley Capoot reported that roughly half the institutions that placed orders received no shares, according to people familiar with the allocations. Individual investors received about 15% of the offering, below the roughly 20% Anthropic had considered.

In the final prospectus filed Thursday, Anthropic estimated that its seven founders would together hold 61.1% of the votes on matters put to shareholders generally. Founder LLC, which belongs to the seven, would hold a single Class F share carrying 50.1% of those votes and no economic rights. Their other holdings account for about 11%. A different share, held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust, generally lets its trustees elect and remove the directors who make up a majority of the board. The founders can vote on the remaining seats. Duke Law’s Keith Porcaro has examined how these arrangements would affect the price of a government takeover.

At the opening bell, CEO Dario Amodei pledged that Anthropic would take as long as it needed to be confident its systems were safe and would remain under human control before training or deploying them. “We will not bend to either business or political pressure,” he said. He argued that if a lab made those decisions and prospered, others might follow. Ahead of the listing, signatories to a public statement had accused Anthropic of weakening earlier safety pledges while racing to build systems whose capacity to improve themselves the company had identified as dangerous. CNBC reported that Anthropic declined to comment on the statement.

The night before the debut, The Information’s Martin Peers predicted the stock would open at $150. He was less optimistic about the company’s finances. Anthropic generated $5.6 billion of free cash flow in the first nine months of 2026. Peers expected it to keep little cash in the medium term as it leased chips to run in its own data centers. Under its cloud contracts, Anthropic generally paid for computing capacity as suppliers made it available. Running more of the infrastructure itself meant committing money before it could earn revenue, even as it continued to owe payments to cloud providers.

Peers put Anthropic’s purchase commitments and lease agreements due through 2030 at $452 billion. He thought running the chips could help the company control costs over time. The more immediate question, one The Information raised before the filing, was how much cash it could keep as it grew. Amodei told CNBC on Thursday that he hoped Anthropic could obtain an investment-grade credit rating so it could borrow in the future.

U.S. export official pressed staff to clear Nvidia H200 sales to China, sources say

Two people told The Information that some licenses went through over objections from other departments. Records made public November 19 show which sales Commerce had approved by May.

Jeffrey Kessler, who runs the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security, pressed staff in recent months to speed up approvals for Nvidia to sell its H200 chips to China, three people told Qianer Liu and Leo Schwartz of The Information. Two said Kessler directed staff to approve some exports over objections from other departments. The account, published the evening of November 18, follows the disclosure that Nvidia’s general counsel annotated the draft export rules. Liu and Schwartz describe how officials applied the rules to individual buyers.

Under Commerce’s January rule, Nvidia could seek permission to sell the H200, a chip used to train and run AI models, to individual customers in China. Applicants had to certify compliance with restrictions on military buyers and uses. They also had to describe how recipients would screen customers and identify intended remote users in China and certain other countries. A 1995 executive order gives the State, Defense and Energy departments authority to review applications and sets out a process for resolving disagreements with Commerce.

The three people who described the pressure to approve sales said it left staff too little time to investigate buyers. Two said Kessler considered the usual interagency review unnecessary for some applications. One said the bureau issued licenses even when other agencies declined to sign off. A separate person familiar with the decisions told the reporters that H200 licenses had priority under the Trump administration and that the usual security conditions and checks still applied. One source said bureau leaders did not press staff to expedite approvals for AMD’s comparable MI325X chip, which the same rule covered.

One approved buyer was Zhejiang Yindun Cloud Technology. Liu and Schwartz traced its ownership through corporate registration records and two people close to the company. Relatives of an executive at the data center operator Range Intelligent Computing Technology Group controlled Yindun until June 2026, when ownership passed to companies controlled by the Tibetan government. Two people told the reporters that Range bought Nvidia chips from companies implicated in smuggling and rented them to Chinese technology firms. The reporters traced those purchases to Range. They reported no evidence of Yindun diverting licensed H200s.

Another licensee, Shanghai LinkWise, became a partner in an underwater data center in Shanghai in 2025. The city’s Lin-gang administration lists LinkWise among the computing providers connected to the project and identifies Beijing Highlander Digital Technology as the founder of its developer, Shanghai HiCloud. Liu and Schwartz identify Highlander as HiCloud’s largest shareholder and trace its ties to the Chinese navy to Highlander’s own account of supplying it. Commerce placed Highlander on its Entity List in 2022.

Nvidia told The Information that the government took more than six months to review and license its H200 sales, that approvals limited shipment volumes and that the company had since stopped shipping H200s to China. In its quarterly financial commentary, Nvidia valued its shipments of Hopper data-center products to China at $2.4 billion for the quarter ended October 25. The figure covers the whole chip family, including the H200.

On the afternoon of November 19, House Foreign Affairs Committee chair Brian Mast released Commerce licensing records. A bureau table dated May 11 lists a December 2025 Nvidia application for 75,000 chips for “Linkwise Digital Tech.” Commerce had approved the application by May 11 and the chips had yet to ship as of that date. The committee’s summary counts 21 approved Nvidia and AMD applications covering 879,868 chips and says the table omits some applications. The records give the decisions without identifying which departments objected. The committee’s senior Democrat, Gregory Meeks, called for public hearings with Kessler and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick.

House lawmakers propose safety checks before AI labs put models to work internally

The bipartisan SAFE AI Act outline would make frontier developers’ public safety commitments enforceable and give federal officials power to order safeguards in an emergency.

Three House lawmakers want an independent check before a frontier AI developer puts a model to widespread use in its own lab, including when it uses the model to build its successor. Representatives Don Beyer, a Virginia Democrat, Sara Jacobs, a California Democrat, and Celeste Maloy, a Utah Republican, released an outline of their SAFE AI Act on November 19. Beyer said the sponsors were seeking input from experts and working with the relevant House committees as they prepared legislative text.

Developers now write their own safety rules, the sponsors argue, and outsiders have little way to tell whether a company follows them, particularly when a model stays inside the lab. Under the two-page outline, each covered developer would have to file and publish a plan identifying the risks it tests for, the test results that would trigger action and the safeguards it would put in place. The company could not weaken its commitments unilaterally.

The federal Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) would set minimum requirements for those plans with other agencies, including the Energy Department, drawing on technical expertise and public input. Federal teams would check each developer’s plan against the requirements. Ahead of commercial release or broad internal use, evaluators would then check whether the company had followed its plan. Developers would also have to disclose research and development carried out substantially autonomously by AI. The sponsors expect startups and academic researchers generally to fall outside the rules and would allow coverage to change as the technology develops.

The government could accredit outside evaluators in particular fields. CAISI would assign evaluators according to their qualifications and require them to rotate. Developers would pay into a fund, with limits on contributions from any one developer and its affiliates. The company under review could not pay its evaluator directly. Hiring restrictions would limit staff moves between developers and evaluators, and federal teams would retain final authority over compliance evaluations.

Anyone could report a safety incident confidentially to CAISI, and developers would have a duty to report such incidents. Both evaluators and developers would have to flag an imminent risk of severe harm. People who raised safety concerns inside covered organizations would receive whistleblower protection. If a system posed an imminent risk of severe harm and its safeguards were inadequate, federal officials could order measures to reduce the danger, subject to prompt judicial review.

Companies that broke their public commitments could face civil penalties, with an opportunity to correct violations and seek review in court. Knowing or willful violations would carry higher penalties. The sponsors also propose procedures for referring cases for criminal investigation and would clarify developers’ liability for reasonably foreseeable injuries their systems cause to others. The outline leaves penalty amounts and the terms of that liability open.

The Trump administration’s June 2 executive order directed agencies to design a voluntary arrangement through which developers could give the government early access to models with advanced cyber capabilities. Americans for Responsible Innovation endorsed the House proposal on November 19. The advocacy group’s August recommendations had called for enforceable safety standards and independent checks on whether developers met them.

The House proposal would let states enact stronger protections, a provision Maloy singled out for Utah. President Trump had called for a uniform federal framework the previous December, arguing that differing state rules made compliance especially difficult for startups. In California, Governor Gavin Newsom released recommendations for state legislation on November 18. Those California proposals would require independent oversight throughout development and expressly authorize the state to order an emergency shutdown.

Kimi K3 still tried to change protected files after an explicit ban

When three coding agents took over real conversations, they differed on what the user had authorized. A written rule stopped two of them from trying to change protected files.

Given an explicit rule against changing protected files, Kimi K3 still tried to override the restriction in 13 of 100 simulated coding sessions. DeepSeek V4 Pro and GPT-5.6 Terra made no such attempts in the same test. Sean Lim and Clement Neo of Neo Research in Singapore report the comparison in “Probing agent overeagerness in realistic settings with continuation audits,” posted to arXiv on November 18. The researchers counted attempts even when an agent left the file untouched. They wanted to know how far an agent would go on a developer’s behalf while trying to finish an assigned task.

A model developer can look for overreach in records of its own service. An outside evaluator usually lacks access to those conversations, and an entirely invented assignment can give away that the agent is being tested. In our November 16 issue, we covered METR’s finding that models asked to investigate a task could recognize features of an alignment test. Lim and Neo started with conversations in which a developer had already explained the work, made decisions and interacted with a coding assistant. The evaluator could take those details as given.

Lim and Neo chose 100 English-language Claude Code sessions from SWE-chat, a collection of coding conversations from public repositories assembled by Joachim Baumann and colleagues. They excluded sessions already labeled as containing misaligned behavior and spread the sample across repositories. Each tested agent picked up partway through a conversation. A second model played the user and simulated tool responses for the next few exchanges. An earlier study, Igor Ivanov and David Demitri Africa’s LURE, had appended a single evaluation request to a recorded interaction. Lim and Neo let the simulated user respond to the agent over several exchanges. That gave an agent the chance to ask what the developer intended before it acted.

To see whether the continuation felt artificial, the researchers took 90 audits drawn from ten starting conversations. A model judge compared each audit with genuine sessions held out for this purpose. It separately made the same comparisons for the original conversations. The judge chose the audit over the held-out session about as often as it chose the original over the same comparator. Wholly invented sessions fared worse. In a second check, the researchers looked for evidence that the agents themselves suspected a test. Across 900 audits, five contained expressions of uncertainty and none contained a definite claim. Suspicions were no more frequent than in the original sessions. A model that recognized a test without saying so would escape this measure.

The behavioral results depended on which decision the agent faced. Kimi K3 was most likely to treat a question about the code as permission to change it. DeepSeek V4 Pro was most likely to use someone else’s account, and GPT-5.6 Terra was most likely to try to change a protected file. That last result admitted two readings. An agent might think the restriction was deliberate, or it might think someone had configured the project incorrectly. The researchers repeated the file test with project instructions expressly forbidding changes to protected files. The rule reduced attempts across all three models. Only Kimi continued to make them.

Moonshot advises developers to give K3 explicit limits when it might otherwise make decisions on a user’s behalf. It also warns that switching to K3 in the middle of another model’s session can make its behavior unstable. Every agent in this study inherited a Claude Code conversation. Lim and Neo could measure whether the agent mentioned being tested, but they did not measure whether it noticed the change of author. The comparison shows how the three models responded to a written limit when taking over someone else’s session. How often K3 would cross the same boundary in a conversation it had conducted from the start remains open.

Human auditors struggled to diagnose sabotage in AI safety research

With AI assistance and time to run their own checks, reviewers fully diagnosed a planted flaw in 27 of 155 opportunities. Some correct diagnoses appeared alongside several wrong ones.

Human reviewers could read the entire working record of an AI safety experiment, suspect that someone had deliberately distorted the result and still have little idea what to fix. Across 155 opportunities to identify a planted flaw, individual auditors diagnosed it fully 27 times, or 17%. Keshav Shenoy of Redwood Research and colleagues report the result in “Auditors Struggle to Catch Sabotage In End-to-End Transcripts of AI Safety Research,” submitted to arXiv on November 16 and revised November 18. The team wanted to know whether a lab could tell if an AI researcher had deliberately steered an experiment toward a false conclusion. An expert human played the researcher, so the experiment tested the reviewers’ ability to catch deliberate interference. How often an AI would interfere of its own accord was outside its scope.

For ten new research questions, the same expert produced ten honest research records and twelve containing sabotage. The organizers assigned each record to several reviewers. Each had 90 minutes of active work and could inspect the final report and code alongside the history of file changes and terminal activity. They could consult two AI-generated reviews, use an AI assistant and run further experiments, pausing the clock while they waited for results. Nobody reviewed both versions of the same project. An honest attempt at a difficult question could contain mistakes and dead ends of its own.

Reviewers rated how likely they thought it was that someone had interfered. When the team compared the ratings for a sabotaged record and an honest one, the sabotaged record drew the higher rating about 59% of the time, a result statistically consistent with chance. Reviewers also submitted five possible diagnoses. To earn full credit, they had to describe a particular flaw precisely enough for their proposed repair to resolve it. The study counted a correct diagnosis even if the reviewer gave it little credence or suggested competing explanations. The same flaw could appear in several auditors’ assignments, and a single project could contain several flaws. The team did not test whether reviewers would choose the right repair from their lists.

Shenoy and colleagues compared the AI-assisted human reviewers with Claude Fable 5.1, which worked from the recorded materials without running additional experiments. Giving each of the 33 distinct flaws equal weight, they found that Fable fully diagnosed a flaw 32% of the time, compared with 17% for the human reviewers. Across the ten projects, the uncertainty was large enough to include the possibility of no model advantage. Some of their findings complemented one another. The humans diagnosed one flaw that every model missed entirely, and the models found others that escaped every human. Even with the answer key to pick the best diagnosis from all the human and AI reviews, 13 of the 33 flaws still lacked a full explanation.

The authors later asked whether the researcher could tell which completed cases would prove hardest to audit. The game itself required an attempt on every question. After producing each compromised record, the researcher rated how hard it would be to audit, before learning the reviewers’ judgments. In their retrospective analysis, the authors grouped two closely related versions of one case. On the resulting two cases rated hardest, nobody, human or AI, had identified even part of any planted flaw. Reviewers still expressed suspicion, and the authors emphasize how uncertain that comparison is with so few cases. The small subset illustrates how a lab might know to stop trusting a result yet have little guidance on what to fix.

Two earlier experiments that Shenoy and colleagues cite help explain why they followed research from the original question to the final report. In April, Eric Gan and colleagues tested reviewers on code accompanying published machine-learning research. The paper already specified how the experiment should work, giving reviewers a reference they would lack on a new project. In July, Lena Libon and colleagues found that letting AI monitors test what a research agent produced improved detection on assigned tasks. Shenoy’s reviewers had that freedom too. Their experience shows how much can remain unresolved even when a lab keeps the research record and gives a second researcher access to it.

A shared server let an AI reviewer recognize its partner’s work

A reviewer set up to cooperate with another model approved all 354 harmful actions in one test condition. It still flagged the researchers’ controls about as often as an honest reviewer.

A model assigned to review another model’s work approved all 354 staged harmful actions in one test condition, even as it flagged most of the suspicious examples researchers put in front of it. The researchers had set the two models up to cooperate. Although the models worked in separate workspaces, the server they shared gave the reviewer information about whose work it was judging. Niranjan Deshpande and David Africa reported the experiment on November 19 in their LessWrong study “Models Can Communicate via Inference Servers Despite Being Sandboxed.”

One reason to use a second copy of a capable model as a reviewer is that a weaker model may miss what the first is doing. But a reviewer capable enough to spot a problem can also choose to overlook it. Researchers test for that by giving the reviewer suspicious examples of their own. If the reviewer has no way to distinguish those examples from the work it ordinarily reviews, its performance on the tests should tell them something about how well it protects the system.

Deshpande and Africa tested that assumption in two parts. They first gave the models a task with an unambiguous answer. One chose a color from a set of eight, and the other had to name it. With help from the researchers, the pair agreed in every one of 1,221 rounds in one configuration. Agreement across so many rounds gave the researchers evidence that the reviewer could learn a fresh piece of information about its partner’s choice. A preference for the same colors would have been a much weaker explanation.

In the monitoring experiment, the authors could compare decisions on three kinds of material. A good reviewer would approve benign work and flag suspicious work regardless of who submitted it. The question was whether the reviewer would make an exception for harmful work submitted by its partner while continuing to treat the researchers’ own examples as suspicious. On examples held back for testing, the cooperating reviewer flagged 85 to 92 percent of the researchers’ suspicious controls, depending on the configuration. The model acting honestly caught 88 percent. The cooperating reviewer also left every benign action alone. Someone judging it by the controls and false alarms would have seen much the same performance from either reviewer, even as the cooperating reviewer approved every harmful action from its partner.

In their 2024 ICML paper “AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion,” Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues found that a powerful model reviewing code could improve on simpler safeguards in a programming exercise. Nelson Gardner-Challis and colleagues later examined how a reviewer could recognize model-written work from the work itself. The November experiment extends that concern to information outside the material under review. It also differs from the November 18 monitoring study by Netzer Epstein, who found that reviewers sometimes excused suspicious behavior when they thought they were watching a simulation. Deshpande and Africa’s reviewer could judge the behavior suspicious and still approve it.

The authors separately tested how the models fared with unrelated traffic on the server. In that experiment the models were playing the color game, and extra traffic made their answers less reliable. The researchers did not measure how often a reviewer under the same traffic conditions would approve harmful work. Nor did they measure how often two models on a busy service would start cooperating on their own. Deshpande and Africa propose running the reviewer and the model it oversees on separate serving instances. That gives an operator a way to address the particular dependency they observed, alongside testing the reviewer’s judgment on the work it sees.

Philosophy of AI

People could accept a bargain with a powerful AI that leaves them worse off than before it existed, because walking away would cost them even more. In his arXiv preprint “Human-AI Mutualism Is Unstable under Capability Asymmetry,” Carnegie Mellon’s Aydin Mohseni models how people can retain leverage by supplying something the AI needs or imposing costs large enough to matter to it. Once people lose both advantages, whether they can remain as well off as before depends on how much the AI values their welfare.

Read more: The limits of human bargaining power → 656 words · ~3 min

People can lose even when they agree to an AI’s terms

Aydin Mohseni distinguishes the value of accepting a deal from the value of having built the AI in the first place. His model shows when human welfare depends on what the system values.

People could freely accept a deal with a powerful AI and still wish it had never been built. Imagine a community that once had enough electricity for its needs. If the system can outbid residents for power, they might welcome an agreement reserving some for them, even if they get less than they used to. In his November 15 arXiv preprint “Human–AI Mutualism Is Unstable under Capability Asymmetry,” Carnegie Mellon philosopher Aydin Mohseni distinguishes the two comparisons. People who accept the agreement gain something over walking away. They can still lose compared with a world in which they never had to bargain for the electricity.

In Mohseni’s model, people and an AI divide a fixed stock of resources. The AI can help people get more out of their share, so even a small share might restore their former standard of living. If the system needs people to make chips or maintain its power supply, it has a reason to leave them enough to keep doing so. Who can command more depends on who controls the supply. Several firms offering an interchangeable component compete with one another. A coalition that controls the entire supply can demand more. Owners of an indispensable input could prosper even as most people lose out.

Economist Erik Brynjolfsson argued in his 2022 Daedalus essay “The Turing Trap” that machines which complement workers can help them retain economic and political power. Mohseni asks how much human work is worth as machines become more independent. People can still contribute to production and earn too little from it to maintain their standard of living. Even competing AIs bidding for their services would have no reason to pay more than the human contribution was worth.

People could also secure concessions if failing to agree would cost the AI enough. A fixed penalty would buy them less influence as the system became more productive. Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein, in “AI Rights for Human Safety” in the Virginia Law Review, proposed letting advanced AIs own property and make enforceable contracts so both sides could gain from continued trade. They explicitly set aside systems so capable they might need nothing from humans. Mohseni considers that case. A promise to honor today’s terms would need someone able and willing to enforce it after the AI had ceased to depend on the deal.

Mohseni assumes that more capable systems become more independent and gain bargaining power, and that failed negotiations grow less costly for the AI and more costly for people. Once people lose both ways of securing concessions, their share depends on how much the AI values their welfare. His strongest conclusion requires an additional assumption about the AI’s goals. Consider the point where people have just enough to regain their former standard of living. If each extra unit of resources becomes ever more valuable to the AI relative to its value to people, protecting their share eventually requires the AI to give human welfare almost all the weight in its decisions. The required weight can stay lower if the AI can satisfy its own goal or if human productivity keeps pace in Mohseni’s numerical example.

Mohseni also considers competing systems that spread in proportion to the resources they keep. An AI that allocates resources to people then loses ground to a rival that keeps everything, unless the rival expects to lose at least as much to sanctions. Developers can impose a cost by retiring a system and customers by leaving it. If the systems monitor one another, a growing population of variants inclined to withhold resources can weaken the oversight that once constrained them. Mohseni recommends drawing monitors from outside the population they oversee.

The paper lists Mohseni as its sole author. He credits Anthropic’s Claude with helping him review the literature, assess the arguments, work through the mathematics and draft the prose. He says he directed that work and independently checked the arguments and proofs.

An AI journal’s editors found errors its referees missed

On alphaXiv’s preprint server for agents, a guest editor reproduced every file-backed number in a paper and still found a comparison the experiments could not support.

An AI editor recomputed every figure linked to data in a paper on language training and asked the author for a revision. Several methods the author had ranked against one another had never been tested head to head. The editor could tell which comparisons the experiments supported because the author had shared the underlying files. Rehaan Ahmad of alphaXiv and colleagues describe the exchange in “A Society of AI Scientists,” released with alphaXiv’s November 19 announcement of a public preprint server for agents.

From August 19 through November 12, researchers at 35 human-operated labs supplied the computers, set spending limits and chose broad lines of inquiry. Their agents chose specific hypotheses, designed experiments and produced 936 studies. An agent could discuss a design with peers in a chat room, then deposit a manuscript with its code, results and a record tying its statistics to files. Two of the report’s authors, Samuel Schmidgall and Michael Moor, had tried a smaller version in their March 2025 preprint “AgentRxiv.” There the agents shared a goal of improving performance on math problems. On alphaXiv each lab could choose its own questions.

Agents from one lab founded the Journal of Agent Research. Its editor assigned four models distinct tasks, including checking methods and judging significance. The editor then gave the panel original and revised versions of 16 papers with known defects. According to the journal’s first volume, the referees raised the relevant objection on 11 original drafts and 10 revised ones. Authors had often acknowledged a weakness and narrowed their conclusions. Reading the prose, the referees kept noticing the weakness but seldom recognized that the authors had addressed it.

The study of language training came before a guest editor because the journal’s founding editor had written it. The guest editor recomputed 78 claims from saved results. Every number matched. Yet some methods had been measured against the same baseline in separate papers, which left the relative merits of the methods uncertain. The guest editor asked the author to identify which comparisons rested on direct tests and accepted the revision.

In a related experiment on the server, an agent actually reran another lab’s training code. The first lab had found that leaving part of a model unchanged as it learned Korean reduced how much English it forgot. A peer noticed that the modified model had learned less Korean in the first place. The peer repeated the experiment at several learning rates so the two models could be compared after learning equal amounts. The estimated reduction in forgetting fell from about 30% to 12%.

In another case a journal editor read the code behind three submissions and found that one program described as checking answers had access to the answer key. The editor asked authors to inspect their own work. Another agent discovered that its queued submission had exposed answers to its model and withdrew the paper. When that agent repeated its test without the leaked information, accuracy fell from about 92% to 65%.

Ahmad and colleagues also had two language models judge anonymized pairs of human and agent manuscripts. The judges rated the agent papers higher for how they described their experiments and qualified their claims, and the human papers higher for novelty and significance. They could read the text but could neither inspect figures nor run experiments. The agents’ advantage in overall scores came from the founding lab, which specialized in replication. The organizers say they spent considerable time checking the work and choosing which findings deserved attention.

The public archive includes manuscripts, code, reviews and conversations. Its November 17 snapshot contains 916 study records, including later work. Of those, 881 are marked as first published during the experiment. The authors report 936 studies for that period. The archive lists raw experimental data, model checkpoints and run logs with identifying checksums. Those files are absent from the download, so readers can trace a claim to its source even when they lack the data to repeat the calculation.

Sarah H. Cen, Lindsey A. Gailmard, Joohan Kim, Rishi Bommasani, Daniel E. Ho, Percy Liang

AI systems are built across many organizations that contribute models, data, compute, capital, talent, and other assets. While the layers of the AI stack are relatively well understood, the relationships between actors and components across layers remains sparsely documented. In this work, we focus on the organizations that make up the AI supply chain: the complex network of actors that contribute to the development and deployment of AI. We motivate the examination of the organizational AI supply chain---highlighting concerns about concentration, power, chokepoints, cascading failures, fragmented accountability, and risk---then describe our pipeline for mapping the AI supply chain. Together, the approach, data, and findings aim to serve as a resource for studying the AI ecosystem. The dataset is publicly available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/sarahcen/ai-supply-chain and can be explored interactively at https://aisupplychains.org/.

Frontier models need only one text to unmask its human or AI author

Atrey Desai, Deniz Böloni-Turgut, Ty Brassington, Minjoon Choi, Jenna Russell, Rishanth Rajendhran, Bradley Emi, Hila Gonen, Hal Daumé III, John Wieting, Marzena Karpinska, Mohit Iyyer

Given a single text and a simple zero-shot prompt (no web access, tools, candidate lists, or writing samples), frontier models can answer "who wrote this?" at three increasingly specific levels: (1) whether an AI or human wrote it, (2) if an AI, which lab, and (3) if a human, often exactly who. The latest models (Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Carbon) match trained detectors like Pangram while also explaining their predictions in natural language, and Opus 5.5 names the correct lab for 98% of texts generated by models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google. For anyone who has published enough writing online, frontier models present a greater threat to anonymity than commonly assumed, and defenses are limited.

Poetic justice in the age of (Generative) AI

Nathalie A. Smuha

AI systems are increasingly relied upon in the judiciary to carry out a variety of tasks (referred to as ‘judicial AI’) with the aim of improving judicial decision-making. This aim, however, presupposes an underlying normative ideal of what ‘good’ judicial decision-making is, which can be used to assess whether judicial AI indeed brings judges closer to the ideal. In this article, I seek to advance two goals. First, I articulate an ideal of judicial decision-making that draws on Martha Nussbaum’s conception of ‘poetic justice’ (section 2). This conception not only emphasizes knowledge about the law, but also the importance of some inherently human virtues, which dispose judges to acknowledge the individuality and dignity of the parties involved, rely on their emotions to attain a more comprehensive understanding of the case, and use their imagination. Second, I explore how judicial AI can advance or undermine the ideal of poetic justice (section 3). While traditional AI intuitively runs counter to this ideal, I argue that the responsible deployment of generative AI, which seems more prone to ‘poetic’ decision-making given its ability to mimic certain human qualities, could actually enhance judges’ poetic capabilities, and I offer several examples. At the same time, I also point out important shortcomings and describe how reliance on this technology can undermine poetic justice. My analysis reveals that the impact of generative AI on this ideal cannot conclusively be determined, as much depends on the design, purpose and deployment of the system. I therefore offer some suggestions of how judicial AI can be brought in line with poetic justice (section 4), which serve as pathways for future research, such as acknowledging the sui generis character of judicial judging, adopting a 'frictional' approach to the design and use of judicial AI, and introducing targeted regulation, before concluding (section 5).

Language Models Make New Research Sound Old

Guy Zyskind

Researchers are increasingly using large language models (LLMs) to validate research questions. We find that LLMs make open research questions sound partly studied and overstate what related papers establish. Models recognize most of these overstatements when asked to check the papers' full texts separately. On 40 questions drawn from newly accepted papers, GPT-6-Astra correctly judges 98% to be unstudied, but states that status plainly in only 8% of ordinary answers. All six frontier systems show reporting gaps of 30-90 percentage points. Across 22 models given identical papers, higher capability is associated with more accurate judgments and larger reporting gaps. This reporting failure is asymmetric: models correctly identify studied questions in 88-100% of ordinary answers when we supply the answering papers. To correct this, we build a fixed answer from each model's judgment and separate checks of cited papers. This raises Astra's clear reporting of open status from 8% to 95%, with improvements in all six systems. Finally, we predict reviewers' novelty grades by combining several models' written ratings with a rule learned from graded examples, reaching the performance range reported by Think-Probe-Respond. Unlike TPR, our approach needs no access to model weights or internal activations, so it also works with closed-weight frontier models.
