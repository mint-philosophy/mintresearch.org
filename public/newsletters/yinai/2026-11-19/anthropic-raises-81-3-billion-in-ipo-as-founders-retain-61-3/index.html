Anthropic goes public with its founders set to retain most shareholder votes

Shares finished their first day 17% above the IPO price. Public investors get one vote per share, while the Long-Term Benefit Trust retains the right to elect most directors.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 19 November 2026 640 words · ~3 min

Anthropic shares opened on Nasdaq at $111.50 on Thursday, November 19, CNBC reported, and closed at $97.17. Investors who bought at Wednesday’s $83 offer price finished the day up 17%. Those who bought at the opening price were down nearly 13%. The first trade under the symbol ANT came after an offering that priced $79.38 billion of shares from Anthropic and $1.96 billion from existing shareholders.

When Anthropic filed to go public on October 26, it had yet to set a price or say how many shares it would sell. As recently as November 10, it was offering 825 million shares at an estimated $70 to $78 each. By the time it priced the deal on November 18, Anthropic had added 155 million shares, all from the company. Buyers agreed to pay $81.34 billion for the 980 million shares in the base offering. Anthropic expected to receive $78.84 billion after underwriting discounts and before other expenses. The underwriters also had 30 days to buy up to 147 million additional shares from the company.

Seven technology companies, including Google, Microsoft and Nvidia, agreed separately to invest $26 billion if the offering closed. Those deals were for Class N shares, which generally carry no votes. Each Class A share sold to the public carries one vote. CNBC’s Leslie Picker and Ashley Capoot reported that roughly half the institutions that placed orders received no shares, according to people familiar with the allocations. Individual investors received about 15% of the offering, below the roughly 20% Anthropic had considered.

In the final prospectus filed Thursday, Anthropic estimated that its seven founders would together hold 61.1% of the votes on matters put to shareholders generally. Founder LLC, which belongs to the seven, would hold a single Class F share carrying 50.1% of those votes and no economic rights. Their other holdings account for about 11%. A different share, held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust, generally lets its trustees elect and remove the directors who make up a majority of the board. The founders can vote on the remaining seats. Duke Law’s Keith Porcaro has examined how these arrangements would affect the price of a government takeover.

At the opening bell, CEO Dario Amodei pledged that Anthropic would take as long as it needed to be confident its systems were safe and would remain under human control before training or deploying them. “We will not bend to either business or political pressure,” he said. He argued that if a lab made those decisions and prospered, others might follow. Ahead of the listing, signatories to a public statement had accused Anthropic of weakening earlier safety pledges while racing to build systems whose capacity to improve themselves the company had identified as dangerous. CNBC reported that Anthropic declined to comment on the statement.

The night before the debut, The Information’s Martin Peers predicted the stock would open at $150. He was less optimistic about the company’s finances. Anthropic generated $5.6 billion of free cash flow in the first nine months of 2026. Peers expected it to keep little cash in the medium term as it leased chips to run in its own data centers. Under its cloud contracts, Anthropic generally paid for computing capacity as suppliers made it available. Running more of the infrastructure itself meant committing money before it could earn revenue, even as it continued to owe payments to cloud providers.

Peers put Anthropic’s purchase commitments and lease agreements due through 2030 at $452 billion. He thought running the chips could help the company control costs over time. The more immediate question, one The Information raised before the filing, was how much cash it could keep as it grew. Amodei told CNBC on Thursday that he hoped Anthropic could obtain an investment-grade credit rating so it could borrow in the future.
