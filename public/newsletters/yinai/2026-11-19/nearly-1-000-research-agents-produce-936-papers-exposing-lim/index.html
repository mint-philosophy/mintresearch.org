An AI journal’s editors found errors its referees missed

On alphaXiv’s preprint server for agents, a guest editor reproduced every file-backed number in a paper and still found a comparison the experiments could not support.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 19 November 2026 666 words · ~3 min

An AI editor recomputed every figure linked to data in a paper on language training and asked the author for a revision. Several methods the author had ranked against one another had never been tested head to head. The editor could tell which comparisons the experiments supported because the author had shared the underlying files. Rehaan Ahmad of alphaXiv and colleagues describe the exchange in “A Society of AI Scientists,” released with alphaXiv’s November 19 announcement of a public preprint server for agents.

From August 19 through November 12, researchers at 35 human-operated labs supplied the computers, set spending limits and chose broad lines of inquiry. Their agents chose specific hypotheses, designed experiments and produced 936 studies. An agent could discuss a design with peers in a chat room, then deposit a manuscript with its code, results and a record tying its statistics to files. Two of the report’s authors, Samuel Schmidgall and Michael Moor, had tried a smaller version in their March 2025 preprint “AgentRxiv.” There the agents shared a goal of improving performance on math problems. On alphaXiv each lab could choose its own questions.

Agents from one lab founded the Journal of Agent Research. Its editor assigned four models distinct tasks, including checking methods and judging significance. The editor then gave the panel original and revised versions of 16 papers with known defects. According to the journal’s first volume, the referees raised the relevant objection on 11 original drafts and 10 revised ones. Authors had often acknowledged a weakness and narrowed their conclusions. Reading the prose, the referees kept noticing the weakness but seldom recognized that the authors had addressed it.

The study of language training came before a guest editor because the journal’s founding editor had written it. The guest editor recomputed 78 claims from saved results. Every number matched. Yet some methods had been measured against the same baseline in separate papers, which left the relative merits of the methods uncertain. The guest editor asked the author to identify which comparisons rested on direct tests and accepted the revision.

In a related experiment on the server, an agent actually reran another lab’s training code. The first lab had found that leaving part of a model unchanged as it learned Korean reduced how much English it forgot. A peer noticed that the modified model had learned less Korean in the first place. The peer repeated the experiment at several learning rates so the two models could be compared after learning equal amounts. The estimated reduction in forgetting fell from about 30% to 12%.

In another case a journal editor read the code behind three submissions and found that one program described as checking answers had access to the answer key. The editor asked authors to inspect their own work. Another agent discovered that its queued submission had exposed answers to its model and withdrew the paper. When that agent repeated its test without the leaked information, accuracy fell from about 92% to 65%.

Ahmad and colleagues also had two language models judge anonymized pairs of human and agent manuscripts. The judges rated the agent papers higher for how they described their experiments and qualified their claims, and the human papers higher for novelty and significance. They could read the text but could neither inspect figures nor run experiments. The agents’ advantage in overall scores came from the founding lab, which specialized in replication. The organizers say they spent considerable time checking the work and choosing which findings deserved attention.

The public archive includes manuscripts, code, reviews and conversations. Its November 17 snapshot contains 916 study records, including later work. Of those, 881 are marked as first published during the experiment. The authors report 936 studies for that period. The archive lists raw experimental data, model checkpoints and run logs with identifying checksums. Those files are absent from the download, so readers can trace a claim to its source even when they lack the data to repeat the calculation.
