Cass Sunstein's sixteen notes toward an AI Regulatory Commission

Sunstein wants a five-member commission modelled on the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, with rulemaking, disclosure, penalty and emergency powers; the Supreme Court's June ruling means it probably cannot be independent, and the House bill already on file puts most of the same powers inside the Commerce Department.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1437 words · ~7 min

Cass Sunstein, the Robert Walmsley University Professor at Harvard and Administrator of the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs from 2009 to 2012, published "The AI Regulatory Commission" on his Substack on September 11, under the subtitle "Needed Now." The post runs to 640 words, most of them in sixteen numbered design notes. It opens by naming the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the Environmental Protection Agency and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, then states its claim: "The United States needs an AI Regulatory Commission (AIRC), and it needs it now." His reasons are current risks and growing public concern. The hard questions, he writes, are about design, since "The AIRC could be relatively weak; it could be extraordinarily powerful," and he twice calls his notes "preliminary, incomplete, and tentative."

He builds the design on the NRC, which his first notes contrast with the single-headed EPA. Note 3 argues for a multimember body with a chair because it deliberates more and brings more perspectives to the top: "five is a good number." The NRC's founding statute, 42 U.S.C. 5841, gives it five members, no more than three from one party, staggered five-year terms, and removal "for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office." That formula is word for word the Federal Trade Commission provision the Supreme Court struck down on June 29 in Trump v. Slaughter. Chief Justice Roberts, writing for a 6-3 majority, held that the FTC's for-cause protection violates the separation of powers, added, "If anything more is left of Humphrey's, the Court overrules it," and named the Federal Reserve as a possible exception. Sunstein's note 4 puts the decision in his own words: the Court has "thrown the whole idea of independent agencies into the constitutional garbage heap," Federal Reserve excepted. He argues for independence anyway, to reduce political pressure, then concedes in note 5 that "It is unlikely (but not impossible) that Congress has the constitutional authority" to grant it. His fallback is a public presidential promise to honor for-cause protection for the commissioners: "This agreement would not be enforceable, but still, it could be important."

Sunstein had written about that ruling on the day it came down. In a New York Times column of June 29, excerpted on his Substack the next morning, he wrote that "the unitary executive is now the law of the land," recalled being at the Justice Department in the early 1980s alongside John Roberts and Samuel Alito when the theory took hold, and concluded, "Forty years later, I now think we were wrong," because they had overlooked the value of stability and the dangers of politicizing everything. He named the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and the Consumer Product Safety Commission as agencies whose decisions "are suddenly subject to presidential control" and warned that for technical bodies whose decisions should not be political, that could be dangerous. The September post takes the NRC as its template without returning to that warning or saying how a commission answerable to the President would avoid the danger he described in June.

Several of the remaining notes add detail. Note 15 asks for explicit authority to issue "emergency rules and orders against imminent or catastrophic threats," even though, as Sunstein acknowledges, such power could already exist under the Administrative Procedure Act's good-cause exception, 5 U.S.C. 553(b)(B), which lets an agency skip notice and comment when it finds them "impracticable, unnecessary, or contrary to the public interest"; he prefers an explicit grant, with judicial review. Note 14 would generally require the commission's rules to weigh costs and benefits, the analysis he oversaw at OIRA, and note 7 would require a report on risks and dangers within 90 days of the commission's creation, possibly in classified and unclassified versions. Only two notes end in "(This is not clear.)": whether the commission should be funded by a legal levy on AI companies, and whether it should have licensing authority. On what the commission would regulate the post says nothing; it contains no definition of a covered system, no compute or revenue threshold, and no mention of models, frontier developers or open weights.

Congress has already written most of these powers into a bill and lodged them elsewhere. The FRONTIER Act, H.R. 9925, introduced on July 23 by Representative Jay Obernolte with Lori Trahan and four co-sponsors, prescribes its regulations under section 553 of the APA, imposes disclosure duties, sets a "civil penalty of not more than $1,000,000 per violation," and lets the Secretary of Commerce issue emergency orders "suspending or restricting a frontier developer's development, deployment, or internal use of a frontier model" on a written finding of imminent catastrophic risk. All of it sits inside the Department of Commerce, much of it under a new Under Secretary of Commerce for AI Security, and the bill answers the scoping question Sunstein leaves open: a "large frontier developer" has more than $50 million in gross revenues and at least $1 billion in AI-related development spending over 36 months, and a "very large" one crosses $5 billion and $10 billion. The Great American Artificial Intelligence Act discussion draft that Obernolte and Trahan released on June 4 likewise created no new agency; it would raise the Center for AI Standards and Innovation's budget from $15 million to $100 million a year, and Trahan told Tech Policy Press that "That agency has to be the credible federal counterpart to the frontier labs." Sunstein's post mentions none of this and does not say why a new commission should hold the powers the bill gives to Commerce.

Sunstein cites no earlier proposal, though there have been several. Ryan Calo, of the University of Washington, made the case for a Federal Robotics Commission at Brookings in September 2014, but his body "would not 'regulate' robotics in the sense of fashioning rules regarding their use"; it would be a small interdisciplinary staff advising other agencies, lawmakers and courts. Andrew Tutt's "An FDA for Algorithms," in the Administrative Law Review in 2017, asked for far more: "federal uniformity, expert judgment, political independence, and pre-market review," with some classes of algorithm barred from sale without agency approval. Daniel Carpenter and Carson Ezell tested that licensing analogy, the question Sunstein's note 10 leaves undecided, against frontier AI in an August 2024 paper and concluded that "approval regulation, simplistically applied, would be inapposite for frontier AI risks," partly because the regulated product is so hard to define. In Congress, the Blumenthal-Hawley framework of September 2023 made a licensing regime under an independent oversight body its first principle; it was never enacted.

Sunstein posted the essay to X twice. On September 12 he wrote that "Congress should create, in the coming months, an AI Regulatory Commission," and on September 13 he framed the case as a presumption overcome: "Strong presumption against creating any new regulatory agency, but for AI right now," he wrote, "the presumption is overcome." Gillian Hadfield, who set out the regulatory-markets model with Jack Clark in a 2023 paper, replied that "I don't think a regulatory agency is the right route," because regulatory domains differ too much and a single body would be "like an agency to rule them all." Under her model, governments license private regulators scoped to particular risks and require regulated firms to buy their services, the scheme she identified as the FRONTIER Act's Independent Verification Organizations. She also argues that no institution can fix the main problem yet, because "we don't have the science or engineering of effective testing and verification"; a federal agency, she allowed, "could orchestrate licensing bodies but it can't be source of regulations per se." Michael Rappaport objected on X that since "agencies often produce harmful regulations that reduce competition and reduce safety," Sunstein's "recommendation would be more persuasive if he addressed the government failure problem."

Two days after the post went up, the Speaker of the House said Congress would not lead. Mike Johnson told CNN's State of the Union on September 13 that lawmakers must "resist Congress jumping in and imposing some sort of emergency moratorium," and told NBC's Meet the Press that he would recall members for a vote "if we had a solution," adding that "Congress is obviously less qualified than the people who are pushing this frontier"; Axios reports he had been urged that week to cancel the House recess to pass AI safeguards. Sunstein's other premise, public concern, has survey support: in an Annenberg Public Policy Center survey of 1,330 U.S. adults released in May, 65 percent said government has done too little to regulate AI, including 77 percent of Democrats and 53 percent of Republicans.
