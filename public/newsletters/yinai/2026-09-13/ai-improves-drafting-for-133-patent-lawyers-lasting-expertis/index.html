AI improved patent drafting; unaided advantages concentrated among senior lawyers

Junior lawyers had the largest estimated gains with a drafting assistant. After three months, experienced participants outperformed comparable controls on a task where AI was prohibited; juniors showed no average advantage.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1499 words · ~7 min

Patent lawyers given an AI drafting assistant produced better applications, with the largest estimated gains among juniors. After three months, senior participants also outperformed comparable controls at correcting an existing application under instructions to work without AI; juniors showed no average advantage. David Autor of MIT and colleagues report this in Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise? Evidence from a Three-Month Field Experiment in Patent Drafting, NBER Working Paper 35720, published September 7. Their randomized trial involved 133 practicing lawyers at eleven US intellectual property firms.

The authors recruited through Google's supplier relationships: eleven of 24 firms approached agreed to participate. Within each firm, researchers randomly assigned lawyers to InFlow, an unreleased Google Labs writing tool, or a control group, aiming for twice as many AI participants. InFlow could generate and revise text and critique inconsistencies. Both groups could take basic AI training; treated lawyers received additional instruction on the tool. The staggered trial ran from May 2025 to February 2026, with three months of AI access for treated participants. InFlow's features changed during the study.

At ten and ninety days, lawyers drafted dependent claims, which add conditions to a main patent claim, and a detailed description from invention materials. At ninety days they also marked up a deliberately flawed, AI-generated application, with AI use prohibited. That exercise emphasized evaluating and correcting existing work. Of the 133 randomized lawyers, 105 completed the first drafting task, 98 the second and 91 the redlining task. Redlining was introduced after the first firm had finished; completed submissions came from nine firms. Two patent attorneys from an outside firm, unaware of treatment assignments, scored each submission on enforceability, technical accuracy, strategic ambiguity, completeness and clarity, using a rubric developed with Google's in-house counsel. The ambiguity score concerned deliberate breadth in a patent's wording, preserving future enforcement options.

AI access raised drafting scores by about a third of a standard deviation, measured against the spread of control-group scores, at both assessments, mostly by reducing poor work. At ten days, the chance of a bottom-quintile score fell by 19 percentage points. Estimated gains at ten days for lawyers with under seven years' experience were more than twice those for seniors, although the difference between their treatment effects was not statistically clear. Juniors reported saving 18 minutes on a task that took controls about 112 minutes; the study found no statistically clear time saving among seniors. Assisted juniors' speed and quality approached those of senior lawyers. Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence, by Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang in Science in 2023, similarly found greater gains for weaker writers among 453 professionals doing occupation-specific tasks.

On redlining, the treated group outscored controls by 0.32 standard deviations; seniors' estimated advantage was 0.45, while juniors' average difference was near zero. Treated juniors had fewer mediocre scores and more poor ones, alongside an imprecisely estimated increase in good scores. The authors suggest AI may have supported learning for some juniors and substituted for practice for others. The experiment did not establish that explanation or measure changes in each lawyer's skill: partner firms had rejected a baseline assessment. Interpreting the endline differences as learning therefore depends on balance between the randomized groups. Available pretrial measures, including experience and confidence, showed no detected imbalance, but did not measure initial skill. The study did not follow skill retention after the intervention ended.

The authors used NotebookLM to explore how the redlines differed. Treated seniors more often rewrote whole sections to remove language limiting a patent's commercial scope and explained the legal principles behind their edits. Juniors tended to work sequentially through stylistic fixes, sometimes running out of time before reaching the claims, and flagged substantive defects without correcting them. That pattern also appeared among control juniors. It does not establish that AI taught juniors a habit of issuing instructions instead of executing edits. The analysis had no preregistered hypotheses or estimates of the behaviors' frequency.

The authors separately asked Gemini 2.5-flash to score every submission using the same rubric as the attorneys. Its evaluations generally produced larger treatment-control gaps, but agreed only weakly with the human raters on the ninety-day tasks. The main findings rely on the blinded attorneys' assessments. Supplementing them with model ratings broadly preserved the redlining pattern, while offering a less reliable check on the magnitude of the effect.

The authors propose two possible mechanisms. AI practice may make writers more willing to replace existing prose and explain their reasoning. Alternatively, delegating text generation may free experienced lawyers to concentrate on strategy while reducing juniors' opportunities to develop that capacity through practice. Four post-experiment interviews, split between juniors and seniors, described different uses: seniors treated the tool as a source of first drafts or a check on their reasoning; juniors emphasized getting past an empty page. Treated juniors also reported greater satisfaction. Those accounts did not predict an average advantage on the redlining task. As the authors write, "Raising measured performance and building expertise are distinct objectives." They expect unaided judgment to remain necessary in client meetings and courtrooms.

Compliance with the redlining restriction could not be directly enforced: privacy commitments prevented observation of individual tool use. A stylistic screen using NotebookLM, supplemented by manual review, flagged 15 submissions as possible noncompliance, deliberately erring toward overclassification. These were suspicions, not confirmed instances of AI use. Direct usage evidence covered only one redlining feature at one firm; it could not rule out other AI tools. Excluding the flagged submissions reduced the senior estimate to 0.39 standard deviations, with a p-value of 0.06 compared with 0.02 in the main analysis. The direction persisted, with greater uncertainty and without meeting the conventional 5 percent significance threshold. The overall redlining advantage also became statistically uncertain when flagged submissions were excluded.

Evidence from everyday patent work was less precise. Survey responses from 68 participants suggested faster completion and fewer revisions, but the estimates could not establish an effect on workplace efficiency. The estimated completion-time saving was about three-quarters of a week from a control baseline just over five weeks, with wide uncertainty. The May 2025 preregistration planned a maximum of 350 participants and an additional comparison of actual Google patent drafts against firms' earlier work, scored by Google's counsel. The paper does not report that comparison. Its authors also caution that firms serving one sophisticated client do not represent professional services generally, and that expertise develops over years.

In the SSRN paper Artificial Intelligence and Human Legal Reasoning, Nicholas Bednar and colleagues at Minnesota studied about 100 law students. Those who used AI for an initial synthesis memo later outperformed controls on an unassisted application task, contrary to the researchers' preregistered expectation of harm. Earlier research with employed practitioners measured different outcomes: Colleen Chien and Miriam Kim's Generative AI and Legal Aid, in the Loyola of Los Angeles Law Review, gave 91 legal aid professionals access to tools and randomized additional support; outcomes came from surveys. Autor and colleagues claim their study is the first in law to assess skill acquisition among practicing lawyers.

In How AI Impacts Skill Formation, published by Anthropic in January, Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin reported that developers learning a Python library with AI averaged 50 percent on an immediate quiz, against 67 percent among those coding by hand. In Hamsa Bastani and colleagues' 2025 PNAS paper Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning, unrestricted GPT-4 access raised practice grades by 48 percent, followed by 17 percent lower grades when access ended. A tutor designed to give hints instead of answers largely avoided that penalty.

In the CEPR paper The Generative AI Learning Penalty: Evidence from Chinese Secondary Education, David Strömberg and colleagues followed 26,811 students for thirty months. They report that the full exam penalty associated with AI adoption emerged after about two years. They used differences in adoption timing to compare students; access was not randomly assigned. Autor and colleagues identify time spent practicing as a possible explanation across settings; neither study experimentally varied practice time.

Google funded the experiment's direct costs. Five coauthors disclosed Google employment and equity interests, and Josh Martin disclosed paid contracting. Autor also acknowledged support from the Google Technology and Society Visiting Fellows Program.

Writing at Patently-O on September 9, Dennis Crouch emphasized avoiding poor work and asked how attorneys could use AI while building skill. After NBER circulated the paper on September 13, Kevin Bryan of Toronto's Rotman School read it as evidence that tacit knowledge complements AI. His further claim of worse skill erosion among less skilled workers goes beyond the study's finding of no detectable average junior decline. Matt Beane of UC Santa Barbara, whose book The Skill Code the paper cites, connected the result to his research on surgical apprenticeship and unequal opportunities to build skill. The next morning, September 14, Tyler Cowen argued that task allocation would change over time and randomized trials could miss gains from those adjustments.

Sources & documents

  • Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise? Evidence from a Three-Month Field Experiment in Patent Drafting (NBER Working Paper 35720, full PDF) — The randomized trial supplies the drafting and redlining results, completion counts, blinded assessments and seniority comparisons. It lacked a baseline skill test and post-intervention retention follow-up; subgroup contrasts, exploratory explanations and suspected noncompliance require caution, with the senior redlining estimate falling to 0.39 standard deviations and p = .06 after flagged submissions were excluded. Supplemental model ratings agreed weakly with expert ratings and do not independently validate the effect size.
  • Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise? (NBER landing page, abstract, disclosures and metadata) — The NBER record dates publication to September 7 and discloses five coauthors’ Google employment and equity interests and Josh Martin’s paid contracting. The paper’s acknowledgments separately identify Google’s funding of direct costs and Autor’s visiting-fellows support.
  • Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence (Noy and Zhang, Science) — The Science abstract and editor’s summary describe 453 professionals performing occupation-specific tasks and larger benefits for weaker writers. The comparison is limited to those summaries, rather than a new analysis of the full study.
  • AI & Expertise research with patent lawyers (AEA RCT Registry entry AEARCTR-0015823) — The preregistration, submitted May 12 and first published May 21, 2025, planned up to 350 participants and an additional comparison of actual patent drafts with earlier firm work. That component was not a separately randomized treatment arm and is not reported in the paper; the registry’s status label does not establish whether the completed trial occurred.
  • Artificial Intelligence and Human Legal Reasoning (Bednar, Cleveland, Erbsen and Schwarcz, SSRN) — The SSRN abstract reports higher unassisted application performance and no downstream comprehension decline, contrary to the preregistered expectation. The approximate 100-student sample comes from the NBER paper’s discussion; the comparison otherwise stays within the abstract and metadata.
  • Generative AI and Legal Aid: Results from a Field Study and 100 Use Cases to Bridge the Access to Justice Gap (Chien and Kim, Loyola of Los Angeles Law Review) — The publisher’s abstract describes 91 legal aid professionals, access to AI for all participants, randomization of additional support and survey-based outcomes. The cohort is not described as consisting exclusively of attorneys.
  • How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills (Anthropic; Shen and Tamkin) — Anthropic’s January 29 report describes 52 mostly junior Python engineers learning an unfamiliar library and the immediate quiz averages of 50% with AI and 67% without. It measures immediate comprehension, not long-term retention, and the qualitative interaction patterns do not establish causal mechanisms.
  • Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics (PNAS, via PMC) — The article’s abstract and significance statement report a 48% practice improvement, a 17% deficit after access ended and mitigation with teacher-designed hints. The comparison relies on those sections rather than a full-paper analysis.
  • The Generative AI Learning Penalty: Evidence from Chinese Secondary Education (CEPR DP 21577, via RePEc) — The CEPR-supplied RePEc abstract and metadata describe 26,811 students followed for thirty months and a full exam penalty emerging after about two years. Adoption was staggered rather than randomized; the comparison does not add a standardized effect size beyond the abstract.
  • Who Keeps the Skill: A Randomized Trial of AI Patent Drafting at Eleven Firms (Patently-O) — The publicly available portion of Crouch’s September 9 article emphasizes avoiding poor work while asking how lawyers can build skill with AI. The discussion does not rely on the membership-only remainder.
  • NBER announcement post on X — NBER’s September 13 post establishes renewed circulation of the paper. The research itself was published September 7.
  • Kevin A. Bryan on the paper (X) — Bryan interprets the result as evidence that tacit knowledge complements AI. His stronger claim about average erosion among less skilled workers goes beyond the study’s near-zero, imprecisely estimated junior effect.
  • Matt Beane on the paper (X thread) — Beane connects the paper to his work on surgical apprenticeship and unequal opportunities to develop skill. That connection is his interpretation, supported by the paper’s citation of The Skill Code, rather than a new causal finding.
  • Does AI assistance enhance or erode expertise? (Marginal Revolution) — Cowen’s next-morning response on September 14 argues that task allocation will adjust and that randomized trials can miss gains from those changes. It is a follow-up to the Sunday discussion.
  • Bednar, Cleveland, Erbsen and Schwarcz on AI and Human Legal Reasoning | Legal Theory Blog — An April 16 reproduction of the abstract for the earlier Minnesota law-student experiment. The authors report no detected decline in subsequent comprehension and better unassisted application performance after initial AI use. The primary paper is linked separately through SSRN.
