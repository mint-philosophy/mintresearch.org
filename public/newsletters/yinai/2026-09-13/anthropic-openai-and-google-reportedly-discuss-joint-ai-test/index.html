Yesterday in AI · 13 September 2026

Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have held standards-body talks since July, The Information reports

A working group below chief-executive level has met regularly on Demis Hassabis’s FINRA-style proposal. Sam Altman told staff the labs would probably have to proceed without Washington, Leo Schwartz reports; the talks have produced no reported agreement on a body’s structure.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1383 words · ~7 min

The Information’s Leo Schwartz reported on Sunday, September 13 that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have been discussing a standards body for the AI industry since July. A working group of representatives below chief-executive level has met regularly, according to people familiar with the matter, and one said it met again in the past week. The talks began before Dario Amodei’s Saturday call for coordination on testing and auditing. Schwartz also reports, citing a person familiar with the remarks, that Sam Altman told an OpenAI companywide town hall earlier in the week that he supported an industry testing and auditing organization but expected the major labs to create it without US government support. He had hoped Washington would back a body, but industry consensus was lacking and some companies opposed the idea; he now favored trying to build something with other labs. A Reuters summary of Bloomberg’s earlier report on the meeting also described Altman as open to pacing development alongside other companies, though some might not agree. Schwartz names no working-group participant and reports no agreed structure, funding or timetable. The article carries no comment from the three companies.

Schwartz clarified the subject of the talks on X after Gary Marcus presented the scoop as confirmation that the companies had discussed Amodei’s ideas in advance. They were discussing Hassabis’s proposal, Schwartz replied; Marcus corrected his post five minutes later. Google DeepMind co-founder Demis Hassabis published that framework on July 14. He proposed a federally supervised public-private body resembling the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, or FINRA, funded mainly by industry and governed with independent technical experts and open-source representatives. Labs would initially submit models voluntarily up to 30 days before release; after the assessment process proved effective, passing it could become a condition of US deployment. Tests would be updated regularly, perhaps quarterly, and the body would eventually develop undisclosed tests independently of the labs to reduce overfitting. Its responsibilities could extend to coordinating a development slowdown. His benchmarks would define which models count as frontier-class, covering qualifying models regardless of country or open-source status while exempting less capable systems. The body would also work with federal agencies and national laboratories on national-security testing and with developers on serious post-release vulnerabilities. Hassabis pointed back to the proposal when endorsing Amodei on Saturday. Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark had welcomed it on July 15, saying frontier labs agreed that third parties should test models and develop standards. His endorsement did not establish agreement on the full institutional design.

OpenAI had publicly committed to industry standards four days before Schwartz’s article. Its chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, wrote on September 9 that the company would work with other frontier labs, “building a voluntary effort now, with or without government support”. He said this would complement mandatory federal safeguards and democratic oversight, and asked Congress for capability-based national regulation before adjournment. Altman’s town-hall remarks describe the outcome he expects. Amodei’s essay, accompanying Anthropic’s evaluator-access pledge, says government need not participate in coordination but should provide a narrow antitrust waiver for certain safety conversations. He names Hassabis’s mechanism as a possible venue; OpenAI has separately asked Congress for antitrust guidance. The companies were divided over Massachusetts’s model-testing requirements: Anthropic supported independent model-risk evaluations, while OpenAI and Google opposed them. OpenAI supports annual compliance audits, a separate duty in the Senate text. Those audits check adherence to safety frameworks; the model evaluations independently assess catastrophic risks. In Schwartz’s September 3 reporting, OpenAI said it favored technical assessments at the federal level. Google’s objection, according to a person familiar with its position, concerned undefined testing standards and the difficulty of predicting results or compliance duties. Agreement on audits therefore does not establish agreement on the tests a standards body would require.

Schwartz had reported on August 27 that a draft executive order creating an AI self-regulatory organization had circulated in the White House. It still needed senior officials’ approval, including the president’s, and progress had stalled; Schwartz could not establish why. The proposal drew on Hassabis’s essay and a July proposal from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent reported by Bloomberg. David Sacks, co-chair of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, had called a FINRA-like body a “horrible idea” on All-In, Schwartz reported. Sacks objected to pre-release testing and government supervision, preferring the Motion Picture Association’s independent ratings model. Sophia Cai and Charles Rollet reported in Politico on September 3 that officials had previewed the FINRA-style plan for Trump and major labs in mid-August, and Zuckerberg opposed it in a call the week of August 17. The administration was still considering both the FINRA and MPA approaches. NIST’s Center for AI Standards and Innovation already serves as industry’s federal contact for testing. Schwartz’s August account described its downsizing and renaming from the AI Safety Institute, and said a voluntary evaluation framework created by a June executive order remained unpublished as of August 27 despite being finalized earlier that month.

Sacks returned to the antitrust dispute late Saturday evening, telling Amodei and Altman they could slow development themselves and accusing them of seeking permission to form a cartel. He argued that product-liability exposure already encouraged restraint and disputed METR’s independence, alleging ties to Anthropic’s investors and staff. METR discloses that it accepts no funding from AI companies or donations directed by frontier-company employees, while using free model access; those financial rules do not resolve every question about personnel ties. Cohere’s Aidan Gomez had mocked Altman’s endorsement on Saturday with “Some great ideas here from the cartel”, objecting to access requirements, shutdowns and rules China would ignore. Nicholas Felstead argued in Lawfare on March 5 that antitrust uncertainty discourages joint testing, incident sharing and coordinated pauses. He proposed tailored statutory protection or regulatory guidance, citing cybersecurity information-sharing protections as a possible model. His analysis distinguishes collaboration that improves safety from agreements restricting competition; legality depends on the agreement’s details. An agreed pause, he wrote, could be construed as an output restriction even if its purpose was safety. He suggested tying any new statutory protection for pauses to specific threats, limiting its duration and making it reviewable, while retaining scrutiny of anticompetitive conduct. Mark Thomas argued in Lawfare on April 29 for a statutory AI regulator, using FINRA’s federally supervised structure as the precedent. FINRA itself describes authority under federal law and SEC supervision. A private agreement among labs would not by itself create that authority.

The three companies already belong to the Frontier Model Forum, which they launched with Microsoft in July 2023 to develop safety practices, standardized evaluations and information sharing. Amazon and Meta have since joined. The Forum’s institutional description identifies it as a member-funded 501(c)(6), led by executive director Chris Meserole, that does not lobby. Thomas proposes having Congress create a supervising agency and recognize the Forum as the industry regulator, making membership compulsory for companies above specified thresholds. Under his plan, the supervisor would have final authority over rules and enforcement; lab fees would pay staff without relying on annual congressional appropriations. Industry representatives and independent directors would share the board, with agency approval for independent appointments and technical committees drawing on labs’ research and safety staff. That is his institutional proposal; Schwartz does not report that the working group has adopted it or decided whether to create a new organization or expand the Forum. Meta’s membership also leaves its position on the current talks unresolved: Schwartz explicitly says its stance is unclear. Alexandr Wang’s early-Sunday post on alignment expressed no position on the body. The Forum’s published membership list includes neither xAI nor Cohere, although Elon Musk endorsed Amodei’s essay. Schwartz does not name Cohere among the working-group participants; the report does not establish the eventual membership.

The reported July meetings establish sustained work on a standards body before the weekend’s public pledges. They do not establish a charter agreed by the three companies or a commitment to Hassabis’s proposed pre-release tests. His design depends on federal involvement in supervision and in eventually making approval a condition of US deployment. Altman’s reported expectation of proceeding without government support leaves those features unsettled. Both his comments about pacing and Hassabis’s proposal envisage slower development, but Schwartz reports no agreement among the three companies on a coordinated slowdown.
