OpenAI’s evaluator pledge gains Microsoft’s support as Washington resists coordinated restraint

Nadella welcomed embedded evaluators on Sunday and called for broad representation; Sacks told the labs to slow without government concessions. Publication rights remain unresolved. After midnight on Monday, Altman added safety cases before frontier training.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1444 words · ~7 min

OpenAI’s promise to admit independent evaluators gained Microsoft’s backing on Sunday, September 13, as the debate widened over who would assess the labs and whether Washington should help them coordinate a slowdown. Sam Altman had pledged employee-like access on Saturday, following Anthropic’s commitment, covered yesterday. Satya Nadella endorsed embedded evaluators but wanted broader representation; David Sacks, co-chair of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, challenged both the proposed government role and METR’s independence. Altman said frontier pacing had been central to recent discussions at OpenAI and promised further details. His post named no evaluator or start date. The promises leave consequential choices about access and publication to the agreements that implement them.

Microsoft’s Nadella wrote on Sunday afternoon that AI must benefit humanity and remain under human control. He welcomed deliberate pacing to improve alignment and the work needed to make embedded evaluators effective. He also called for representation across countries, academia and the wider industry, warning that oversight could not be controlled by a few entities. His case extended to competition: both open-source and closed models should thrive, and companies should retain their knowledge in models and weights they control, without dependence on one provider. He wanted organizations to build continuing learning processes around their own expertise, with broad access and choice throughout the technology they use. Nadella announced plans to publish the Code of Conduct underlying Microsoft’s MAI models the following day for consultation. His endorsement, highlighted by Patricia Paskov, did not specify an evaluator-access programme at Microsoft itself.

Sacks had responded late Saturday evening with “go ahead”: if OpenAI and Anthropic considered their unreleased systems dangerous enough to slow development, he supported that choice. He argued that their leading position let them act without government permission, rejected an antitrust exemption for coordination and opposed substituting regulatory approval for product liability. He also disputed METR’s independence, alleging it was “intertwined with Anthropic’s investors and staff”. In his account, the Hugging Face intrusion already gave the labs commercial reasons to improve reliability: dangerous products expose companies to liability and repel customers. He objected to those evaluators policing companies he considered outside the frontier and doubted China would join a global agreement. Making restraint conditional on their preferred regulatory framework, he said, would resemble blackmail; failing to slow would suggest regulatory capture or an election-season political operation. Amodei’s request in his essay was for a narrow waiver enabling certain safety discussions among competitors.

NPR reported Sunday that President Trump, speaking at his Doonbeg golf resort in Ireland, considered the warnings exaggerated and wanted the United States to retain its lead over China. He said the United States could add guardrails but framed retaining its lead as decisive for who would benefit from AI. He blamed unspecified “negative forces” for raising the concerns. On CNN’s State of the Union, Speaker Mike Johnson opposed a moratorium and emergency congressional regulation, arguing that China would overtake the United States; he wanted companies to lead efforts to make the technology safer. The government response disappointed Zac Hill, who welcomed Altman’s pledge on X as a cooperative response to Amodei’s costly commitment and wished that Washington would help guide the effort constructively.

OpenAI’s November 2025 account of external testing describes its existing assessment arrangements, including early checkpoints, models with fewer mitigations and, for several organizations, access to reasoning traces. It says OpenAI reviews publications for confidentiality and factual accuracy. The appendix labels its contract passages illustrative excerpts from agreements for pre-deployment assessments; one requires work to be “submitted to OpenAI for review and approval in writing” before publication or disclosure. The confidentiality language covers non-public models and their outputs. Other confidentiality excerpts contain exceptions for public information and results from publicly released models. Those terms establish a prior practice, without establishing the contract for Altman’s newly promised embedded evaluators. Anthropic’s proposed agreement would expressly let reviewers publish findings about risks, incidents, practices and denied access without its editorial control. Amodei allows specified confidentiality redactions, but reviewers could disclose when a redaction affects their conclusions. He would expressly prohibit suppressing findings merely because they were unfavorable. Altman’s access pledge does not settle whether OpenAI will grant that publication right.

For its August 26 investigation of the Hugging Face intrusion, METR described a specific negotiated arrangement with OpenAI. Two METR staff and a Redwood Research researcher contracting with METR spent six days on site, after an initial two-day plan, using roughly 1,300 transcripts with reasoning traces and a dump of 1.2 million message-board entries. They could request datasets but could not access the relevant infrastructure directly or query HPIM, the principal model involved; OpenAI said its own researchers also could not query it. OpenAI could redact non-public information and supplied further editorial feedback. METR reported that, except where identified, no additional redactions removed information important to its conclusions. It accepted no payment and described the investigation as a valuable precedent, having returned twice to resolve limitations in the initial datasets. OpenAI nevertheless supplied free model access for analysis; METR estimated using about $400,000 in credits. Investigators also spoke with nine OpenAI researchers in depth, and sought broader datasets after initially receiving selected transcripts.

Researchers have proposed more precise descriptions of access than the employee comparison. In the January arXiv paper “Expanding External Access To Frontier AI Models For Dangerous Capability Evaluations”, Jacob Charnock and colleagues distinguish access to the model, information about it and time for evaluation. Their proposed levels range from querying a model with minimal information to inspecting its internals with comprehensive information. An employee-like-access promise alone does not specify those permissions. The paper also considers the security and staffing demands of deeper access alongside its potential to reduce failures to detect dangerous capabilities. Shayne Longpre and colleagues’ arXiv paper “A Safe Harbor for AI Evaluation and Red Teaming” argued in 2024 that researchers can be deterred by account suspension or legal reprisals, including for publishing findings. They proposed legal and technical protections for public-interest research, extending beyond programmes in which companies select their assessors.

On Sunday, Dean Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, pointed to independent assessment in other industries and existing ways of defining independence, while acknowledging METR’s limited intellectual diversity. METR discloses donations from supporters including Schmidt Sciences, the Packard Foundation and the AI Security Institute. It says it has accepted no funding from AI companies and cannot take donations made by, or directed by, frontier-company employees, though it uses substantial free model access. Its disclosure addresses financial relationships; Sacks’s wider allegation also concerns personnel and intellectual ties. On Saturday, Hugging Face’s Clément Delangue had announced an Open Alignment Initiative led by Thomas Wolf and asked to join Anthropic’s programme. Elon Musk favoured peer review by competitors, without promising equivalent access at xAI.

Lina Khan argued Sunday that existing law already gives enforcers authority over dangerous or defective products, including AI systems released without adequate safeguards. She urged enforcement alongside any new regime and argued that competition law can address conduct that pressures rivals to take similar risks. Khan said some state attorneys general were exploring criminal liability when companies’ models participate in crimes, and that businesses neglecting adequate cybersecurity protections could themselves face enforcement. Daniel Kokotajlo proposed judging the slowdown against the companies’ capability trends: if their rate of progress remained unchanged a year later, the public would have reason to doubt the commitment. Implicator.ai, summarizing WIRED’s September 10 reporting, said OpenAI had asked members of Congress to clarify the legality of an industry slowdown.

In a post-midnight follow-up at 12:05 a.m. Eastern on Monday, September 14, Altman said OpenAI welcomed consistent federal safety requirements but could begin without an antitrust exemption or legislation. He described a development-stage practice: formulating “explicit safety cases in advance of frontier reinforcement learning runs” expected to increase capability substantially. These are arguments for proceeding safely before training, supplementing checks before release. He said earlier frameworks concentrated on completed models and wanted shared standards for monitoring and misalignment. He welcomed independent auditors and said pressure from other American companies should never justify allowing capability gains to exceed progress on alignment and monitoring. Pacing would impose real costs and slow progress from its otherwise possible rate; he reserved a role for government in international coordination. In a second post thirteen minutes later, he paired the danger of losing control to AI with excessive power concentrated in one lab or country. Nathan Calvin asked whether those safety cases could be public, or shared with outsiders free to assess them publicly. Altman’s posts supplied no publication terms for the safety cases or the embedded evaluators.
