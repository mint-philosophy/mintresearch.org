How a North Korean job-fraud cell works, from its own files

The Wall Street Journal's August documentary, built on hacked data from one team's computers, shows operatives applying to a thousand companies in three months, reading ChatGPT answers aloud in interviews, and paying an Ohio man to front for them.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1488 words · ~7 min

The Wall Street Journal’s 32-minute documentary “Infiltrated: North Korea’s Secret U.S. Workforce”, published on 12 August after a year of reporting by Sam Kessler, Emma Scott and Robert McMillan, is built on the operatives’ own files: a trove of hacked data from one North Korean remote-work cell’s computers, found in an online archive, with browser history, emails, calendars, stolen identity documents and screen recordings the team made of its own interviews and meetings. In just over three months the cell applied for jobs at more than a thousand companies; one week of the team leader’s calendar from January 2025 shows what appear to be 22 interviews under at least seven identities; and the narration counts at least nine jobs won in the United States and Britain (the Journal’s own summary of the film puts it at eight companies). The Journal re-promoted the film on X on Sunday with a clip about Michael Brown, the Georgia man whose stolen identity runs through it.

The team leader uses the codename EagleVision. Three men appear on video posing as tech workers under the names Lucas Lee, Patrick New and Michael Brown. They coordinate resumes and cover letters for each alias on a private Discord server, prepare for interviews in shared Google Docs and look up how to pronounce their cover names. In one screen recording of an interview, the interviewer asks about experience with AWS Lambda; the worker copies the question out of a transcription tool, “pastes it into ChatGPT, and then reads the response verbatim”. Kessler wrote on X that recordings like this one, obtained in summer 2025, led the reporters to the larger data set. The Journal traced an IP address tied to the team to Shenyang, a Chinese city near the North Korean border, and reached EagleVision on Telegram; he said the Journal had the wrong person and declined an interview.

The team buys its identities. The Journal found Brown’s for sale on Telegram, and a document recovered from a different North Korean cell’s computer sets out the procedure: buy an identity from a site that sells stolen personal data, check it against e-Verify, a US government website, to see whether it will pass a background check, then buy a driver’s licence from a company called TrustID Card, which added a notice to its website saying it only makes souvenirs after the Journal made contact. Six days after the film, Recorded Future’s Insikt Group published a report on North Korean clusters it calls PurpleDelta that names the same service, trustidcard.com, and describes operators pasting transcribed interview questions into ChatGPT; those clusters applied to more than 1,100 companies, at 60 or more applications a day, and kept at least 22 fabricated personas. Recorded Future’s Alexander Leslie told IT Pro the scale is “easy to miss when a company sees only one application”, and that operators read the ChatGPT answers back, sometimes word for word, occasionally repeating wrong ones.

Brown learned his identity was in use when he went to a Department of Labor office to check on unemployment. The department’s records showed him employed at nine companies he had never heard of, with $100,000 in income he says he never received; the film does not attribute all nine to North Koreans, but the Journal found them using his name at two more companies. In early 2025 Tim and Charlie Richardson, a father and son in Florida, hired the fake Brown through a recruiter as a developer for their startup Visual PT, and learned he was misrepresenting himself only when the Journal approached them; “My mind is blown right now,” one of them says on camera. The same worker was employed by the contractor DT Consulting, placed through it at Amazon, and under another name worked at Subly, a British tech company. Amazon told the Journal it has blocked more than 2,900 suspected North Korean operatives since April 2024, has never directly hired one, and in rare cases has removed contractors’ staff found using fraudulent identities; in December 2025 its chief security officer Steve Schmidt had put the count at more than 1,800 against the same start date. Brown received a letter in October 2025 naming him a victim in an identity-theft case involving fake IDs, but says “I’ve been blacklisted so I can’t get a bank account” and cannot rent property in his name.

Derrik Goon ran the American end. The Journal tracked him to rural Ohio, where he says he was paid to run a laptop out of his home and did not know EagleVision was North Korean. Because he has a programming background, the team sometimes applied under his real name and he sat the interviews himself; when he got the job, he ran the laptop and attended meetings while the team did the work. He says the team took its share in cryptocurrency, that he would send about $600 and keep the rest, and that on one $75,000-a-year job his cut was half. “There’s no way I’m the only guy doing this,” he says. He has since found an Upwork 1099 showing nearly $100,000 in freelance income under his name that he believes went to the North Koreans. A law-enforcement official in the film says investigators have “arrested or charged 39 individuals”, both IT workers and Americans, and that the biggest laptop farm found so far held about a hundred machines. The film names no cases, but the public record does: Christina Chapman of Arizona was sentenced to 102 months in July 2025 for a laptop farm that placed workers at 309 companies using 68 stolen identities and earned North Korea $17 million, with more than 90 laptops found in her home. Bloomberg told the story from Chapman’s side in a December 2025 documentary built around her; the Journal’s film starts from the operatives’ own machines.

EagleVision’s browser history led the Journal to his cryptocurrency wallet. Tracing it placed him in a wider North Korean financial network that includes wallets tied to an $11 million crypto heist; cybersecurity researchers traced some payments to Ryonbong, a company the US sanctioned for its role in Pyongyang’s weapons programme. The film puts the scheme’s take at “around $800 million a year by recent estimates”; the Treasury Department, which on 12 March sanctioned six people and two companies for running IT-worker fraud, said the schemes generated nearly $800 million in 2024, and the United Nations, NBC News reports, estimates as much as $600 million a year. One of the film’s sources says individual workers can earn upwards of $300,000 a year and that Treasury has found up to 90 per cent of it going back to the regime. The Journal’s own earlier reporting fills in the workers’ side: in a February piece by Dasl Yoon, a defector, Anton Koh, described 16-hour days in a state-run dormitory in China against monthly quotas the regime raised to $8,000 during the pandemic.

The film arrived twelve days after eleven governments, including the United States, Britain, Japan and South Korea, issued their first joint alert on North Korean IT workers, which warns that they use increasingly sophisticated methods, “including the integration of AI, to obfuscate their identities”, and recommends in-person interviews, verification of video calls and suspicion of requests to be paid in cryptocurrency. North Korea’s foreign ministry answered on 4 August through KCNA, calling the alert “a stereotyped political accusation” and false information; the regime did not respond to the Journal. The Journal also contacted the nearly 300 companies, recruiters and hiring managers who appear in the film; many did not respond, declined to comment or denied knowingly hiring anyone under a false identity.

The film ends on how the operation is changing. One source expects face-swapping tools to remove North Korean faces from interviews altogether: “getting North Korean’s faces off camera is huge for them”. Another describes teams winning a job and immediately reposting it at a lower price: one $5,000 job went to a worker in the Middle East for $500. The teams are also recruiting English speakers to sit interviews; the Journal quotes the pitch of one North Korean worker it spoke to, which admits a language gap costs them projects, “that’s why we are looking for a fluent English speaker”. NBC News took that thread forward on 11 September, reporting that North Korean teams have recruited stand-ins in Iran, Syria, Lebanon, South Africa, Saudi Arabia and other countries at $500 a month as part-time “interview associates”; at least 14 Iranians have been recruited since 2024, two of whom received formal offer letters from US employers, and “once the job is secured, a North Korean agent typically takes over”. The film’s on-camera sources treat the revenue as the lesser problem: companies are handing these workers usernames, passwords and network keys, one says, and the scheme could pivot to espionage, theft of intellectual property or sabotage of critical infrastructure.
