AI proofs and the problem of recognizing expertise

Solving a famous problem has helped make deeper mathematical work legible. Goedecke asks what happens to that recognition if AI can supply the proof first.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1259 words · ~6 min

Sean Goedecke, an Australian software engineer, argues that AI could disrupt the way people recognize expertise before replacing all the abilities they value. His September 13 essay, “AI is breaking our proxies for expertise,” responds to the Fields medallists’ declaration covered on September 11. He rejects dismissing the mathematicians’ concerns as a familiar complaint about automation. Solving a recognizable problem has helped outsiders appreciate work whose deeper value they cannot judge. If a machine reaches the answer first, the explanatory work may remain valuable while becoming harder to reward.

He distinguishes solving stated problems from generating mathematical ideas. An unfamiliar concept can look indistinguishable from an arbitrary invention: he proposes a “Goedecke set” of natural numbers whose digits sum to a prime and asks why anyone should care. Solving a worthwhile problem supplies one answer. It validates the concepts used and gives nonspecialists a recognizable achievement through which to acknowledge their creator. Goedecke says he cannot appreciate Tao’s mathematics but can recognize a Fields Medal. These functions overlap: in a footnote, he treats demonstrating an idea’s usefulness as part of developing it.

Goedecke reconstructs the objection that AI companies can collect the prestige of solved problems without supplying the illuminating concepts that made those achievements valuable. His linked examples include OpenAI’s September 8 Navier-Stokes announcement and Anthropic’s August 10 improvement to a bound on Riemann-zeta zeros. The latter is a partial result, not a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. These announcements motivate his question; neither company’s claim alone establishes that the work lacks useful ideas.

The proxy argument has a close precursor in Terence Tao’s August 17 ICM essay, “Mathematics in the age of AI,” discussed in our September 3 coverage of verification and exposition. Tao considers how historically aligned goals can diverge when one becomes easier to optimize. Goedecke acknowledges interpreting Tao and other mathematicians, without identifying that essay. W. T. Gowers’s older “The Two Cultures of Mathematics” distinguishes theory-building from problem-solving as priorities, emphasizing that mathematicians need both. His complaint that theory-oriented fields enjoyed greater professional fashion concerns recognition within mathematics. Goedecke’s account of legible achievement concerns outsiders, so the two prestige claims address different audiences.

Goedecke also questions the premise he has reconstructed. Whether models solve problems without generating valuable ideas remains unclear to him; he gives “basically zero credence” to intrinsic incapacity. He bases that confidence on what he regards as the poor record of predictions about abilities models could never acquire. Even if an AI proof supplies little understanding, he argues, humans can seek an illuminating proof alongside it. Knowing the result is true could make the search easier by removing doubt that there is an answer to find. His supporting link, Gwern Branwen’s “On Really Trying,” collects motivational anecdotes, including Claude Shannon’s experience with a jigsaw puzzle. It offers illustrations, not an experiment establishing this effect in research mathematics.

A September 13 comment by mathematician Nicholas Shepherd-Barron supplies a concrete case. He calls the initial manuscript constructing a complex structure on the six-sphere “100 pages of AI slop,” but praises Philip Engel’s exposition as part of the mathematical conversation. Engel’s “Complex structures on S6” explains that he recasts the geometry obscured by the original manuscript’s presentation through period matrices and gives a self-contained construction and proof. He credits the initial manuscript to Claude under Levent Alpöge’s direction. His own account also discloses using ChatGPT 5.6-Sol to explore the original paper and perform computations, while taking responsibility for independently checking the mathematics. The example involves explanatory work with AI assistance, complicating a clean separation between human and machine contributions.

Recognition remains harder. Goedecke suspects discovering an answer is more motivating, and more readily rewarded, than explaining an answer already known. He proposes chess and speedrunning as models for communities that continue to value human performance under distinct constraints. The speedrunning comparison needs care: tool-assisted runs are constructed through human choices, with tools such as saved states, repeated attempts and frame-by-frame input. They are not simply autonomous AI defeating human players. The useful parallel is the possibility of preserving separate standards of achievement.

The chess evidence also has limits. In “Computers and chess masters: The role of AI in transforming elite human performance,” Merim Bilalić, Mario Graf and Nemanja Vaci analyzed over 11.6 million elite decisions. Their British Journal of Psychology paper, first published online in 2024, reports mostly gradual improvement across four decades, with faster gains among the youngest top players during the late-1990s computer and internet expansion. It found little evidence of an additional improvement attributable to the later neural-network engines. The authors describe the first association as largely circumstantial and acknowledge limited insight into the mechanisms. Learning from computer play is plausible, but this study does not establish a general route from stronger machines to better human understanding.

Silvia De Toffoli and Eamon Duede challenge the games analogy in “After Math,” their September 12 guest essay on Tao’s blog. They distinguish logical certification from proofs that people can understand and use. They accept formal verification as a contribution while arguing that a mathematical solution also needs intelligibility: “There is no mathematical checkmate.” Mathematics continues through concepts, questions and communities. On Hacker News on September 13, daniel-sc similarly objects that mathematical ideas matter to subsequent progress. Wren_ops proposes rewarding explanations that make machine proofs reusable; daniel-sc replies that judging a good explanation remains unresolved.

Goedecke extends the concern to his own profession. Engineers usually write code for commercial purposes, he says, yet still need ways to demonstrate individual ability. A substantial GitHub project, such as an emulator or toy operating system, once offered persuasive evidence. He now expects observers to suspect AI generation. Likewise, rewriting a system over a weekend loses its signaling value if many people can produce the output with an AI subscription. He proposes separating human work from AI work or finding other recognizable skills that are difficult to counterfeit. These are his observations and proposals, not measured findings about hiring outcomes.

A historical study helps specify the signal. Jennifer Marlow and Laura Dabbish’s “Activity Traces and Signals in Software Developer Recruitment and Hiring,” presented at CSCW 2013, interviewed seven employers and six job seekers. Participants considered GitHub evidence relatively credible because they could inspect code, contributions and interactions, rather than relying on résumé assertions. The evidence included how someone worked with others, not merely a finished artifact. The authors acknowledge their small volunteer sample and propose following later job performance to test initial impressions. Their study documents perceptions of those signals before current generative AI; it does not establish that portfolios have now become worthless.

The concern reaches teaching, too. In Ian Sample’s September 12 Guardian report, Manchester mathematician David Silvester questions take-home coursework because instructors cannot vouch for its authenticity. He also anticipates spending more time auditing machine-written proofs. Those comments describe professional anxieties about assessment and daily work, rather than demonstrating that mathematical expertise has disappeared.

Goedecke’s philosophy footnote asks what happens when a field generates ideas without comparable puzzles to validate them. He connects that absence to philosophy’s lower prestige. In a separate September 13 comment on Leiter’s post, David Wallace argues that establishing a philosophical result ordinarily depends on producing work other philosophers regard as good. Clever counterexamples and narrow interventions can function more as credentials, he suggests, while acknowledging exceptions such as Gettier’s original paper. He doubts that losing some of that credentialing function to AI would necessarily harm philosophy. His response leaves open which signals deserve preservation and which a discipline could usefully stop rewarding.
