Extinction risk cannot settle AI policy, Demsas argues

The Argument editor argues that even a credible threat leaves governments responsible for showing which responses work and why their costs to rights and freedoms are justified.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 September 2026 1348 words · ~7 min

Even a credible threat of human extinction would leave people disagreeing about which AI policies work and which sacrifices are justified, Jerusalem Demsas argues in “Politics at the End of the World”, published on 13 September. The Argument’s founder and editor-in-chief begins with the appeal of making every other concern subordinate to survival: “The apocalypse is a trump card.” Her objection concerns the authority claimed in its name. Legislatures, lawsuits and public argument let people cooperate despite incompatible priorities. Treating catastrophe as an overriding emergency can make civil rights, constraints on executive power and legal scrutiny appear expendable, without establishing that abandoning them would make anyone safer.

Demsas borrows her opening perspective from Susan Sontag’s “The Imagination of Disaster”, published in Commentary in October 1965. Sontag describes disaster movies’ promise to release viewers from ordinary obligations and make humanity unite against a single danger. She also describes their fantasy of perfect consensus: “The universal rule of reason meant universal agreement.” Demsas applies that attraction to political life. Disagreement about ends and means normally survives agreement that a problem is urgent; people may agree to fund public health and still dispute what education should receive. Apocalyptic rhetoric offers relief from those choices. Her concern is that governments could mistake that relief for a justification to bypass the institutions that adjudicate them.

Her immediate example is Jacob Coxon’s resignation thread. In his reply, Evan Hubinger placed extinction risk above 10 per cent within a decade. CNBC described Hubinger as an alignment lead at Anthropic. Those warnings were part of our 9 September coverage; Demsas’s essay asks what their political consequences should be. She links Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares’s If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies and the AI Futures Project’s AI 2027 as examples of extinction’s prominence in safety advocacy. She herself fears AI-enabled surveillance, biological threats, attacks on infrastructure and banking, misaligned weapons, and the loss of independent human thinking. Her doubt concerns the death of every human, for which she invokes the adaptability of more than eight billion people. She grants that this judgment could be wrong. Her subtitle asks whether p(doom), used here for an estimated probability of AI causing human extinction, helps people decide. She associates the fixation on that metric with a “fringe ideology”, without specifying which doctrine the calculation requires. Her concluding concession leaves the political argument intact even if her own estimate is mistaken: neither the probability nor the severity of a danger tells us whether a particular intervention will reduce it.

Demsas uses climate politics to distinguish recognizing a danger from choosing an effective response. She attributes the largest US emissions reduction to the shale revolution’s substitution of gas for coal and gives the climate movement no credit for it. The Energy Information Administration’s 2021 analysis estimates that coal-to-gas switching accounted for 65 per cent of the decline in electricity-sector carbon dioxide emissions between 2005 and 2019; growing renewable generation accounted for another 30 per cent. These figures measure changes in the generation mix. They do not establish her wider judgment about activism’s contribution. Demsas acknowledges that climate change remains unsolved; she argues that a justified fear of catastrophe does not reliably produce effective policy.

Her examples of damaging tradeoffs range from authoritarian proposals to energy decisions. James Lovelock told the Guardian in 2010 that climate action might require putting democracy temporarily on hold. Germany’s official timeline records its post-Fukushima acceleration of nuclear closure and the final shutdowns in April 2023. A study by Stephen Jarvis, Olivier Deschenes and Akshaya Jha estimated that shutdowns between 2011 and 2017 displaced nuclear output mainly with coal and imports, imposing roughly $12 billion in annual social costs, mostly from air-pollution mortality. That estimate concerns the earlier closures, not the final three plants. Demsas also invokes Andrew Cuomo’s closure of Indian Point, which she argues sacrificed clean electricity while worsening costs.

Nick Bostrom’s “The Vulnerable World Hypothesis”, published in Global Policy in 2019, is her example of a philosopher contemplating continuous worldwide surveillance. Bostrom’s argument is conditional: some possible technologies could make civilization vulnerable unless preventive policing and global governance became much stronger. His surveillance vignette equips everyone with a “freedom tag” carrying cameras and microphones. But he explicitly recognizes that such powers could protect despots and impose a dominant ideology, and says his argument does not by itself settle their desirability. His paper therefore describes the tradeoff between security and liberty as well as proposing capacities Demsas finds alarming.

Demsas’s concern about law is clearest in the March 2023 TIME essay she links, where Eliezer Yudkowsky endorsed military destruction of rogue data centres and placed preventing AI extinction above avoiding a full nuclear exchange. She asks how much hearing lawyers would receive when disputing the legality of an action presented as humanity’s only hope. Japanese-American internment and surveillance of American citizens are her historical warnings about sacrificing legal protections during emergencies. The National Archives’ account of Executive Order 9066 records that Justice Department representatives raised constitutional and ethical objections before the forced removals. Congress nevertheless backed the policy with criminal penalties, and the Supreme Court upheld military restrictions on Japanese Americans; Congress acknowledged the injustice and apologized in 1988. The example concerns legal institutions failing to protect rights as well as executives claiming exceptional authority. She nevertheless welcomes nuclear nonproliferation as evidence that extreme danger can motivate international cooperation. Her conclusion explicitly allows that AI might threaten humanity: establishing the threat would still leave officials responsible for explaining why their chosen response would help and why its costs were justified.

Related scholarship separates questions that Demsas brings together. Barry Buzan, Ole Wæver and Jaap de Wilde’s 1998 book on security distinguishes securitization from ordinary politicization. In “Democratising Risk”, dated 2021, Carla Zoe Cremer and Luke Kemp apply the concern to existential-risk studies: moving an issue into national security can encourage emergency powers and control by unelected officials. They call for democratic policy recommendations and a separation between evaluating extinction risk and promoting a particular vision of civilization’s future. Cass Sunstein’s “Probability Neglect”, in the Yale Law Journal in 2002, identifies a different failure: vivid outcomes can make people disregard probabilities, leading either to excessive precaution or indifference to substantial risks. His argument supports better probability assessment; Demsas also asks what an assessment can legitimately authorize.

Bruce Ackerman’s “The Emergency Constitution”, published in the same journal in 2004, offers a concrete institutional comparison. After an initial period, continued emergency authority would require progressively larger legislative majorities: 60, then 70, then 80 per cent. Repeated votes would prevent emergency measures from becoming routine and give smaller minorities increasing power to end them. Ackerman was addressing terrorism; Demsas leaves open which safeguards should govern an AI emergency. Her skepticism about extinction has a more specific parallel in RAND’s May 2025 report by Michael Vermeer, Emily Lathrop and Alvin Moon. Across nuclear, pathogen and geoengineering scenarios, they distinguish global catastrophe from extinction and emphasize the obstacles posed by human resilience. They still decline to rule extinction out.

The essay appeared on the Sunday when Trump dismissed dire AI warnings and Speaker Mike Johnson opposed a moratorium. Demsas had already argued in a 9 September thread that sincere belief in winner-take-all competition could explain the labs’ conduct, and urged pressure on Altman and Amodei. Fortune’s Emily Forlini had pressed Coxon on 10 September for evidence and actionable specifics. Demsas’s essay reaches a further question: how to justify action even after granting the danger. Within three hours of publication, Alex Trembath shared it and a commenter challenged her nonproliferation example: apocalyptic warnings had accompanied nuclear arms-control politics without preventing it. Another commenter, posting as Ben, argued that sufficiently advanced AI could defeat her confidence in human survival. He also questioned her account of disaster rhetoric: warnings of partial catastrophe can encourage people to imagine themselves among the survivors and indulge the very apocalyptic fantasies she deplores. These objections distinguish the effects of alarming rhetoric from the underlying risk; Demsas’s demand for political justification applies even if that risk is real.
