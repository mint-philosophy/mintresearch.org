Where DeepMind's value lock-in warning comes from

Tomašev pitches cheap mathematical models as alignment foresight; the preprint's lock-in worry descends from MacAskill and Pettigrew, its agenda extends DeepMind's 2024 assistants-ethics survey, and its central abstraction drew an immediate challenge on X.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 22 July 2026 440 words · ~2 min

On X, Nenad Tomašev of Google DeepMind introduced the preprint with a thread that sells a method as much as a finding. Alignment practice, he wrote, treats human values as "static optimization targets for model releases," and the paper's population-level models are meant as cheap foresight, a "social physics" pass that yields testable predictions before anyone commits to expensive agentic simulations. AI coding tools, he argued, have made "rapid formal experimentation as foresight in computational social science" affordable for domain experts. He also bounded the claim: the models are "not meant to be in any way definitive," only an illustration of how the questions should be posed. Matija Franklin and Simon Osindero, his co-authors, are also at DeepMind.

The paper's intellectual debts sit in moral philosophy and decision theory. Its lock-in worry traces to William MacAskill's 2022 book What We Owe the Future, which warned that artificial intelligence could lock humanity "into a perpetual dystopia." The authors also build on Richard Pettigrew's Choosing for Changing Selves, a formal decision theory for agents whose preferences change across a lifetime, and on L. A. Paul's Transformative Experience, which argued some choices alter our values in ways we cannot evaluate beforehand. The AI-influence term in their equations has empirical backing. In a randomized controlled trial published in Nature Human Behaviour, Francesco Salvi and colleagues found that participants who debated GPT-4, when the model had access to their personal information, showed 81.7 percent higher odds of increased agreement with their opponent than participants who debated humans; the paper also cites Costello and colleagues' finding that dialogues with AI reduced conspiracy beliefs by 20 percent on average.

Institutionally, the preprint extends an agenda DeepMind set out in April 2024, when Iason Gabriel and 56 co-authors, Tomašev and Franklin among them, released The Ethics of Advanced AI Assistants, a survey spanning manipulation, persuasion, trust, and "AI value alignment, well-being, safety and malicious uses." That document mapped the hazards of assistant deployment qualitatively; the new paper takes one of its questions, what deep personalization does to a population's values, and gives it equations.

The first responses probe the framework's central abstraction. A reader posting as Seref asked whether users even have a stable "value vector" for an assistant to track, proposing that AI model contextual preferences instead of inferring a persistent identity. Tomašev called it "an excellent point," allowing that "this framework is obviously still somewhat simplistic" while noting how much linear representations capture as first-order approximations. The paper's limitations section concedes adjacent ground: linear value updates cannot represent polarization, backfire effects, or hysteresis, and the fixed population leaves generational turnover out.
