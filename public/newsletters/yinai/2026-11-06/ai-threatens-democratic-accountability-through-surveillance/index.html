Botvinick proposes democratic reforms for an AI economy

In a Cosmos Institute interview, Matthew Botvinick connects the loss of work to weaker political accountability and proposes changes to citizen participation, surveillance law and oversight of government AI.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 November 2026 959 words · ~5 min

Matthew Botvinick argues that AI could weaken the economic dependence that helps keep governments accountable to citizens, and that protecting democracy will require changes to how people participate in government. In a November 6 interview published by the Cosmos Institute, he develops that argument alongside specific proposals for surveillance reform and oversight of government AI. He speaks in a personal capacity. We covered his book, AI and Political Freedom, in October; the interview explains the reasoning behind its proposals and gives more detail on the reforms he wants.

Botvinick starts with familiar democratic vulnerabilities that he expects AI to intensify. Losing income or social standing can make people more receptive to authoritarian appeals. AI-related security crises could then give leaders reasons to claim emergency powers, while AI in intelligence agencies could make it easier to monitor political opponents. He worries that democratic governments experiencing an authoritarian turn will adopt the surveillance practices he associates with China. In his account, AI increases both the likelihood of democratic breakdown and the difficulty of reversing it.

His longer-term concern depends on a prediction he acknowledges is contested: AI will eventually eliminate most jobs. Governments that rely on taxing workers have an economic reason to remain responsive to them. If AI removes that dependence, he argues, citizens could lose political influence along with their jobs. He compares this with oil-rich states whose rulers can raise revenue without taxing much of their population, calling it a “political resource curse”. He had already made the petrostate comparison in an October 12 Guardian essay. A precedent is Michael Ross’s 2001 World Politics paper “Does Oil Hinder Democracy?”, which examined whether oil revenues reduce demands for representation by allowing governments to tax less.

Botvinick’s proposed response is to give citizens a more direct role in governing. He draws on Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson’s account of liberty as depending on a balance between an effective state and a society capable of challenging it. Losing economic influence could upset that balance; citizens’ assemblies and deliberative polls could help people exercise power through participation in government itself. His comparison with Athens concerns citizens taking turns governing, and he explicitly sets aside its enslaved population. He argues that AI could make deliberation feasible at the scale needed for participation to carry legitimacy in a modern country.

He points to Stanford’s Deliberative Democracy Lab as an existing example. In the arXiv chapter Technology-Supported Moderation in Online Deliberation, Lodewijk Gelauff, Ashish Goel and Sukolsak Sakshuwong describe software that manages speaking turns and meeting agendas across small discussion groups. More than 30,000 people used the platform between 2018 and 2025. That is a cumulative count across separate deliberations, with groups ideally containing eight to fifteen people; the example shows how moderation can expand participation without requiring a human facilitator for every group.

Botvinick also examines how AI might change the views people bring to politics. An assistant drafting a letter to Congress could help someone who lacks confidence in their writing. But it could express a position they do not hold, or spare them the effort through which they would otherwise discover what they think. AI supplying political information creates another problem: a system acting independently of its builders would speak without belonging to the political community whose decisions it influences. He proposes protecting people’s ability to form their own judgments as an aim for AI alignment, while acknowledging that he cannot yet say how to achieve it. He invokes Philip Pettit’s ideal of non-domination as a guide to his thinking.

For Botvinick, even successful alignment and regulation would leave substantial political work undone. Societies still have to decide how institutions will use the technology and how power over it will be constrained. He wants AI researchers to study the histories of corporate power and democratic breakdown, including the different political uses of radio under Hitler and Franklin Roosevelt. His argument also allows for improvement on existing politics: following Joseph Schumpeter, he criticizes parties for treating persuasion as marketing instead of encouraging independent political judgment.

His first legislative priority is the Government Surveillance Reform Act. The Senate bill introduced on March 12 proposes restrictions on warrantless searches of Americans’ communications collected under foreign-intelligence authorities, limits on government purchases of personal data and stronger supervision of surveillance courts. Botvinick wants these protections as part of a wider revision of executive powers, including the Insurrection Act and the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. He hopes public anger can produce reforms comparable to those that followed Watergate.

He would also make government AI prompts and outputs federal records, put procurement requirements into statute, and equip Congress and the courts to scrutinize an executive branch whose operations AI may accelerate. Congress, he argues, will need technical capacity of its own. The National Archives’ AI records guidance already applies a contextual test involving official use, agency control and preservation obligations. It lists prompts and outputs saved in agency systems and used officially among likely records, but also requires retention where legal or business obligations demand it. Botvinick’s proposal calls for explicit inclusion of prompts and outputs; he does not specify the legal changes. We covered criticism of the guidance in September, including concerns that consequential chatbot conversations could go unpreserved.

For an immediate democratic benefit, Botvinick favors improving ordinary public services, such as passport applications and access to welfare. Reliable services could rebuild confidence that democratic government can solve problems. He closes by comparing anger over data centers with nineteenth-century anger at railroads, which helped generate the Progressive movement. He thinks today’s anger could similarly support public-interest reforms, but could also strengthen authoritarian politics. Whether people believe democratic institutions can address their grievances may help determine which direction they take.
