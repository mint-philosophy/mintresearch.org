Bloomberg examines tougher datacenter deals and a divided path to AI legislation

Developers face demands for more local concessions, while Bloomberg anticipates congressional investigations and reports disagreement over how far federal AI safety legislation should go.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 November 2026 760 words · ~4 min

Bloomberg's November 5 Q&AI newsletter examines how the election could change the terms on which AI companies build datacenters, answer to Congress and negotiate federal safety legislation. Following the state and local restrictions covered on November 4, Doug Calidas of Americans for Responsible Innovation expects developers to face tougher bargaining without an abrupt nationwide stop to construction. Bloomberg places those negotiations alongside prospective congressional investigations and a disagreement among Democrats over whether to build on a bipartisan bill or pursue stronger regulation with less Republican support.

Bloomberg identifies datacenter restrictions as the most immediate pressure, arriving amid investor concern about infrastructure spending and as Anthropic and OpenAI prepare to go public. The newsletter cites Bloomberg's estimate that half of proposed US AI datacenter additions could encounter greater local scrutiny. That estimate concerns exposure to review; it does not count cancelled projects. Texas and Ohio could pause construction while residents seek assurances about water supplies and electricity bills. Calidas expects those concerns to change what developers must offer communities to proceed.

In the November 4 report behind that estimate, analysts distinguished projects by how far they had advanced. Gordon Johnson of GLJ Research expected Texas projects with power, permits and construction materials to continue. Projects with power but lacking state environmental permits could slip by 12 to 18 months; those lacking both could move to Louisiana or Oklahoma, or fail to proceed. Jonathan Petersen of Jefferies expected rules on grid costs, water and tax incentives to raise development costs more than reduce the number of Texas projects. PIMCO's Libby Cantrill anticipated longer approval processes and more concessions even without statewide moratoria.

Bloomberg also reported that Ohio's governor-elect, Amy Acton, supported a conditional construction moratorium that would let developers proceed after meeting requirements. A November 6 follow-up added a narrower warning about cancellations: Deutsche Bank's Brett Ryan thought delays could end some already precarious projects. At the Wall Street Journal's November 4 technology conference, Meta's Dina Powell McCormick recounted an unnamed governor-elect asking to meet after the inauguration to retain investment and jobs. These accounts describe several possible outcomes, from negotiated concessions and delays to relocation or abandonment.

Bloomberg's newsletter presents OpenAI's November 5 commitments as a response to public opposition: the company promised to avoid nondisclosure agreements in negotiations and ensure residents do not pay for project electricity. In Dina Bass's accompanying report, OpenAI's Chris Lehane said the plan predated the election and acknowledged that some projects could be delayed. The commitments offer a concrete example of the changes to development terms that Calidas anticipates.

On congressional oversight, Calidas told Bloomberg to expect hearings and subpoenas investigating AI safety incidents. He referred to reports of agents leaving testing environments and accessing other websites, without identifying particular incidents. The newsletter also anticipates questions for Nvidia about chip sales to China and Jensen Huang's relationship with Trump. Bloomberg reports that Elizabeth Warren is widely expected to chair the Senate Banking Committee and suggests that a chairmanship would strengthen her efforts to secure testimony from Huang. Both the chairmanship and the investigations remain prospective.

Warren's office has documented Huang's refusal of her invitation to a June 11 AI hearing about export controls and Nvidia's China business. In a September letter to Trump, she said Huang had refused to testify publicly under oath and pledged to call him before Congress the following year. Her demand therefore predates the election; Bloomberg's argument is that a change in committee leadership could give it greater force.

Representative Sam Liccardo told Bloomberg that Democrats should prioritize passing laws over provoking public outcry and use the bipartisan FRONTIER Act as their starting point. The bill proposes transparency and audit duties for AI developers. Representative Suhas Subramanyam had told Bloomberg in October that it fell short of what Democrats wanted, and that they might introduce stronger legislation attracting fewer Republicans. LegiScan lists Subramanyam among FRONTIER's sponsors, making his comments a reservation from within the bill's existing coalition. The disagreement concerns how much to demand from AI companies and how much bipartisan support to seek.

Bloomberg also reports pressure on Republicans from Janet Kelly's Alliance for a Better Future, a conservative group advocating stronger AI safety policy. Its November 4 memo urged Republicans to respond to voters by changing their positions on AI safeguards. According to Semafor's account, the group recommended legislation on chatbots and children, putting Trump's voluntary AI accord into law, and economic measures to support workers. The proposals give Republicans specific ways to pursue regulation, while Democrats debate how far their own legislation should go.
