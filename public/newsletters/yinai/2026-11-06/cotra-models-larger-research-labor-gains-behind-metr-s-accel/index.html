Cotra models the research labor gains behind Anthropic’s acceleration

Under Cotra’s assumptions, roughly doubling AI progress could require research labor to run about ten times faster. She explores how continued gains might compound and argues that security and oversight are unprepared.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 November 2026 953 words · ~5 min

Ajeya Cotra’s November 6 Planned Obsolescence essay argues that the labor gains behind Anthropic’s faster AI progress could be much larger than the acceleration itself. She tentatively estimates roughly tenfold gains under a simplified economic model, with substantially more or less plausible. She then asks how continued gains could compound toward extreme superintelligence within a year. The essay extends METR’s assessment, covered on November 5: METR measured a faster benchmark trend and judged AI responsible for most of the current acceleration. Cotra develops the implications of that judgment; she did not work on the investigation or see its unredacted report.

Cotra begins with the relation between software and computing resources. Following Dario Amodei’s account of compute multipliers, she treats capability as the product of training compute and improvements that achieve equivalent performance with less compute. Since software supplies only part of progress, doubling overall progress through software requires software to improve faster still. She uses a range of two to three times the previous pace. Researchers also need computing resources to test their ideas. Assuming labor and experiment compute contribute equally, her model requires labor acceleration equal to the square of software acceleration: four to nine times faster.

Cotra argues that maintaining this faster pace requires growing labor gains. Earlier discoveries exhaust easier improvements, making subsequent discoveries harder; hiring and computing resources, growing at their old rates, cannot indefinitely sustain the new pace. After several months of acceleration, she suggests the required labor gain might be around tenfold. Changing the assumed contribution of labor changes that estimate. Her diminishing-returns argument has a precedent in Nicholas Bloom and colleagues’ 2020 American Economic Review paper, “Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?”, which documented rising research effort needed to maintain progress, including in chip density.

Cotra measures labor gains by how much faster researchers can complete valuable work, allowing them to finish sequential tasks sooner. Adding people cannot reproduce that gain when later work depends on earlier results. She borrows this definition from Ryan Greenblatt’s 2024 account of research labor acceleration, and simplifies his subsequent modeling. She expects uneven gains across tasks whose net value resembles uniform acceleration. Informal Anthropic staff reports of twelvefold productivity gains resemble her estimate numerically, but METR treats those polls as weak evidence and explicitly distinguishes individual productivity from company-wide progress. Its analysis connecting the two was redacted.

Cotra separately judges that Anthropic has probably crossed its policy threshold for dramatic acceleration. Anthropic’s policy requires observed or expected doubling against both a historical baseline and the expected pace without significant AI contributions, with substantial attribution to automation and further acceleration likely. Cotra relies partly on expectations that AI’s contribution has continued growing. Anthropic’s amended filing, covered on November 2, anticipated potentially meeting the threshold soon but designated no current model above it. Cotra’s judgment is her own.

Her one-year extrapolation assumes AI’s contribution increases smoothly, overcoming diminishing returns without intervening bottlenecks. If a year’s capability progress took AI from modest assistance to doubling progress, the next comparable advance would take six months, then three months, then roughly six weeks. An infinite sequence of halving intervals sums to one year. Cotra expects physical limits on software efficiency to intervene, but far beyond today’s capabilities, citing Tom Davidson and Tom Houlden’s Forethought analysis. She presents this as a conditional implication of public evidence and withholds a precise personal forecast because she may have seen other confidential information. METR found no clear near-term bottleneck, while expressing considerable uncertainty about compounding.

Cotra had described gradual research automation in a 2023 essay: successive models could perform more research, producing substantial acceleration before superintelligence. Daniel Eth and Davidson’s 2025 Forethought analysis formalized the contest between growing automated labor and diminishing returns. Greenblatt, in his November 5 thread, said METR’s findings substantially exceeded his expectations and revised his outlook; he subsequently corrected one calculation downward. These models make the future path depend on how much additional progress each increase in research effort produces.

Cotra argues that the industry lacks the safeguards such a feedback loop would require. She expects resulting systems could rapidly develop weapons and overpower civilization; severe misalignment could then lead to human extinction. Anthropic’s May 2025 policy required at least ASL-4 security against state-level model-weight theft at an earlier research-acceleration threshold she considers weaker. She cites recent OpenAI and Anthropic breaches, which she describes as amateur attacks, as evidence of inadequate security. Beyond protecting weights and algorithmic secrets, she calls for comprehensive monitoring, defenses against colluding monitors, convincingly tested alignment training, and governance able to slow development when safeguards fail.

Several responses question the inference from observed acceleration to future compounding. In the essay’s comments, Julian Bradshaw calculated that attributing four-fifths of an observed doubling’s increase to AI implies about 1.67-fold progress relative to the no-AI counterfactual, below Cotra’s 1.8-fold figure. Before her essay appeared, Toby Ord argued that a one-time improvement from the Mythos pre-training run could explain much of METR’s trend break. Karthik Tadepalli’s parallel analysis supported substantial acceleration but argued that research judgment could constrain compounding even as AI speeds up readily checkable work. Parker Whitfill and Cheryl Wu’s arXiv paper, “Will Compute Bottlenecks Prevent an Intelligence Explosion?”, found different relationships between compute and labor under different model specifications, illustrating uncertainty in the assumptions Cotra simplifies.

Nathan Lambert also challenged Cotra’s catastrophic-risk framing in the comments as alarming language without enough context; Daniel Kokotajlo replied that extinction would entail those deaths. Cotra closes by explaining how much her conclusion depends on trusting METR’s investigators. Most of their most informative evidence was redacted. Returning to her September argument for transparency, she wants outside researchers to inspect the evidence and compare models themselves.
