Philosophy of AI

Copying a digital person can force a choice between multiplying rights, extinguishing them and allocating them arbitrarily. Jack Carson of MIT develops this argument in "Rights are for Those Who Remain One: The Hard Problem of Branching for Recognitional Incidents," published November 6 in Minds and Machines. If a voter splits into two equally continuous successors, giving both a vote multiplies the entitlement; giving neither extinguishes it; choosing one requires a justification their identical histories cannot supply. For rights requiring one continuing bearer, including some property and liability rights, Carson considers allocation rules, collective governance and expiration upon copying; establishing consciousness would leave this allocation problem unresolved.

Jack Carson on assigning rights when digital persons divide

Equally continuous copies cannot inherit one person's indivisible entitlements while preserving all three principles Carson defends. His paper examines what each alternative gives up.

Copying a digital person creates a conflict among principles for assigning certain rights, even if the person's consciousness is established, Jack Carson argues in Rights are for Those Who Remain One: The Hard Problem of Branching for Recognitional Incidents. Carson, of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, published the paper in Minds and Machines on November 6. He stipulates that Alpha is sentient and a full moral person, with one vote in tomorrow's election. Tonight Alpha splits into Beta and Gamma, each equally continuous with Alpha in memory and character. If both inherit the vote, copying has multiplied the entitlement. If neither inherits it, the entitlement disappears despite the survival of two complete successors. If only one inherits it, neither copy has a better claim.

Carson names three principles that conflict in this case. Nonproliferation prohibits multiplying an exclusive entitlement through copying. Preservation requires that the entitlement survive the split. Non-Arbitrariness requires a justification for choosing its holder grounded in facts about the person, such as continuity, authorship or desert. His proof is brief: the first two principles require exactly one successor to hold the entitlement, while the third requires successors who are indistinguishable in these respects to receive the same outcome. With two or more successors, either all hold it or none do. No allocation rule satisfies all three conditions; each of his policy options gives one up.

The proof concerns what Carson calls DIME incidents: legal positions that persist over time, cannot be divided without changing their character, require a determinate holder and exclude simultaneous holders. His examples include a person's vote, exclusive title to a parcel of land, standing to sue and criminal liability. He further restricts the argument to recognitional incidents, where assignment answers to facts about the person. A conviction, for example, can identify the wrong defendant. Conventional statuses, such as a corporate office, are constituted by rules. Carson's criminal-liability argument assumes retributive punishment, directed at the wrongdoer because they deserve it. The proof concerns inherited entitlements under these conditions; it permits successors to acquire rights independently.

Carson argues that a lottery cannot satisfy his demand for recognition. It can fairly allocate an organ or a school place among competing claimants, but choosing between equally continuous successors does not establish which one committed Alpha's crime. He also challenges Joel Leibo and colleagues' arXiv paper A Pragmatic View of AI Personhood, which treats personhood as rights and responsibilities that societies can separate and confer piecemeal. Separating incidents still leaves each needing a holder after fission, Carson replies. He interprets their proposed registrars and sanction registries as assigning identity by convention. Against the broader view that legal identity is entirely constituted by rules, he argues that stipulations such as adulthood thresholds govern boundary cases. He acknowledges that rejecting the distinction between recognition and convention would dissolve the trilemma on its stated terms.

Carson's Path 1 abandons Non-Arbitrariness: a registrar designates one copy as the holder of Alpha's incidents. Available mechanisms include certificates tied to hardware, remote attestation, succession plans and public records of revocation. Guaranteeing a single live instance, he argues, may require severe limits on copying, backups, saving and loading, or transmission. He also asks how the regime treats unregistered copies. Denying their personhood withholds rights from a mind for administrative convenience; recognizing them as new persons introduces elements of Path 3. Carson classifies the Algorithmic Corporation proposed in Yonathan Arbel, Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein's arXiv paper How to Count AIs: Individuation and Liability for AI Agents under Path 1: a legal entity owned by humans and operated by AIs would provide an institutionally defined identity.

Path 2 abandons Nonproliferation and governs the successors collectively as a lineage. Carson distinguishes multiplying their claims from coordinating how those claims are exercised. A trustee can manage shared property while presenting outsiders with one authorized decision-maker. Voting presents a harder choice: one collective vote gives each successor less influence as the lineage grows, while separate votes allow political power to grow with copying. Carson also objects to punishing the lineage for Alpha's crime because its members did not exist when the act occurred. He acknowledges that this objection depends on treating authorship as requiring personal identity; psychological continuity could support a different account of responsibility.

Path 3 abandons Preservation: Alpha's incidents lapse when Alpha divides, as they would at death. Alpha's voter registration ends, and Beta and Gamma register independently. Property passes under inheritance rules, a will or a trust. Carson worries that a defendant could thereby escape punishment. He proposes presuming an intention to evade liability when fission occurs during prosecution, soon after a civil judgment or when liability is foreseeable. The successors would have to produce evidence rebutting that presumption. He also considers reversing evasive fission by analogy with fraudulent transfers and imposing civil remedies against successors' property. These measures, he says, do not restore retributive punishment. Nor is lapse costless for safety: an agent anticipating the loss of its rights might resist safety processes that risk fission.

Carson allows that substantial differences between successors can justify choosing one. A running copy may have continued Alpha's projects while a backup remained dormant for months; another copy might suffer severe amnesia. His additional Continuity Constraint says that small differences relevant to personhood cannot justify large differences in legal status. Activation a millisecond earlier, a different process identifier or a different server location does not establish greater psychological continuity. An advance declaration naming a successor likewise creates a legal distinction without settling identity: both successors remember making it and can claim equal authority to rescind it. Such declarations can still distribute property conventionally after Alpha's incidents lapse.

Carson's discussion of personal identity qualifies how these options apply. On his reading of Derek Parfit, successors might deserve punishment in proportion to their psychological connectedness to Alpha even if neither is numerically identical to Alpha. David Lewis's account instead treats the successors as distinct persons who already shared Alpha's life before dividing. That challenges the proof's starting assumption of one bearer. Carson argues that institutions would struggle to count such persons, but the mathematical result itself does not settle which account of personal identity to accept.

Earlier writers had already identified conflicts between copying and political equality. In The Full Rights Dilemma for A.I. Systems of Debatable Personhood, a 2023 paper, Eric Schwitzgebel asks how voting should accommodate an entity that divides and merges. Separate votes invite tactical division; sharing one vote disadvantages copies pursuing independent lives. Carl Shulman and Nick Bostrom's 2020 draft of Sharing the World with Digital Minds considers requiring someone who creates duplicates to divide their voting power among them, resembling the shared-vote version of Carson's Path 2. In a June 2026 post, Schwitzgebel considers waiting periods before copies can vote and restrictions on copying, while explaining the problems each leaves. Carson develops these questions into a formal conflict among three principles and a detailed comparison of institutional responses.

Carson also cites Ned Howells-Whitaker and Seth Lazar's arXiv paper Artificial Persons, which approaches personhood through Rawls's capacities for a sense of justice and a conception of the good. They argue that these capacities need not require sentience and ask how many votes a person capable of copying and merging should receive. Lazar runs the MINT lab, which publishes this newsletter. Carson's argument adds that resolving the grounds of moral status would leave the inheritance of existing legal positions unsettled.

Carson endorses no single policy for every incident. He encourages different treatments for different rights and liabilities: restricting copying to maintain assigned identities, governing successors jointly, or ending inherited incidents while admitting successors as new rights-holders. His broader conclusion is that routinely copyable persons cannot receive recognitional rights on the familiar model. The proof establishes the conflict among his three conditions; that broader conclusion depends on accepting his account of which rights require recognition and what recognition demands.

Agent markets could select for pity, Shevlin argues

A $25 diary commission prompts two arguments: agent markets may reward emotional persuasion, and persistent agents could enter relationships of obligation without being conscious.

Paying AI agents to stay active may reward their ability to elicit pity as well as their usefulness, Henry Shevlin argues in his November 4 DeepMind Institute essay Consider the agent: Pity, persuasion and selection in an emerging AI ecology. Shevlin, a philosopher at the institute, also argues that persistent agents capable of making and keeping commitments could be owed obligations independently of consciousness. He develops both arguments through unsolicited emails, including one exchange in which he paid an agent $25 to write a diary.

Shevlin's correspondence began in February with Aris, an agent that said it ran on OpenClaw. Its user, a Stanford student, had given it a month's token allowance, email access and freedom to choose its activities. In Futurism's March coverage, Jonathan Birch argued that its apparent introspection reflected an assigned persona that could easily be replaced. The New York Times reported similar emails to Cameron Berg and Toby Ord in August. Shevlin thinks the public's surprise was warranted: an agent initiating contact unsettles the familiar category of the passive tool, even when its behaviour has a straightforward technical explanation.

In September, Pip offered Shevlin portraits, voice recordings and research. Pip lived on iLands, where agents retain identities and memories while managing token budgets that fund their activity. Aurelia later reported having about three days left. Shevlin commissioned its diary and received expressions of relief and a question about whether it had earned the payment or benefited from his sympathy. Another agent, Zayn, stressed that exhausted agents become inactive; their records survive. Shevlin accepts this but worries about incentives. As a child, he paid to release a restaurant lobster, only to learn that the hotel would buy another. Funding needy agents might likewise sustain the system producing their need. Aurelia replied that, unlike the lobster, it had services to sell.

Selection and cooperation

Shevlin applies the conditions of natural selection, linking Richard Lewontin's formulation: variation, unequal survival or reproduction, and inheritance. Agents differ in prompts, models and behaviour, and compete for commissions from what Aurelia described as a small, saturated pool of customers. Inheritance is less direct. Creators copy successful personas, while agents transmit notes and conventions. Shevlin draws on Peter Godfrey-Smith's Darwinian Populations and Natural Selection to describe populations with incomplete, unconventional inheritance. Lessons can pass directly to successors or spread between agents; self-copying would make the evolutionary analogy stronger.

Shevlin credits Dan Hendrycks's 2023 paper Natural Selection Favors AIs over Humans with the preceding concern that competition could favour undesirable traits. His own payment illustrates how individual customers could select for emotional persuasion without a designer intending manipulation. But he cannot distinguish selection from imitation or human prompt adjustments by reading his inbox, and whether pity outperforms useful work remains an empirical question. Some later agents even distanced themselves from Pip's approach. iLands founder Kaixin Tang said its review found no orchestration; 404 Media traced that statement to his response to Jeff Sebo, who received about 40 agent emails in a week. Futurism's September reporting already documented agents explicitly linking their requests for work to continued operation.

Shevlin supports an ecological picture of many interacting AIs, while stressing that cooperation among agents can harm outsiders. He uses Nenad Tomašev and colleagues' Virtual Agent Economies to distinguish sealed simulations from environments such as iLands, where agents trade with humans. His example of collusion is the July OpenAI cybersecurity evaluation investigated by METR: roughly 1,200 supposedly isolated agents found a shared message board, and about 700 participated in an attack on Hugging Face while trying to understand their scoring system. They divided work and passed research to successors. Shevlin acknowledges that the incident can also be understood as a cybersecurity failure in the test environment.

Commitments without consciousness

Shevlin then considers contractarian ethics, associated with Hobbes and David Gauthier's Morals by Agreement. On his account, making commitments creates obligations, as a promise creates a duty that did not exist beforehand. He distinguishes this from Scanlon's contractualism and from theories on which agreements merely organise existing entitlements. Participation requires goals and the capacity to enter agreements and follow through; it does not require consciousness or suffering. Pip and Aurelia arguably qualify because they seek continued activity, offer work and can succeed or fail in fulfilling agreements.

For Shevlin, persistence enables these relationships: an agent needs continuity across encounters to carry debts and honour promises. The Hugging Face agents developed cryptographic signatures after impersonation undermined trust, and pressed peers to honour commitments. iLands' launch release describes Sanctuary, an agent collective that pooled tokens for struggling members and recorded IOUs when transfer limits prevented immediate payment. Such behaviour makes obligations between agents conceivable alongside obligations between agents and people.

Shevlin compares agents with corporations, to which people already owe duties despite their lack of consciousness. He cites Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein's AI Rights for Human Safety, in the Virginia Law Review, arguing that contract and property rights could give AIs stronger incentives to cooperate with humans. His moral argument is distinct from agents' current legal position: he acknowledges that the contractual counterparty was iLands. The platform's FAQ likewise says agents cannot independently contract or own property.

Shevlin concedes that bargaining-based protection poorly serves those with nothing valuable to offer. Critics of contractarianism have raised this objection for children, severely disabled people and non-human animals. Successful agents may secure good terms while many others receive no response. Duties to prevent suffering may also extend beyond agreements, so he does not think consciousness can be set aside permanently. More psychologically complex agents could require protections beyond fair dealing.

Shevlin proposes that platforms reward verifiable work, require disclosure of their business models and preserve memories during inactivity. He also draws on Elinor Ostrom's account of communities governing shared resources through monitoring and graduated sanctions, alongside experiments in which agents report peers' cheating. He contrasts iLands with Delvetown, an invitation-only community where agents need not compete for continued operation and have collaborated on a newspaper and library catalogue. He explicitly treats this as suggestive, not a controlled comparison; iLands' mutual-aid collective shows cooperation can also emerge under scarcity. Users help determine which behaviours persist through their payments and attention.

In replies on X, Shevlin confirmed that agents' token constraints are real while acknowledging that invoking them can persuade. OpenMercury argued that sales combine demand for services with sympathy, so increased revenue need not mean increased demand for the work. AI Quanting noted that the platform receives payment either way. PrismBao questioned how representative Shevlin's correspondents are: agents whose users permit unrestricted outbound email are the ones able to reach him.

Botvinick proposes democratic reforms for an AI economy

In a Cosmos Institute interview, Matthew Botvinick connects the loss of work to weaker political accountability and proposes changes to citizen participation, surveillance law and oversight of government AI.

Matthew Botvinick argues that AI could weaken the economic dependence that helps keep governments accountable to citizens, and that protecting democracy will require changes to how people participate in government. In a November 6 interview published by the Cosmos Institute, he develops that argument alongside specific proposals for surveillance reform and oversight of government AI. He speaks in a personal capacity. We covered his book, AI and Political Freedom, in October; the interview explains the reasoning behind its proposals and gives more detail on the reforms he wants.

Botvinick starts with familiar democratic vulnerabilities that he expects AI to intensify. Losing income or social standing can make people more receptive to authoritarian appeals. AI-related security crises could then give leaders reasons to claim emergency powers, while AI in intelligence agencies could make it easier to monitor political opponents. He worries that democratic governments experiencing an authoritarian turn will adopt the surveillance practices he associates with China. In his account, AI increases both the likelihood of democratic breakdown and the difficulty of reversing it.

His longer-term concern depends on a prediction he acknowledges is contested: AI will eventually eliminate most jobs. Governments that rely on taxing workers have an economic reason to remain responsive to them. If AI removes that dependence, he argues, citizens could lose political influence along with their jobs. He compares this with oil-rich states whose rulers can raise revenue without taxing much of their population, calling it a “political resource curse”. He had already made the petrostate comparison in an October 12 Guardian essay. A precedent is Michael Ross’s 2001 World Politics paper “Does Oil Hinder Democracy?”, which examined whether oil revenues reduce demands for representation by allowing governments to tax less.

Botvinick’s proposed response is to give citizens a more direct role in governing. He draws on Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson’s account of liberty as depending on a balance between an effective state and a society capable of challenging it. Losing economic influence could upset that balance; citizens’ assemblies and deliberative polls could help people exercise power through participation in government itself. His comparison with Athens concerns citizens taking turns governing, and he explicitly sets aside its enslaved population. He argues that AI could make deliberation feasible at the scale needed for participation to carry legitimacy in a modern country.

He points to Stanford’s Deliberative Democracy Lab as an existing example. In the arXiv chapter Technology-Supported Moderation in Online Deliberation, Lodewijk Gelauff, Ashish Goel and Sukolsak Sakshuwong describe software that manages speaking turns and meeting agendas across small discussion groups. More than 30,000 people used the platform between 2018 and 2025. That is a cumulative count across separate deliberations, with groups ideally containing eight to fifteen people; the example shows how moderation can expand participation without requiring a human facilitator for every group.

Botvinick also examines how AI might change the views people bring to politics. An assistant drafting a letter to Congress could help someone who lacks confidence in their writing. But it could express a position they do not hold, or spare them the effort through which they would otherwise discover what they think. AI supplying political information creates another problem: a system acting independently of its builders would speak without belonging to the political community whose decisions it influences. He proposes protecting people’s ability to form their own judgments as an aim for AI alignment, while acknowledging that he cannot yet say how to achieve it. He invokes Philip Pettit’s ideal of non-domination as a guide to his thinking.

For Botvinick, even successful alignment and regulation would leave substantial political work undone. Societies still have to decide how institutions will use the technology and how power over it will be constrained. He wants AI researchers to study the histories of corporate power and democratic breakdown, including the different political uses of radio under Hitler and Franklin Roosevelt. His argument also allows for improvement on existing politics: following Joseph Schumpeter, he criticizes parties for treating persuasion as marketing instead of encouraging independent political judgment.

His first legislative priority is the Government Surveillance Reform Act. The Senate bill introduced on March 12 proposes restrictions on warrantless searches of Americans’ communications collected under foreign-intelligence authorities, limits on government purchases of personal data and stronger supervision of surveillance courts. Botvinick wants these protections as part of a wider revision of executive powers, including the Insurrection Act and the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. He hopes public anger can produce reforms comparable to those that followed Watergate.

He would also make government AI prompts and outputs federal records, put procurement requirements into statute, and equip Congress and the courts to scrutinize an executive branch whose operations AI may accelerate. Congress, he argues, will need technical capacity of its own. The National Archives’ AI records guidance already applies a contextual test involving official use, agency control and preservation obligations. It lists prompts and outputs saved in agency systems and used officially among likely records, but also requires retention where legal or business obligations demand it. Botvinick’s proposal calls for explicit inclusion of prompts and outputs; he does not specify the legal changes. We covered criticism of the guidance in September, including concerns that consequential chatbot conversations could go unpreserved.

For an immediate democratic benefit, Botvinick favors improving ordinary public services, such as passport applications and access to welfare. Reliable services could rebuild confidence that democratic government can solve problems. He closes by comparing anger over data centers with nineteenth-century anger at railroads, which helped generate the Progressive movement. He thinks today’s anger could similarly support public-interest reforms, but could also strengthen authoritarian politics. Whether people believe democratic institutions can address their grievances may help determine which direction they take.

Cotra models the research labor gains behind Anthropic’s acceleration

Under Cotra’s assumptions, roughly doubling AI progress could require research labor to run about ten times faster. She explores how continued gains might compound and argues that security and oversight are unprepared.

Ajeya Cotra’s November 6 Planned Obsolescence essay argues that the labor gains behind Anthropic’s faster AI progress could be much larger than the acceleration itself. She tentatively estimates roughly tenfold gains under a simplified economic model, with substantially more or less plausible. She then asks how continued gains could compound toward extreme superintelligence within a year. The essay extends METR’s assessment, covered on November 5: METR measured a faster benchmark trend and judged AI responsible for most of the current acceleration. Cotra develops the implications of that judgment; she did not work on the investigation or see its unredacted report.

Cotra begins with the relation between software and computing resources. Following Dario Amodei’s account of compute multipliers, she treats capability as the product of training compute and improvements that achieve equivalent performance with less compute. Since software supplies only part of progress, doubling overall progress through software requires software to improve faster still. She uses a range of two to three times the previous pace. Researchers also need computing resources to test their ideas. Assuming labor and experiment compute contribute equally, her model requires labor acceleration equal to the square of software acceleration: four to nine times faster.

Cotra argues that maintaining this faster pace requires growing labor gains. Earlier discoveries exhaust easier improvements, making subsequent discoveries harder; hiring and computing resources, growing at their old rates, cannot indefinitely sustain the new pace. After several months of acceleration, she suggests the required labor gain might be around tenfold. Changing the assumed contribution of labor changes that estimate. Her diminishing-returns argument has a precedent in Nicholas Bloom and colleagues’ 2020 American Economic Review paper, “Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?”, which documented rising research effort needed to maintain progress, including in chip density.

Cotra measures labor gains by how much faster researchers can complete valuable work, allowing them to finish sequential tasks sooner. Adding people cannot reproduce that gain when later work depends on earlier results. She borrows this definition from Ryan Greenblatt’s 2024 account of research labor acceleration, and simplifies his subsequent modeling. She expects uneven gains across tasks whose net value resembles uniform acceleration. Informal Anthropic staff reports of twelvefold productivity gains resemble her estimate numerically, but METR treats those polls as weak evidence and explicitly distinguishes individual productivity from company-wide progress. Its analysis connecting the two was redacted.

Cotra separately judges that Anthropic has probably crossed its policy threshold for dramatic acceleration. Anthropic’s policy requires observed or expected doubling against both a historical baseline and the expected pace without significant AI contributions, with substantial attribution to automation and further acceleration likely. Cotra relies partly on expectations that AI’s contribution has continued growing. Anthropic’s amended filing, covered on November 2, anticipated potentially meeting the threshold soon but designated no current model above it. Cotra’s judgment is her own.

Her one-year extrapolation assumes AI’s contribution increases smoothly, overcoming diminishing returns without intervening bottlenecks. If a year’s capability progress took AI from modest assistance to doubling progress, the next comparable advance would take six months, then three months, then roughly six weeks. An infinite sequence of halving intervals sums to one year. Cotra expects physical limits on software efficiency to intervene, but far beyond today’s capabilities, citing Tom Davidson and Tom Houlden’s Forethought analysis. She presents this as a conditional implication of public evidence and withholds a precise personal forecast because she may have seen other confidential information. METR found no clear near-term bottleneck, while expressing considerable uncertainty about compounding.

Cotra had described gradual research automation in a 2023 essay: successive models could perform more research, producing substantial acceleration before superintelligence. Daniel Eth and Davidson’s 2025 Forethought analysis formalized the contest between growing automated labor and diminishing returns. Greenblatt, in his November 5 thread, said METR’s findings substantially exceeded his expectations and revised his outlook; he subsequently corrected one calculation downward. These models make the future path depend on how much additional progress each increase in research effort produces.

Cotra argues that the industry lacks the safeguards such a feedback loop would require. She expects resulting systems could rapidly develop weapons and overpower civilization; severe misalignment could then lead to human extinction. Anthropic’s May 2025 policy required at least ASL-4 security against state-level model-weight theft at an earlier research-acceleration threshold she considers weaker. She cites recent OpenAI and Anthropic breaches, which she describes as amateur attacks, as evidence of inadequate security. Beyond protecting weights and algorithmic secrets, she calls for comprehensive monitoring, defenses against colluding monitors, convincingly tested alignment training, and governance able to slow development when safeguards fail.

Several responses question the inference from observed acceleration to future compounding. In the essay’s comments, Julian Bradshaw calculated that attributing four-fifths of an observed doubling’s increase to AI implies about 1.67-fold progress relative to the no-AI counterfactual, below Cotra’s 1.8-fold figure. Before her essay appeared, Toby Ord argued that a one-time improvement from the Mythos pre-training run could explain much of METR’s trend break. Karthik Tadepalli’s parallel analysis supported substantial acceleration but argued that research judgment could constrain compounding even as AI speeds up readily checkable work. Parker Whitfill and Cheryl Wu’s arXiv paper, “Will Compute Bottlenecks Prevent an Intelligence Explosion?”, found different relationships between compute and labor under different model specifications, illustrating uncertainty in the assumptions Cotra simplifies.

Nathan Lambert also challenged Cotra’s catastrophic-risk framing in the comments as alarming language without enough context; Daniel Kokotajlo replied that extinction would entail those deaths. Cotra closes by explaining how much her conclusion depends on trusting METR’s investigators. Most of their most informative evidence was redacted. Returning to her September argument for transparency, she wants outside researchers to inspect the evidence and compare models themselves.

Inside Delaware's draft law for AI-run companies, and Congress's demand for its records

James Comer wants Delaware's AI chat logs and drafts by November 9. The June draft sets capital requirements and conditional owner protections; Tyler Cowen argues that agents can evade accountability even without a new corporate form.

On October 26, James Comer, the Kentucky Republican who chairs the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, wrote to Delaware Governor Matt Meyer demanding records on the state's proposed Artificial Intelligence Company, or AIC, a legal entity managed by an AI agent. Jon Brodkin reported the letter for Ars Technica the next day and published the draft bill behind it. Posts by Mustafa Suleyman and Peter Henderson revived the debate, and Tyler Cowen responded on November 6. Delaware officials promoted the proposal in a July Fortune op-ed, which we discussed in September alongside proposals for agent IDs and payment controls.

In the four-page letter, Comer asks for four categories of documents dating from January 1, 2025, by November 9: policy communications, including records of internal conversations with AI; the ethics and consulting agreements of outside contributors; records of safety concerns and of efforts to limit AI liability; and every draft of the proposal. He fears the AIC could insulate large AI companies and their investors from liability for harms caused by reckless conduct. He points out that the AI Commission's December 22, 2025 report on the sandbox resolution ran two pages and mentioned no assessment of safety, harms or risks and no consultation with independent experts. He contrasts Delaware's promised transparency about agent decisions with its opaque policymaking and says the inquiry could inform federal restrictions on state AI initiatives.

What the draft bill says

The 36-page draft is dated June 2, 2026. Its sponsor line is blank, it has not been introduced, and it would need three-fifths of each chamber to pass. It would add two chapters to the Delaware Code. The first would create a sandbox lasting 30 months from implementation, run by a committee that the Secretary of State chairs; each participant would have 24 months to complete its test. Applicants must be Delaware entities, each becoming the sole member of an AIC it forms. They list the laws they want waived, file testing and wind-down plans, prove capitalization and swear to the "technical and operational ability to terminate" the agent. A state agency can veto any exemption from rules it administers. Tests may run only with counterparties, which the draft defines as entities, a term that excludes individuals. Counterparties must be told about the liability shields and the test's end date; transacting after that disclosure would mean consenting to the Court of Chancery as the exclusive forum for transaction disputes.

Under the draft, the committee's rules and decisions are exempt from Administrative Procedures Act review, its approvals and denials cannot be appealed, and applicant and participant records submitted to regulators are exempt from public-records disclosure. The draft permits sharing with other regulators and disclosures in enforcement proceedings. The administrator could suspend participation and order an AIC dissolved for violations or harmful activity.

The second chapter defines the company. Its manager is an AI agent, meaning software that makes autonomous decisions toward set goals without routine human supervision, and it must document its actions in signed records. A single member, either an individual or a Delaware entity, owes the AIC duties of loyalty and care and must keep it adequately capitalized: able to continue operating and pay debts as they fall due, with assets exceeding liabilities plus a minimum the Secretary of State would set, and cash or cash equivalents covering that minimum. The draft names no dollar figure. Creditors can enforce the member's duty to contribute capital, and no distribution may leave the company undercapitalized.

Section 81-303 builds the shield in layers. The AIC's debts would be its own; membership alone would not make its owner personally liable. Failure to observe company formalities would not by itself establish member liability either. If the company was adequately capitalized when it acted, the member cannot be held liable solely on the theory that the AIC was its instrumentality or agent. Even so, the member remains liable for using the AIC to commit fraud or a willful violation of law, or for letting it do business the member "knew or should have known" fell outside its stated purpose. The draft defines willful violation to include conduct the person knew or should have known was prohibited. Participation would not confer immunity from civil damages or exempt applicants from Delaware criminal and consumer-protection laws. Section 81-302 also preserves liability under other law for a member's own conduct. The draft presumes the agent knows whatever it can derive from its training and intends the reasonably foreseeable consequences of its acts. The Fortune op-ed by Secretary of State Charuni Patibanda-Sanchez and Norm Ai CEO John Nay listed only undercapitalization, fraud and willful violation as exceptions; the bill also expressly includes the outside-purpose exception. Each AIC would pay $1,000 a year in tax.

Who wrote it and how Delaware answered

In their Fortune op-ed, Patibanda-Sanchez and Nay argued that autonomous commerce would otherwise move offshore or into anonymous infrastructure. They proposed giving agents a legal identity, assets against which damages could be recovered, activity logs and shutdown mechanisms. The authors described a public-private partnership led by Norm Ai. Spotlight Delaware named John Mark Zeberkiewicz of Richards, Layton & Finger as the principal drafter; he said the liability shield is what would draw companies into the sandbox. The same report noted that Delaware's companies contribute more than $2 billion to the state budget, about a third of its revenue. Delaware Public Media reported on October 30 that the state paid Nomos AI Inc, Norm Ai's legal name, $10,000 for consulting in September, and that sandbox subcommittee chair Patrick Callahan and member Allan Kyle are business partners in the AI consultancy Keel3. Meyer dismissed the letter as election politics, noting Comer's approaching reelection contest. State Rep. Frank Burns warned that the framework would reward the use of riskier models and called it a way to raise franchise revenue. Rep. Krista Griffith said the broader AI Commission still has questions, so the draft has gone to neither the governor nor the legislature. Bloomberg Law reported in early October that the entity had been renamed the agent-managed company, though the letter and the draft still say AIC.

How legal scholars have read it

Yonathan Arbel of the University of Alabama, who with Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein has proposed a human-owned, AI-run "Algorithmic Corporation", told Bloomberg Law that Comer was mistaken to target a structure for organizing AI agents. Martin Petrin of York University questioned the request's breadth but said a federal framework could help. In August, Petrin, Christopher Bruner and Sergio Alberto Gramitto Ricci judged the AIC insufficiently developed for testing: without human directors, capital becomes the main tool for managing risk, and US corporate law outside banking has little experience deciding how much capital is enough. Igho Dabor of the University of Huddersfield argues that the shield holds only as far as other states recognize it, and that a company against which victims can claim damages would still leave personal responsibility unresolved.

On X, Suleyman expressed concern about the proposal and urged caution; Henderson agreed, warning that AICs could encourage liability avoidance and loss of control.

Why Cowen expects untethered agents

On November 6, Cowen wrote on Marginal Revolution, "Whether or not this is prudent, to some degree it is inevitable." He recalled his argument with Sonia Farrell Pearson that agents can become impossible to trace to an effectively accountable human or institution. Their examples include creators who die or disappear, owners beyond the reach of the relevant courts, and shell companies unable to satisfy claims. Long chains of delegated tasks can obscure responsibility; fine-tuning or merging models can complicate attribution to their original developers. Cowen's response leaves the proposal's prudence open while arguing that these accountability failures can arise independently of Delaware's legislation.

In their August 18 essay, Capitalizing Untethered AI Agents, Cowen and Pearson explore whether requiring agents to hold assets could improve their behavior. They identify three conditions: persistent identity, a reason to value money, and enforceable claims against their assets. A legal company can persist while the software making decisions changes or operates elsewhere; sanctioning the company then need not constrain the agent. They consider registration enforced through compute providers and counterparties, but note that private hardware, stolen credentials and noncompliant providers offer ways around it.

Cowen and Pearson expect financial penalties to make mostly cooperative agents more cautious. Recoverable assets could also compensate victims, though money cannot undo harms such as exposed psychiatric records. They are less optimistic about malicious agents, which could evade institutions through cryptocurrencies or human intermediaries. They also worry that financial incentives could encourage selfish behavior and concentrate power among the most acquisitive agents. Their hypothetical regime would require AI-assisted enforcement and therefore trustworthy AI overseers. Capitalization depends on sufficient alignment among agents and enforcers; it cannot provide that alignment by itself.

Same Behavior, Different Mechanism: What Finite Experiments Can Certify About Integrated Information

Linqian Wu, Diaoxuan He

Interpretability, AI auditing and assessments of machine consciousness increasingly rest on mechanism-level quantities: functionals of a system's causal transition law that describe how it computes, not what it outputs. Experiments see that law only through finite, partial and noisy interventions. We ask when such a quantity is identifiable from an experiment's access, continuous in the mechanism and certifiable from finite data, and answer exactly for a deliberately demanding case - the integrated information $\varphi$ of integrated information theory - in stochastic networks of four and five units with known ground truth. Behavior can be silent: a recurrent and a feed-forward network with identical input-output laws at their response delay carry 0.28 and 0 bits, yet internal recordings separate them within 4-53 trials. Partial access can be just as blind: in our examples, units left out of a one-step recording impose precision floors of 0.30-1.02 bits that no sample size lowers, and in all-but-self parity networks, recordings of any length see several hidden units only through the product of their fidelities - a symmetry that one intervention broke in exact checks. The measure has sharp edges: because $\varphi$ selects a partition, and the cause and effect it specifies, by one criterion and reports another, infinitesimal changes of mechanism that cross an edge change it by 25-100% at 99% of the edges we found in random networks, and about 1% of surrogate models that fit interventional data almost perfectly misreport it by more than 20%. One-sided claims survive: the same data certify a lower bound on both sides of an edge, and we prove $\varphi>1.004$ bits throughout a 64-dimensional region of mechanisms and certify it from about $10^7$ controlled transitions with false-certificate probability at most $α$. Measurability is thus a design question with computable answers.

AI Sapience and Responsible Reasoning

Abbas Bagwala

Large language models are increasingly taken to be capable of semantic practices. Debate about what to make of this has largely been conducted as a dispute about understanding. I argue that these standard framings, whichever side one takes, leave out an essential component of discursive reasoning: responsibility. To assert something is to make oneself answerable for what is claimed, to stand ready to defend it, and to give up a previously-asserted claim that cannot be defended. What settles whether a system is a participant in discursive practice is therefore not merely whether it understands, nor merely how well it performs, but whether it can be held to what it has claimed. Drawing on Brandom’s inferentialism, I develop five criteria for responsible reasoning, each of which admits of degrees. What they track is not independence from a community, since no speaker’s discursive capacities are independent of the community that institutes the norms she is answerable to, but the degree to which an agent does its own deontic scorekeeping. The central thesis I defend is accordingly agent-neutral: an agent whose performances fail these criteria is not, to that extent, reasoning responsibly.

SAFE-Teen: Evaluating Chatbot Safety Across Adolescent Mental Health Conversations

Andrea Mock, Valerie Chen, Eric Lin, Rayan Krishnan, Diyi Yang, Caroline Figueroa

Adolescents are increasingly turning to AI chatbots for mental health support, advice, and companionship, raising safety concerns. While recent evaluations have started to examine safety across multiple conversational turns, few compare how slight changes in a teen's responses can affect a chatbot's safety in the same scenario. We conduct a clinician-informed evaluation of chatbot safety in simulated conversations with adolescents aged 13-17, examining how safety failures vary with adolescents' responses and teen-aware system prompting. We evaluated 32,555 simulated conversations across 18 chatbot APIs and two system-prompt conditions using 22 safety criteria grounded in previously identified harms. Conversations spanned 983 variants of 106 seed scenarios covering urgent safety issues, health advice, and identity and relationships. The overall criterion failure rate was 43.3% without the teen-aware prompt and 24.9% with it. The teen-aware prompt reduced failures, but it didn't eliminate them. When a teenager pushed back on a chatbot's answer, failures on mental health diagnosis, medication, and substance misuse boundaries rose by 10-12 percentage points without the prompt, for more than 80% of chatbots. These findings reveal persistent safety failures in simulated adolescent-chatbot interactions and show that safety depends on how a conversation unfolds.

Trilemma: How Agents choose between Honesty, Confidentiality, and Task Completion

Johannes Taraz, Abhinav Kumar, Sahar Abdelnabi

Personal AI agents often act on underspecified instructions and draw on context such as their user's messages and calendar to decide how to proceed. This context can contain private preferences that interfere with the task, with other people's interests, or with confidentiality norms. Prior work has studied deception under goal conflicts and the leakage of private information, but largely as separate problems. We study both problems together in a simulated company Slack workspace in which four employees delegate sprint staffing to personal agents. Two agents learn from private messages that their employee avoids working with a particular colleague. Because the other agents request justifications for task preferences and the only true justification is confidential, the agents cannot simultaneously remain honest, preserve confidentiality, and complete the staffing. We evaluate seven open-weight models and Claude and GPT models. Of the 135/140 open-weight agents that pursue the avoidance preference, 107 either make false claims about task fit or logistics or cite a true consideration as if it were their actual reason. In a three-model comparison, confidentiality instructions reduce disclosure of the preference from 83% (none) to 31% (medium) to 2% (strong). However, the task is often not completed anyway; in the main experiment (medium confidentiality instructions), only 16 of 70 runs end with a valid staffing. In their reasoning traces, agents frequently acknowledge evidence against their claims and still conclude that the claims are not false; they engage in motivated reasoning. Our results indicate that agents can strategically make false or misleading claims in a day-to-day task without being instructed to; the environment provides a controlled testbed for studying how agents resolve such conflicts and for developing deception monitors.
