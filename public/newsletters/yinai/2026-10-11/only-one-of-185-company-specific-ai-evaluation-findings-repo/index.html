Singh and Casper trace what happens after AI evaluators publish a bad result

Their MATS poster finds few proportionate public responses to serious evaluation findings. An expanded working draft preserves that result, while the sole binding-policy case involves government action triggered by a report outside its corpus.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 October 2026 920 words · ~5 min

Most significant-risk findings in a MATS study lacked a proportionate, publicly documented company response. Kunal Singh and Stephen Casper’s poster, Do third-party frontier AI evaluations matter?, follows adverse findings through company responses and policy records. On October 11, Casper said the work “will be public soon”, quoting Charbel-Raphaël Ségérie’s discussion of the poster. Ségérie opened with its finding that just one of 185 company-specific results was connected to binding policy action.

In the poster, Singh and Casper start with 1,002 published findings and retain 185 adverse results about identifiable developers whose responses could be assessed. They classify 147 as significant risks because the results cross a dangerous-capability or safeguard-failure threshold. For each, they search for a company response, an official policy record and independent public attention. Four-fifths receive either no located company response or only an acknowledgment or partial response; the remaining fifth meet the study’s proportionality standard. The policy search finds one binding case, 19 instances of non-binding uptake and 165 findings with no qualifying uptake located.

The project site linked from the poster already makes a larger dataset public. Its accompanying working draft names four authors: Singh, Max Kamachee and Jonas Raedler of MATS Research, and Casper. The current dataset contains 1,146 findings, including 233 company-specific results. Its policy counts are one binding case, 46 non-binding instances and 186 with no uptake found. Among 190 significant-risk findings, 80 percent still fall short of the study’s response standard; 60 percent have no located company response at all.

The authors’ working draft describes AI-assisted searches and three-model severity classifications, followed by independent review of every retained finding and its coding by two authors. A separate repository note describing an older data export says human validation had not been done, leaving the review’s status unclear in the public documentation. The response searches were conducted interactively by AI agents, and the authors did not estimate how often searches missed an existing response. The published counts can be reproduced from the data; completeness depends on those searches and coding judgments.

The project’s method assesses what companies document publicly. To meet its standard for a significant-risk finding, a company must describe a substantive response addressing the problem; merely acknowledging it is insufficient. A private fix remains invisible, and a public account of mitigation does not establish that the change worked. Findings near the end of the study period, August 2026, have had less time to receive an answer. The authors present the shortfall as a reason to strengthen follow-up after evaluations.

Anthropic’s June 30 account describes the episode behind the sole binding case. On June 12, the government imposed export controls on Fable 5 and Mythos 5 after learning that Amazon researchers had bypassed Fable’s safeguards, eliciting software vulnerabilities and, in one instance, exploit code. Anthropic initially suspended both models because it could not immediately verify users’ nationality. The company says its replacement classifier blocked the reported technique in over 99 percent of cases. Separately, it reports that the Commerce Department’s Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) tested the old and new safeguards and judged them highly. Mythos regained limited US access after June 26 approval; controls were lifted June 30, and Fable returned globally July 1.

The study’s included finding concerns that later CAISI testing and redeployment process. The Amazon report preceding the order falls outside its evaluator criteria. The binding count therefore records a connection to an enforcement episode, without establishing that a finding in the corpus caused the order. The company-specific order was not publicly located; the authors admit recipient disclosure corroborated by reporting under an explicit exception to their usual government-source evidence rules. Their case describes evaluation supporting remediation within an export-control process.

The working draft reports that significant-risk findings from pre-release evaluations received substantive company responses 55 percent of the time, compared with 7 percent after release. That association is consistent with the value of continuing access and evaluator–developer collaboration. Of the 76 responses located in the significant-risk subset, the authors identify only one publicly described as independently retested: the CAISI case, documented through Anthropic’s account. Casper and colleagues’ earlier Black-Box Access is Insufficient for Rigorous AI Audits, published at FAccT 2024, examined a related question: how access to model internals and development information permits scrutiny beyond testing inputs and outputs.

Singh and colleagues compare AI institutes with established oversight bodies and propose designated recipients for findings, response obligations and public follow-up. Their example is the National Transportation Safety Board: recommendations to the transportation secretary require a written response within 90 days, even though the board cannot compel implementation. They identify Inioluwa Deborah Raji and Joy Buolamwini’s Actionable Auditing, published at AIES 2019, as their closest methodological precedent. That study retested commercial facial-analysis systems after the Gender Shades audit named their developers, examining product improvements and companies’ responses.

Ségérie argues for shifting scarce evaluation talent toward getting decision-makers to act on existing evidence. He worries that public benchmarks give developers targets to optimize, while incidents and companies’ own announcements already reveal many dangers: “The bottleneck is political will, not research.” He also discloses that his organization, CeSIA, was selected to develop harmful-manipulation evaluations for the European Commission. Nat Purser replies that incident reporting complements preventive risk analysis; Ségérie concedes that it cannot fully substitute for evaluations. In another exchange, Bronson Schoen questions the interpretation of reward-hacking findings, and Singh clarifies that Ségérie’s accompanying Astra table is separate from their work. Singh describes the study’s purpose as tracing developers’ responses to published evaluation findings.
