Tim Gowers on the future of mathematicians as explainers

In an interview published on October 3, the Fields medallist fears that AI will erode the motivation to train mathematicians capable of understanding its results.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 October 2026 1127 words · ~6 min

Rowland Manthorpe, Sky News technology correspondent, published “The end of mathematicians?” on October 3: a lightly edited, 3,000-word interview transcript with Tim Gowers, the Fields medallist at Cambridge and the Collège de France. Manthorpe and producer Eleonora Chiarella recorded it for a film about mathematics, which used only a few clips. He recirculated a clip and the transcript on October 11. Gowers worries that AI could remove the pleasures of discovery that motivate years of mathematical training, leaving too few people able to understand what machines produce. Manthorpe sees the possible move from discovering results to explaining them as a predicament other professions will face.

Gowers describes a progression from Olympiad questions to research problems and major discoveries, saying models advanced from ordinary doctoral-student ability to the level of leading mathematicians during 2026. He refers loosely to Navier–Stokes stability and a Millennium problem being solved. In the September 8 announcement we covered, OpenAI claimed a proof that a fluid initially at rest, subject to a smooth external force, can develop unbounded velocity in finite time while retaining finite energy. OpenAI explicitly claims alternatives C and D of Clay’s formulation, which allow forcing; this does not establish unforced Navier–Stokes blowup. The company says it does not intend to claim the prize. Gowers guesses that building extensive theory may still be harder for models, but expects competition to make AI use unavoidable for working mathematicians.

Gowers describes both alarm at his profession’s possible disappearance and excitement at seeing longstanding questions answered. He recalls struggling for as long as two years before a solution arrived, with an intensity of satisfaction he fears losing. If AI takes over discovery, students may lose the motivation to spend years acquiring the expertise needed to understand its results, leaving “an incredible tool that nobody can use”. He presents a collapse in PhD interest as a feared possibility. His pleasure in seeing AI solve a problem depends partly on whether he had hoped to solve it himself: an answer to a question he never realistically expected to settle is welcome. He is less confident that humans can digest discoveries fast enough to sustain a much larger mathematical enterprise.

Gowers distinguishes his perspective as a pure mathematician from that of people who use mathematics. A physicist may welcome a larger stock of available tools, and mathematicians could help physicists, engineers and economists find useful results. Gowers acknowledges that AI might also perform that task. He does not fully share colleagues’ view that understanding has always been the primary purpose of mathematics and solving problems only a means to it. He gives greater social weight to the community’s collective command of mathematics than to individual researchers’ understanding of particular results. If humans cannot solve anything machines cannot, he envisages a profession devoted to studying theories, their relationships and their applications. He considers that useful work, but expects it to be harder to attract students and political support.

Manthorpe compares that future to literary scholarship: academics interpret Shakespeare without having written his plays. He refers to Noah Smith’s August 4 essay “The end of the age of heroes”, which Gowers says he has read. Smith associates mathematical heroism with the need for each discovery to originate in a human mind. He expects society to keep paying “professional math-understanders”, estimating American academic mathematicians’ salaries at $4–5 billion, comparable to spending on bowling alleys or Halloween candy. Gowers takes some comfort from literature’s ability to recruit people who aspire to informed scholarship without expecting heroic distinction. That could sustain mathematics across generations, though he is less confident than Smith about securing support.

Asked about the speed of progress, Gowers says “I’ve been shocked, but I’ve not been meta-shocked.” He identifies AI’s reported solution of the unit-distance problem in his own field as his first major jolt; in May he described it as one of Erdős’s favourite questions. Repeated breakthroughs have made further shocks less surprising. He had hoped for more time to adjust before the hardest problems fell. On advice to new entrants, he wants another year to assess the consequences. He describes anxious doctoral students but says they have not been barred from starting, and rejects doing so. Department closures are a future fear; he says mathematicians have no coherent plan.

Gowers developed similar arguments in his September 17 essay “Why I didn’t sign the Fields medallists’ letter”, which Manthorpe links. Responding to the declaration signed by 25 medallists, he agreed mathematics faced a crisis but disputed subordinating problem-solving to understanding. His older essay The Two Cultures of Mathematics distinguished mathematicians oriented towards problems from those oriented towards theories. In September he imagined 1,000 important new results annually, perhaps 30 for each specialist community to absorb. Even if many remained unread, humans might understand more mathematics overall. He worried that recruitment and funding could fail, destroying the institutions that make such understanding possible.

Gowers had already raised the training difficulty in a May 8 account of using ChatGPT 5.5 Pro. In under two hours, it produced an extension of existing work that he judged substantial enough for a respectable combinatorics PhD chapter. He questioned whether supervisors could still introduce research through relatively manageable unsolved problems if models could solve them, while allowing that students might collaborate with AI and other fields might differ. In September he disclosed free Pro access and early access to OpenAI models, saying the company had never paid him. He also belongs to the independent, unpaid Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, which advises AI companies on presenting and releasing results.

In the parallel September debate, Amit Sahai argued on Terence Tao’s blog for funding many more mathematicians. Research groups could spend months understanding AI-generated work, giving society independent expertise to assess consequential proposals such as an unfamiliar fusion-plant design. Grant Sanderson, writing on the same blog, proposed comparable academic credit for explanations that show how mathematical ideas arise and why they are useful. These are proposals for supporting the work Gowers envisages. The status difficulty predates this year’s advances: in a 2022 essay, Akshay Venkatesh suggested that because mathematical prestige partly reflects perceived difficulty, tasks most accelerated by automation would lose the most status.

Manthorpe’s interview predates OpenAI’s October 6 release of 722 mathematical manuscripts. On October 8, Gowers advised students to pursue interesting projects despite uncertainty over how mathematicians would be assessed in three years. He still favoured doing a PhD, provided assessors would accept broader kinds of work. Assessment also concerned Daniel Litt in his September proposals for using defenses and sustained discussion to establish mathematical understanding. Gowers has said his advisory-group position means he will withhold comments on individual OpenAI results until after October 20.
