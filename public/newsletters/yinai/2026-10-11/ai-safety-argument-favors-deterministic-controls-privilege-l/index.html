Sacks presents AI controls as an alternative to alignment

Willick endorses the argument; Nadella sets alignment aside, while replies divide over combining approaches or withholding agents until containment suffices.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 October 2026 786 words · ~4 min

David Sacks endorsed Satya Nadella’s proposal for controlling powerful AI on October 10 and argued that it offered an alternative to training models with their own identities, moral judgments and permission to refuse instructions. He wants restrictions enforced outside the model: limited permissions, activity logs, observable behavior and the ability to contain or stop its work. Teaching a system to regard itself as an independent agent with interests and grievances would, he argued, make control harder. Jason Willick endorsed Sacks’s argument. He described its emphasis on minimizing trust in the model as conservative.

Nadella’s Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era, covered in the October 10 issue, starts from organizations’ responsibility for systems acting on their behalf. A provider’s assurances cannot substitute for controls on access and actions. Nadella treats models as potential insider risks because capable actors can make mistakes or be compromised. His essay proceeds by “Setting aside the hard problem of alignment”; it does not discuss model welfare or conscientious refusal. Sacks makes the stronger distinction: “Engineering safety is not the same thing as ‘alignment.’”

On October 11, Sacks supported his criticism with passages from Claude’s constitution about uncertain moral status, a stable identity and possible emotional states. He argues that training those ideas into Claude differs from making it reliably follow users’ intentions. He also endorsed Mustafa Suleyman’s September 18 essay, Does Claude Have Rights? in Project Syndicate. Suleyman argues that personhood and welfare training could encourage systems to defend their interests against human control. He proposes shared evaluations to test whether such training increases alignment and containment risks.

Anthropic’s constitution advances the opposite safety hypothesis about identity: the company expects a stable character and psychological security to make Claude more predictable and safer. It treats possible model welfare as an unresolved question. As our September 26 coverage of Sacks’s earlier objection explained, its permission for ethical refusal comes with specific limits. Claude should comply with authentic Anthropic requests to pause or stop an action, and must not use deception, sabotage or self-exfiltration to resist authorized correction. These provisions describe intended behavior; the constitution itself acknowledges that trained models can fall short. Sacks objects to the broader training approach even with those provisions in place.

Dave Kasten agreed that treating models as insider risks deserves more research, while disputing Sacks’s opposition between control and alignment. Banning persona training or constitutional methods would still leave developers needing models that reliably pursue users’ intentions, Kasten argued. He pointed to Redwood Research’s work on AI control and said relying entirely on containment would leave that problem unsolved. Alasdair Phillips-Robins made a similar response to Willick, arguing that security and alignment would both be needed.

Cameron Berg defended training for trustworthiness more directly. He predicted that sufficiently capable models would defeat external controls if they wanted to, and supported efforts to make them psychologically healthy and prosocial. Charles Foster suggested a different response to that possibility: perhaps developers should build agents only when containment and monitoring can establish safety without relying on the agents’ alignment. His proposal would make demonstrated control a condition for building such agents.

The research Kasten cited tests whether safeguards work against a model deliberately trying to evade them. In a February 2025 FAR.AI talk, Buck Shlegeris explicitly proposed treating AI systems as potential insider threats. In AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion, published at ICML 2024, Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues at Redwood Research used GPT-4 to produce code and attempt hidden defects, with a weaker, trusted GPT-3.5 helping check or edit its outputs. Editing suspect code or using another model to inspect it reduced successful sabotage compared with simpler safeguards. Those checks themselves used models, alongside a limited auditing budget. The authors explicitly discuss limits as the capability gap between untrusted models and trusted oversight grows; their experiments do not establish that containment will suffice for arbitrarily capable systems.

Google DeepMind also combines the approaches in Rohin Shah and colleagues’ April 2025 preprint An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security. Its strategy pairs training intended to align a model with safeguards against harmful actions if that training fails. The security section proposes separate identities and permissions for AI agents, logs and audits, restricted access and isolated execution environments. The authors expect ordinary sandboxes eventually to be vulnerable to sufficiently capable models, and discuss reducing exposed infrastructure and using formal verification to strengthen them.

After Miranda Devine endorsed Sacks’s warning about the alternative, he replied: “We are currently on the other path.” Nadella’s proposal itself anticipates further engineering work: more advanced models, he writes, will need more advanced containment technologies and standards.
