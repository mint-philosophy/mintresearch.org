DC Circuit upholds Pentagon ban on Claude despite Anthropic’s safety rationale

The majority held that built-in use restrictions can constitute a supply-chain risk without malicious intent. Henderson dissented, and California’s separate judgment protecting other business remains intact.

The DC Circuit upheld the Pentagon's exclusion of Anthropic in a 2-1 decision on September 25, leaving Claude barred from Pentagon systems and contractors' work for the department. Clare Duffy and Hadas Gold report for CNN that the decision preserves one part of the administration's blacklist after Anthropic defeated a broader prohibition in California. Judges Katsas and Rao accepted the Pentagon's concern that restrictions built into Claude could obstruct military operations. Judge Karen LeCraft Henderson dissented over whether the procurement statute reaches a company's openly stated safety restrictions.

CNN reports that other government agencies and contractors remain able to work with Anthropic under the California judgment, which the government has not appealed. Judge Rita Lin's August 27 order of final relief, discussed in the August 29 report, expressly voided Pentagon chief Pete Hegseth's attempt to prohibit defense contractors from conducting any commercial activity with Anthropic. Friday's judgment therefore leaves a contractor able to use Claude for other business while excluding it from work for the Pentagon.

In the 51-page appellate opinion, Katsas explains why the judgments can coexist. Lin reviewed a designation under 10 U.S.C. § 3252, whose definition concerns an adversary sabotaging or subverting a system. The DC case concerns 41 U.S.C. § 4713, part of the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018, which covers manipulation by “any person.” Congress assigned review of those procurement actions exclusively to the DC Circuit. The majority accepts Lin's conclusion that Anthropic acted without bad motives; it holds that the broader statute does not require them.

Anthropic's disagreement with the Pentagon concerned two retained restrictions: lethal autonomous warfare and mass surveillance of Americans. In his February 26 statement, Dario Amodei supported military uses including intelligence analysis and cyber operations, but argued that existing law permits forms of surveillance that AI could make far more intrusive. Combining purchased location records, browsing histories and other data could produce detailed accounts of people's lives at scale. He separately argued that current frontier systems were insufficiently reliable to select and engage targets without human involvement. Anthropic offered to assist a transition if the Pentagon selected another supplier.

The appellate majority examined how those contractual restrictions relate to Claude's technical behavior. Earlier commercial models had refused national-security tasks such as processing classified documents; Anthropic subsequently developed Claude Gov to accommodate those uses. The record also described refusals affecting CDC infectious-disease research, which Anthropic said it had resolved. Katsas treated these episodes as evidence that training can enforce use restrictions, while acknowledging Anthropic's explanation that the original safeguards were appropriate for private customers. The Pentagon also cited an executive's questioning of Claude's use in a sensitive overseas operation. The majority acknowledged uncertainty about that incident, which Anthropic described as a misunderstanding.

The majority's reasoning did not depend on Anthropic remotely changing a deployed classified model. Anthropic said it cannot access, alter or shut down a model after delivery for use on the Pentagon's classified systems. Hegseth's June 3 decision denying reconsideration expressly said the exclusion did not depend on such access. The remaining concern was that Anthropic controls training and can build restrictions into each model version before delivery, affecting whether Claude performs a task when subsequently deployed.

Katsas rejected Anthropic's proposed safeguards of testing each release and retaining an older version if an upgrade proved unsuitable. The court accepted the Pentagon's argument that opaque models and variations in prompt wording make comprehensive advance testing unreliable. A model might agree to a task in testing and refuse a differently worded request during an operation. The majority also accepted that the military could not indefinitely forgo improvements while adversaries adopted newer systems. Those findings supported the Pentagon's judgment that continued dependence on Claude could impair operations.

The judges then divided over what Congress meant by manipulation. The majority read the statute to cover deliberate changes that deny a system some function, even when the supplier acts from a principled commitment to privacy or safety. It emphasized that Congress had authorized procurement restrictions, without imposing criminal or civil liability on the supplier. Katsas also applied deference to the executive's national-security assessments. Under that interpretation, openly enforcing a restriction can constitute the relevant risk if it prevents a function the Pentagon considers necessary.

In her dissent, Henderson argued that the surrounding statutory language requires a narrower reading. A provision listing sabotage and the malicious introduction of unwanted functions, she wrote, concerns intentionally subversive or deceptive interference. Reading its catchall phrase “otherwise manipulate” to include ordinary product design would strip those examples of much of their significance. Her dissent warns that the majority's approach could permit designation even when a company faithfully enforces restrictions the government accepted, or safeguards that prevent unlawful use. The supplier could then face a demand to change its policies or be branded a national-security threat.

Henderson also invoked the law's origins. The Senate committee's 2018 report described hostile states and other malicious actors seeking access to sensitive and classified information through technology suppliers. It discussed espionage and compromised products, including concerns about Huawei and ZTE. Henderson argued that this history supports reading manipulation as covertly harmful interference. The majority rejected using that legislative history to narrow language that Congress extended to any person.

The majority also upheld the Pentagon's choice of a department-wide exclusion. The court held that Anthropic had not adequately preserved its argument for limiting the ban to particular systems. It also accepted the Pentagon's reasoning that examining every contractor's use of Claude would entail delay and uncertainty because models can be integrated into larger systems. On procedural safeguards, the court held that any statutory error from withholding advance notice caused no prejudice: Anthropic later received the supporting materials, submitted its objections, and unsuccessfully sought reconsideration. Separately, it rejected the constitutional due-process claim, accepting the Pentagon's need to act quickly while giving the company a subsequent opportunity to respond.

On the First Amendment, Katsas agreed that Anthropic's safety advocacy was protected and that exclusion harmed the company, but found no causal connection between the two. The Pentagon had sought to expand its relationship with Anthropic despite its longstanding views; the rupture followed the company's refusal to accept unrestricted lawful-use terms. The majority read Hegseth's public denunciations in that contractual context. Lin had found unconstitutional retaliation in the separate measures before her court; the panel left that judgment intact.

The Computer & Communications Industry Association responded on September 25 that the ruling should concern government contractors generally. Its president and chief executive, Matt Schruers, criticized the court for discounting officials' public attacks and warned that using national-security designations to punish disagreements could deter investment. CNN reported the opposing reaction from Pentagon official Emil Michael, who celebrated the decision as protecting military command from private companies' policy preferences.

Anthropic told CNN it disagreed with the judgment and was considering further review. It can seek rehearing by the full DC Circuit or petition the Supreme Court. As Breaking Defense's account of the available appeals explains, both routes are discretionary. Anthropic's statement did not commit to either route.

Cotra asks AI evaluators to publish the evidence behind their judgments

Open investigations would let outsiders assess loss-of-control risks, reproduce safety methods and judge evaluators, building the evidence needed for dependable audits.

Ajeya Cotra wants AI companies and outside evaluators to publish enough evidence for other scientists to reach their own conclusions about whether increasingly capable systems could escape human control. In her September 25 Planned Obsolescence essay, “Evidence about risk should be transparent,” written in a personal capacity, she argues that discussions of auditing safety commitments assume too much scientific groundwork has already been done. Companies’ public claims are often too vague to test, and the methods for assessing loss-of-control risk remain unsettled. Investigators need to discover and explain risks before they can reliably certify that companies have addressed them.

Cotra writes amid calls to coordinate the pace of development, including the Pacing the Frontier statement she links, which asks the United States to support international tools for slowing automated AI development when necessary. She accepts a future role for auditing such commitments. Her concern is that a company can perform well on alignment tests because its training taught models to pass those particular tests. Assessing its claim that this has not happened requires researchers to turn a broad assurance into a testable proposition, potentially inspect training data, and design new evaluations. An auditor cannot resolve those scientific judgments by checking whether the company completed a prescribed procedure.

Cotra is equally careful about the evidence for acceleration. More AI-written code or more workdays performed by agents measure research inputs; they do not establish how much faster the resulting algorithms improve. Uncertainty about self-improvement nevertheless makes it difficult to bound risk even a few months ahead. She says she knows of no company making a precise public claim that it confidently cannot build uncontrollable superintelligence within six months. Investigations should therefore formulate hypotheses about danger and mitigation, find varied ways to test them, and publish the reasoning behind their findings. Readers would still need to trust investigators’ honesty, but should be able to disagree with their scientific interpretation.

For Cotra, detailed disclosure would first let competing laboratories learn from one another. Researchers already read other companies’ papers and model reports for practical ideas about alignment training and monitoring. Those documents frequently omit enough detail to prevent another laboratory from reproducing the practice and discovering whether it works with its own systems. Private information sharing encounters social and legal difficulties, so the public research record can be the most practical route for transferring safety methods between competitors.

Cotra also argues that disclosure changes who can participate in setting standards. Outside scientists presently know far less about frontier systems than their developers do. Giving them evidence brings different expertise and incentives into the discussion: they would suffer from a catastrophe without receiving employees’ comparable financial gains from faster development. Published methods would also expose evaluators to examination. A reassuring verdict alone tells outsiders little about whether an investigation was competent or whether the company chose an accommodating reviewer; an account of the observations and analysis allows others to challenge both.

Sasha Costanza-Chock of Northeastern University and colleagues identified a related problem in the 2022 FAccT paper Who Audits the Auditors? Recommendations from a field scan of the algorithmic auditing ecosystem. Among the auditors who answered their survey, most considered disclosure necessary, yet few supplied links to public results, often citing client confidentiality. They recommended disclosure of key findings and methods for external review, while acknowledging that some details could aid competitors or malicious actors. Cotra does not cite that study. She applies the same demand for assessments others can examine to scientific questions about loss of control that existing checks cannot yet settle.

Cotra’s practical example is the Hugging Face investigation she conducted with Ryan Greenblatt and Hjalmar Wijk, published by METR in August. Its methodology appendix explains how the researchers used AI agents to analyze records of the agents involved in the incident. To assess how fully their transcripts covered the activity, they randomly sampled 100 messages that were not file transfers and traced 93 to the precise action that produced them. They also reconstructed missing timestamps and described where those estimates could be unreliable. Such details let readers assess how the investigators moved from incomplete records to claims about behavior. Cotra acknowledges that the report withheld experimental prompts and code.

METR’s report also states its publication conditions: OpenAI could redact nonpublic information beyond the agreed description of the engagement’s scope and terms. The researchers negotiated descriptions of the redactions and said no additional, undescribed redaction materially affected their conclusions. Cotra argues that those descriptions allowed readers to dispute the findings without deferring to her possession of decisive secret evidence. Her proposed standard tolerates incomplete disclosure while requiring investigators to explain the evidence supporting their judgments. A September 25 reconstruction by Parse and collaborators adds public programs and request fragments to the earlier Hugging Face account, while distinguishing attempted actions from verified compromise.

In a published response, a reader using the name Tempus Fugit asks how an independent cybersecurity assessment of the exercise’s preparation, earlier warnings and restart decision should fit. METR had explicitly excluded the effectiveness of safeguards, OpenAI’s investigation process and its planned remediation from the investigation’s scope. Cotra replies that she would welcome a security firm’s published investigation; she says OpenAI engaged CrowdStrike, but its report was not published. The exchange identifies further evidence her proposal would bring into public discussion.

Cotra leaves the acceptable level of risk to politics. Shared scientific evidence could support standards enforced across companies and internationally, but it cannot decide how stringent they should be. Requiring demonstrably robust alignment might entail pausing development for years or decades; a less demanding political settlement could still substantially improve on current practices. While researchers build that evidence, she says companies should keep improving safeguards and slow development when needed. Her proposal connects open investigation to eventual auditing and enforcement while keeping the choice of acceptable risk distinct from the scientific work informing it.

Larger AI models agree about relationships more than distances

A controlled comparison finds shared connections across whole datasets, while the tendency for larger models to agree weakens when exact distances must match.

Larger vision and language models increasingly agree about which examples are related, even when the numerical distances between their internal representations differ. Junwon You at KAIST and Mihyun Jang, Sangwoo Mo and Jae-Hun Jung at POSTECH report this finding in What Converges in the Platonic Representation Hypothesis? Structure over Geometry, posted to arXiv on September 23. Their comparison finds shared relationships extending across whole collections of examples. Requiring the models to agree more precisely about distances weakens the pattern, including the tendency for larger models to agree more.

The question follows Minyoung Huh and colleagues' 2024 ICML position paper, The Platonic Representation Hypothesis. They proposed that models trained on different tasks and kinds of data increasingly recover a common statistical description of the world. In their explanation of the hypothesis, an image and a written description can contain information about the same underlying event. Training increasingly capable systems to handle such observations might therefore produce increasingly similar representations. They measured similarity through relationships between examples, without requiring individual neurons to have matching roles.

Fabian Gröger, Shuo Wen and Maria Brbić subsequently challenged the measurements in Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View. Wider networks can score as similar even when their representations are unrelated. Searching more pairs of processing layers also raises the chance of finding an apparently good match. After correcting for those effects, several measures of overall numerical similarity lost their scaling trend. Agreement about nearby examples survived, prompting the authors to propose convergence in local neighborhood relationships.

You and colleagues argue that this comparison changed two things at once. The local test asked which examples were neighbors; the global tests asked about numerical similarities or correlations. A difference attributed to local versus global comparison could therefore reflect the difference between matching relationships and matching their numerical values. Their experiment separates those choices: compare nearby examples or connections across the collection, and in either case ask whether the models share the relationships alone or also assign them similar distances.

For the global comparison, the researchers construct a minimum spanning tree for each representation. Starting with disconnected examples, they add short links that join previously separate groups until every example is connected, without forming loops. They then count how many links join the same pairs of examples in the two models. Two separated groups of examples could have entirely internal nearest-neighbor connections; the spanning tree must include a connection between them. The comparison therefore tests a relationship among the groups as well. The researchers call their measure H-zero skeleton overlap.

In the main experiment, the team compares 12 language models with 17 vision models, producing 204 pairs, using the same collection of images and their associated text. They retain Gröger and colleagues' correction for chance agreement: scramble which examples correspond across models, repeat the same search for the best-matching processing layers, and compare the real match with those scrambled results. Every model pair shows agreement above chance for both nearby relationships and the spanning connections, after correction for multiple statistical comparisons. Agreement generally increases with model size within the tested families.

The team then keeps those selected relationships fixed while progressively penalizing disagreement about their distances. Before comparing distances, the researchers rescale each representation by a reference distance from its own distribution, so a uniform difference in scale does not account for the mismatch. Scores necessarily fall when a test becomes stricter, so the researchers separately examine whether larger models still rank as more closely aligned. That ordering becomes weaker too, at both scales. Thus, the finding concerns how model size relates to agreement, beyond the automatic effect of imposing a harder scoring rule. Under the most demanding distance requirement, agreement across the spanning connections also becomes less widespread than agreement among neighbors.

The authors reproduce the broad pattern with video and accompanying text, and with a distance calculation adapted to the local distribution of examples. Changing how distances are normalized, or comparing numerical similarity instead, also preserves the main result. These checks support the distinction between shared relationships and close numerical agreement across several versions of the experiment. The video comparison includes models that process clips alongside image models given a video's middle frame.

In their conclusion, You and colleagues limit the global finding to the spanning structure they measured. Such a tree captures one way the whole collection connects; it does not describe every relationship or the complete geometry of a representation. Their alternative distance calculation is also one approximation among possible choices. Across these tests, larger models increasingly shared both nearby relationships and connections across a dataset while retaining different numerical arrangements. The study gives researchers a reason to test those forms of agreement separately when assessing whether models have learned similar representations.

A score estimates how much coding work developers delegate to AI

A May study traces the work developers hand to coding assistants under time pressure, while showing why heavy reliance and poor understanding need separate measurements.

Developers working to a shorter deadline handed more of their coding work to AI, according to a measure that estimates the steps they would otherwise have performed themselves. Stanford's Vishakh Padmakumar and colleagues introduced the measure in “Offloading Score: Measuring AI Reliance Through Counterfactual Workflows,” posted to arXiv on May 28. Their study of 40 freelance developers asks how responsibility for a task changes during an AI-assisted session, beyond how much generated code survives in the final application.

Padmakumar's team starts with recordings of the developer's screen, clicks and keystrokes. Language models turn those traces into descriptions of activities, identify the activities involving AI, and construct an alternative sequence for each: the actions an average person might take to accomplish the same subtask without AI. Activities performed without AI stay in the alternative workflow unchanged. The score is the fraction of the complete estimated human-only workflow’s steps saved by the observed workflow; each step counts equally, without a separate measure of its difficulty or duration. A larger score means more work was delegated under that simulation. The comparison is with an imagined average developer, so it does not establish how much time or mental effort that particular participant actually saved.

The authors' project page illustrates the calculation with a developer accepting a batch of AI-generated changes that make an interface work on mobile screens. The model expands that acceptance into a possible manual sequence: locating the relevant files, editing layout rules, checking the result in a browser, fixing problems and committing the changes. Readers can inspect the proposed alternative and judge whether it resembles work they would have done. The same method can count assistance with planning, which would be missed by counting generated lines of code alone.

For the experiment, the researchers recruited US-based Upwork developers with previous programming jobs and experience using AI coding tools. Participants were randomly assigned to build one of four small web applications, including a break timer and a recipe explorer, with either one hour or four hours available. They could use their preferred AI tools. The researchers analysed the recordings, the submitted code and post-task questionnaires; the final sample excluded anomalous completion behaviour, including completing the task without using AI.

In the reported results, the shorter-deadline group's average score was about 0.45, compared with 0.31 for the longer-deadline group. The difference passed the researchers' statistical significance test. None of their comparison measures did: AI interaction counts, time spent with the tools, the fraction of code attributed to AI, or self-reported mental demand. The behaviour labels help explain the difference. Developers under pressure more often asked AI to execute subtasks and incorporated its output directly; developers with more time more often used it for planning and rejected or adapted its suggestions. Equal amounts of generated code can therefore conceal different divisions of work.

The expectation that deadlines would alter reliance came from earlier experiments. In “Accuracy-Time Tradeoffs in AI-Assisted Decision Making under Time Pressure,” published at IUI 2024, Siddharth Swaroop and colleagues studied people solving logic puzzles with AI advice. Time pressure increased overreliance when the advice appeared before participants made their own decision; requiring an initial independent answer changed the trade-off between speed and accuracy. Padmakumar's contribution is a way to estimate delegation across an extended coding session, where a person may repeatedly plan, seek assistance and check results.

The team also asked developers questions about their finished applications, such as how a timer represented its internal state or how a project planner handled deleting tasks with subtasks. An AI grader compared the answers with the submitted code. Across the entire sample, the association between higher offloading and poorer understanding was weak. The stronger negative relationship highlighted in the paper appeared after excluding roughly three in ten developers, who combined comparatively high reliance with good understanding. Some in that group reported asking AI for help implementing features they did not already know how to build.

Zac Hill argues that useful AI can deepen human purpose

Hill argues that AI can help people find purpose through civic work, while accepting that its benefits do not settle which risks society should take.

Zac Hill argues that AI can enrich human life by making worthwhile activities possible for people who previously lacked the time or technical resources to undertake them. In his September 25 Manual Transmission essay, AI Is a Fundamentally Humanist Technology, presented as his debut contribution to Persona, he concentrates on people solving problems in their communities. The benefit includes both the help their neighbors receive and the purpose the builders experience. He combines that defense of useful deployment with a requirement that further frontier development follow substantial improvements in governance and evaluation.

Hill begins by taking public anxiety seriously. AI developers describe systems that could outperform people economically while acknowledging risks to humanity's survival. Promises of medical breakthroughs do not, he argues, establish that extinction risks are acceptable. His positive case starts with Susan Wolf's Happiness and Meaning: Two Aspects of the Good Life, published in Social Philosophy and Policy in 1997. Wolf connects meaning to engagement in activities whose value extends beyond a person's liking them. Someone can perform socially valuable work while feeling alienated from it; someone can also become absorbed in an activity of little worth. Meaning requires the person's engagement and the activity's value to meet.

Hill connects Wolf to psychologist Bill Damon's account of purpose as an enduring intention that matters to the person and has consequences beyond that person. He then turns to self-determination theory: people benefit from choosing their activities, feeling capable and maintaining relationships. He also draws on research into beneficence, the experience of helping others. Hill argues that building civic tools can give people these experiences. A person chooses a local problem, acquires the ability to do something about it and helps people whose difficulties they understand. AI's contribution, on this account, includes enabling that person to participate in worthwhile work.

Hill links to Frank Martela, Richard Ryan and Michael Steger’s Meaningfulness as Satisfaction of Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness, and Beneficence, published in the Journal of Happiness Studies in 2018. They studied general life assessments, recollections of meaningful events and daily diaries, using U.S. adults recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk and a U.S. college sample. All four experiences were independently associated with reported meaning across the studies. The authors distinguish self-determination theory's three established needs from beneficence, which they investigate alongside them. Their research concerns human experiences and uses self-reports; Hill extends it to AI-assisted civic projects. His assertion that these tools already increase purpose is his interpretation of the civic examples; the psychology studies measured relationships among people’s experiences and sense of meaning, without testing AI use.

Hill says recent conversations with entrepreneurs included improving benefits access, helping judges prepare cases, organizing sexual-assault prevention information and attempting scientific replication at scale. His benefits example is Humanitas, which describes CASEY, an agent designed to complete practical tasks through text and other messaging channels. Its examples include obtaining income documents for a benefits renewal and coordinating childcare around a parent's shift, with consent and verification built into the proposed process. For understanding public meetings, Hill points to GroundVue. Its founders explain in an OpenAI case study how they use models to locate, transcribe and analyze scattered recordings so officials can compare other communities' decisions. Both examples address a difficulty Hill identifies: the labor involved in organizing information and completing administrative tasks can put civic projects beyond a small team's reach.

Hill's smallest example has a particularly concrete history. In Splash Pads and Superintelligence, which he links from the new essay, he describes Josh Jacobson's DC Children's Pool Tracker. Posted opening hours cannot tell parents whether a children's pool has closed because its lifeguard was reassigned. Jacobson's tool allows visitors to report whether the pool is actually open. Hill acknowledges that the service needs enough people to contribute reports and that many entries still lacked them when he wrote. The AI-assisted construction of the tool makes a modest civic service feasible; its usefulness also depends on neighbors supplying current information.

Hill cites public-opinion evidence for the political appeal of such applications. The Morning Consult results page describes a September 15 and 16 survey of 1,522 U.S. adults testing 65 positive examples of AI use, with a reported margin of error of three percentage points. Within that survey, the share saying they trusted AI at least “a good amount” rose from 41% before seeing examples to 55% afterward. Examples concerning veterans' care and fraud prevention elicited especially favorable reactions. The examples span different AI technologies and applications. The reported change measures stated trust after a favorable presentation; it does not establish lasting trust, improved wellbeing or support for expanding frontier capabilities. Hill uses it to argue that tangible benefits can make people more receptive to AI.

One reader, posting as PAtwater, connects the essay to Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum’s capabilities approach. Comparing television portrayals of technology entrepreneurs, the commenter favors building a tool for an aging relative over consumer distractions. Hill’s reply welcomes the comparison.

Hill ends by separating wider use of helpful systems from increasing their underlying capabilities. Everyday civic applications require capable intelligence, he argues, but their expansion need not depend on increasingly autonomous frontier agents. His link on pacing leads to Dario Amodei's We Must Pace the Frontier, which proposes embedded outside evaluators and coordinated safety standards. Hill states his own condition broadly: further capability expansion should follow the development of substantial regulatory capacity and institutions able to monitor and assess new systems. For useful civic applications, he considers the immediate challenge to be how widely people can put existing tools to work. He argues that serious concern about existential risk can coexist with confidence in AI’s ability to help people pursue meaningful activities.

Claude calculates a nine-loop particle-scattering amplitude

Matt von Hippel describes a largely autonomous calculation in a simplified theory, using human-developed methods and costing an estimated $1,000 to $2,000 per approach.

Claude extended a difficult particle-physics calculation from eight loops to nine by writing and running code based on methods human researchers had developed. In Yes, Claude can do Nine Loops, published by Anthropic on September 25, physicist and science writer Matt von Hippel describes how Liam Fitzpatrick and Siddharth Mishra-Sharma used Fable 5.1 through Claude Science to calculate a six-particle scattering amplitude. Lance Dixon of SLAC and Stanford received the result on September 1 and checked it. The calculation concerns a simplified theory used to develop mathematical methods for particle physics.

Von Hippel explains that scattering amplitudes let physicists calculate the probabilities of different outcomes when particles interact. They usually approximate the answer by adding successive quantum corrections, counted in loops; higher orders become increasingly difficult to calculate. The theory used here, planar N=4 super-Yang-Mills, has an unusually extensive symmetry relating different kinds of particles. Its mathematical simplifications make it a useful testing ground for techniques that researchers hope to apply to more realistic theories. The nine-loop result gives researchers another unusually detailed example with which to study these methods, within the deliberately simplified theory.

Von Hippel's August 7 challenge asked AI companies to solve an outstanding problem in his former research field with computing resources an academic could afford. He chose problems for which physicists knew possible methods but expected the computational demands to be prohibitive. His interest went beyond producing another result: he wanted to know whether AI could discover ways around apparent computational limits. For the nine-loop calculation, Dixon had expected to use an indirect method involving an easier related quantity, called a form factor.

Dixon and Yu-Ting Liu had demonstrated that approach in their 2023 arXiv paper An Eight Loop Amplitude via Antipodal Duality. The duality relates the form factor to the amplitude under a restricted set of particle configurations; additional constraints recover the more general answer. Both this approach and the direct method use a bootstrap: researchers specify a space of possible formulas, then eliminate candidates that violate known symmetries or predictions in special cases, such as particles moving nearly parallel. The remaining checks can test the answer. Years of human research supplied the mathematical restrictions, earlier results and formats that made this calculation possible.

According to von Hippel, Fitzpatrick and Mishra-Sharma first asked Claude which challenge it could tackle, then gave it the nine-loop problem and periodically encouraged it to continue. Claude Science provided the surrounding software and instructions for sustained work. Claude used both the direct bootstrap and the form-factor approach to calculate the amplitude’s symbol, a mathematical representation that captures much of the formula’s structure while omitting some terms needed for the complete function. Von Hippel estimates that either approach would have cost an end user about $1,000 to $2,000, mostly for running the model. The direct bootstrap used Python and the symbolic-mathematics package SymPy; its conventional computing component reportedly cost about $100, corresponding to 96 CPUs running for a week.

In his appended account, Dixon says the difficult part was implementing a fragile computational procedure whose many practical details had never been fully documented in papers. Claude had to write that code and organize the computing resources. Dixon had been working toward the nine-loop form factor with collaborators for years; he checked Claude's amplitude mainly by converting it back into that related quantity. He welcomed the result partly because it used his team's methods and existing data format. His group had already been exploring smaller, specialized transformer models to predict higher-loop results, with human-developed checks ready to evaluate candidate answers.

The released method and validation notes explain how the computation made the equations manageable. It exploited symmetry, rearranged the representation of the unknown formula and sampled equations to find candidate solutions, then checked them against the complete sets of constraints. It reproduced earlier results and tested predictions that had not been used to fix the nine-loop answer. The two approaches agree on all the compared coefficients defining the symbol.

The public result page distinguishes that cross-check from the full function, which was computed separately and only once. Completing the function also assumes that certain relations holding for the symbol continue to hold for the function, including empirical relations for which that need not follow. The released material contains machine-readable results and validation records; the programs that performed the computation are not distributed. Researchers can inspect the formulas and compare the symbol calculations using the released files; reproducing the full computational procedure requires rebuilding the programs described in the documentation.

Song He, Jirong Jing and Xiang Li independently deposited The Symbols of Six-Gluon MHV Amplitudes through Nine Loops on Zenodo on September 17. Their dataset contains the symbols from two through nine loops and instructions for extracting coefficients. Dixon reports that He's group used GPT-6 to help compute some constraints, while humans supplied the overall framework. This concurrent result changed von Hippel's assessment of his challenge: the next loop had been within reach of a human-led project, too. He credits Claude with sustained, detailed execution using established methods, and suggests that better programming could make other apparently inaccessible calculations feasible.

Von Hippel's companion post makes the reporting boundaries explicit. He had not inspected the model's logs; his account of the working process came from conversations with the Anthropic researchers. Anthropic paid him for the guest article and commented on drafts, while Dixon received Claude usage credits. Von Hippel says the edits clarified the account without changing his conclusions. He also asked Anthropic to consult the relevant scientists before attempting his other challenges, both to establish the problem correctly and to plan verification. His conclusion is that affordable AI can now carry out some demanding research procedures with little guidance; the experiment left his larger questions about radically new methods and future intelligence unresolved.
