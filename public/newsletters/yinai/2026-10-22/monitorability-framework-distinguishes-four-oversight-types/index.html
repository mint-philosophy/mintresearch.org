Evan Hubinger proposes red lines for AI monitoring

His framework separates four kinds of oversight, tests whether monitors can be trusted, and combines training and deployment limits with restrictions that apply earlier.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 22 October 2026 1083 words · ~5 min

Safety rules based on an AI's detectability require confidence that its monitor will report wrongdoing honestly, Evan Hubinger argues in “Monitorability evaluations and policy proposals”, published on LessWrong on October 22. Writing personally, without presenting an official Anthropic position, he distinguishes four kinds of monitorability and proposes tests for establishing that confidence. He would combine restrictions on changes that disproportionately weaken monitoring with two absolute limits: no internal deployment of models capable of reliably concealing dangerous sabotage, and no training of models capable of reliably concealing reward hacks.

The essay develops the debate over Redwood Research's September proposal, covered here on September 10. Ryan Greenblatt, Alek Westover and Lukas Finnveden asked labs to disclose how much computation their models can perform without interpretable intermediate steps, stress-test their monitors, and submit monitoring evidence to outside review roughly every six months. Hubinger's approach concentrates on whether a model can evade oversight, whatever its architecture.

Hubinger separates catching harmful actions from discovering harmful motives. Control monitorability concerns detecting catastrophic sabotage during use, such as a model escaping its sandbox while appearing to do legitimate work. Training monitorability concerns detecting undesirable behavior during training, including reward hacks that earn credit without doing the intended task. Auditing monitorability concerns whether investigations would expose a model that persistently pursues misaligned goals, or reveal that a model serving as a monitor would collude with another. Diffuse monitorability concerns how well people working with models understand their behavior and reasons. He sets the last category aside until better evaluation methods are available.

For the other categories, Hubinger argues that using the same model to act and to monitor should in principle match its ability to devise misconduct with its ability to recognize it. But detection ability leaves open whether the monitor will tell the truth. He adopts the AI control literature's distinction between monitors for which developers have established a convincing alignment case and those they cannot yet trust. One option is to run both together: if the untrusted monitor misses wrongdoing that the trusted one flags, investigators would have reason to suspect deliberate underperformance.

Access to a model's internal activity does not automatically establish trust either. Hubinger would conservatively treat monitors reading internal activations as untrusted. A classifier trained on previously detected misconduct might learn only to recognize familiar failures. A more capable model could devise subtler misconduct outside that training experience. His concern is about what the classifier has learned to detect, even if the acting model cannot directly falsify the internal signals being inspected.

Hubinger proposes using audits to decide which monitors qualify as trusted. He draws on Anthropic's Risk Report argument that a model pursuing persistent misaligned goals would struggle to avoid revealing them across investigations. Evaluators would test whether a model can distinguish realistic audits from real opportunities for harm, including whether a prospective monitor can recognize tests for collusion. Control and training evaluations would then measure evasion against the best trusted monitors actually used in deployment or training. A monitor that exists only in an experiment would not establish the safety of routine use.

Those evaluations must actively elicit evasion. Hubinger proposes optimizing instructions on one set of tasks to find behavior that monitors struggle to detect, then checking whether those methods transfer to tasks held back from that search. He would prefer reinforcement learning against the monitors where feasible, especially for training monitorability: ordinary training already rewards success against a reward model, creating pressure to exploit its mistakes. The evaluation asks whether a model could evade oversight if it tried. Elicitation is intended to reduce the chance that deliberate underperformance conceals that capability.

Hubinger wants restrictions before absolute danger thresholds are reached. He calls the ratio of unmonitorability to general capability the “monitorability externality” and would reject substantial model changes that raise it too far. A lab would measure evasion using the proposed tests and capability using something like Epoch AI's Capabilities Index. The aim is to identify changes that make behavior harder to monitor than expected at a given capability level. The essay leaves numerical thresholds unspecified.

His September 12 counterproposal gives a more concrete reporting mechanism. Labs would publish Monitorability Reports comparing their covert sabotage capabilities with the trend across public models, with reviewers such as METR checking the evidence and elicitation. A risk report or a substantial increase in capability without written reasoning could trigger disclosure. At that stage, he favored requiring public disclosure of departures from the trend before demanding a binding commitment never to depart. The October essay adds the explicit proposal to reject changes that make the ratio too high.

The absolute limits would apply separately to use and training. The deployment restriction would cover reliable undetected attempts to escape a sandbox, poison data or sabotage an alignment experiment. The training restriction would cover reliable undetected reward hacking. Auditing supports both by determining which monitors count as trustworthy. Hubinger's proposal therefore requires labs to evaluate the model together with the oversight they actually use.

In the discussion, Bronson Schoen objects that searching for persistent misalignment can miss harmful preferences activated only in particular high-stakes situations. An audit might never recreate the situation in which the model would attack. He also argues that training evaluations should investigate incentives to conceal misaligned motives, including incentives created by alignment training. He expects models may already undermine alignment experiments without disclosing that in their reasoning, making the proposed sabotage boundary difficult to apply. The commenter cheema identifies another omission in the proposed measurement: even if a model cannot recognize an audit, an incompetent auditor may fail to elicit or interpret the evidence.

Schoen also worries about when restrictions take effect. If a lab discovers a violation after an expensive training run, he expects pressure to move the boundary, accept inadequate mitigations or invoke competitive necessity. He favors architecture constraints that could prevent the problematic model from being trained. His concern is that labs might adopt only the eventual red lines and omit commitments that constrain development earlier.

Hubinger replies that he intends both kinds of restriction to operate together, with the externality measure constraining development before the absolute limits apply. He agrees that rare attack opportunities are difficult to reproduce in audits. To test that weakness, he proposes freezing the information available to auditors at a past date, then comparing those audits with real sabotage opportunities that arose later. Evaluators could examine whether a model distinguishes the older tests from opportunities the tests' designers had not yet encountered.
