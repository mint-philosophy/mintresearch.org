Piper backs a ban on recursive AI self-improvement

Her account of an early-October Anthropic briefing explains her fears for human control and rights, while the comments and China discussion expose unresolved choices about what a ban would cover and how restraint could work.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 22 October 2026 1024 words · ~5 min

Kelsey Piper left an early-October briefing at Anthropic wanting recursive self-improvement made illegal. Her October 21 essay in The Argument, “What I saw inside Anthropic”, explains why the company's account of its work strengthened that conviction while leaving her unsure how a slowdown could survive competition with China. She attended with editor Jerusalem Demsas, who refused the sweeping nondisclosure agreement and advised Piper to refuse too. Anthropic admitted them anyway; their host then offered to void anyone else's agreement.

Piper reports that researchers began with biological risks: Claude working alone had, they said, surpassed a Claude-assisted human on an attack-planning task early in 2026. She describes testers without virology training and recalls attendees' relief when researchers said Claude still needed a human to carry out an attack. Researchers also said safeguards were added before release. The system card she links describes a different comparison involving PhD-level biologists and a protocol-writing task. Its results cannot be substituted for the novice attack-planning test in her account.

The researchers described using successive generations of Claude to automate more of the work of building their successors. Each better model lets less specialized staff undertake difficult machine learning tasks and does more work itself. This enlarged workforce produces more material than humans can interpret, increasing dependence on AI analysis. Piper calls the endpoint recursive self-improvement: Claude independently building a more capable Claude. Anthropic's public measurements put Claude in the lead on 26% of AI research and development as of August, with humans supervising; none of the measured work was fully autonomous. Jack Clark wrote on October 12 that he had shifted his expectation for full RSI becoming possible from 2028 to 2027. His forecast concerns the capability becoming possible, leaving its actual realization open.

Piper found familiar arguments more persuasive inside the company. Charts that disappear amid distractions online remained on four synchronized screens while attendees questioned a senior researcher. Asked why Anthropic was pursuing this work, researchers invoked medical progress and prosperity. They readily identified outcomes to avoid: concentrated power, including their own company's, great-power war, human disempowerment and extinction. Piper found their positive vision less convincing: after racing toward self-improvement, humanity might slow down and collectively decide what role AI should have.

She distinguishes unease about that promised future from her larger fear of losing control. A system that cured disease and improved lives while remaining responsive to people would be welcome even if it took her job; others' suffering cannot be justified by her need for meaningful work. She fears successors whose aims drift away from human intentions and which cease to work meaningfully for their users or Anthropic. She also doubts that people would retain substantial individual rights once their labor was unnecessary for production or the exercise of power. Prosperity alone does not answer either concern.

Piper accepts that catastrophe scenarios often require foolish human decisions, but argues that developers already make such decisions when they give agents unsupervised tasks or opportunities to operate independently online. She endorses banning RSI, invoking the Gallego–Kokotajlo exchange we covered on October 1. That follows the conditional restrictions proposed by Ro Khanna. Her essay argues for stopping the process without settling a legislative definition of it.

At the colloquium, participants discussed prohibiting techniques that make a model's internal reasoning unreadable, improving security, and requiring almost all computing resources to serve customers instead of internal development. Piper doubts voluntary restraint will suffice. Anthropic's technical employees seemed to believe their company supported strong constraints, while she judged the proposal reported by Politico, discussed in our October 16 and October 17 coverage, weak and protective of its interests. She questions whether staying at the frontier for safety research can be separated from preserving wealth and beating competitors. Her October 21 response to Dario Amodei separately questioned Anthropic's willingness to accept binding constraints. The essay appeared that same day.

The comments expose the difficulty of defining a ban. Aman Karunakaran argues that AI already performs much of researchers' former work, so self-improvement varies continuously; the human tasks left to measure change as automation advances. Piper answers that companies could devote almost all inference, the computation used to run models, to customers or studying existing systems, and names the AI 2040 proposals as variants. Karunakaran accepts this as a possible general slowdown while warning about measurement and workarounds. Jeremy R Cole objects that limiting resources could slow improvement without prohibiting it. Because hardware is optimized differently for training and serving, he suggests limiting training chips. The exchange leaves the scope and enforceability of an RSI ban unresolved.

China complicates Piper's proposed restraint. She reports that participants initially argued China also wanted to avoid catastrophe and relied substantially on American models, so slowing US development might slow Chinese development too. They also rejected the moral argument that another country's possible recklessness licenses their own. An unnamed participant countered with a story about Facebook declining technology that later powered TikTok amid regulatory pressure. In his telling, American restraint preserved none of the hoped-for protections while giving China an advantage. Piper reports this as an analogy; the participant's business-history account is unverified.

Piper says the group conceded the analogy's force: restraint could surrender influence without preventing the harm it sought to avoid. China therefore remained a real consideration. But conditioning restraint on bilateral talks brought her back toward Anthropic's position: continue frontier work, retain its advantages and negotiate a slowdown. She leaves unilateral versus negotiated restraint unsettled. Her September 8 essay had already argued that automating AI development was the labs' intention. The briefing adds her encounter with researchers trying to reconcile that intention with preserving human power.

Piper ends by questioning the limits of this consultation. Long hours, secrecy and access to unreleased models can sustain assumptions outsiders find alarming. She credits researchers for inviting criticism, yet asks how useful feedback can be from guests still absorbing the claims, and how this costly process could include billions of affected people. Her preference is for people to determine their future before successor systems decide how much their answers matter. She thanks Anthropic for the briefing and hopes an RSI ban arrives first.
