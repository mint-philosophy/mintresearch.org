A LessWrong explainer lays out how a US-China AI slowdown could be verified

Posting as josefely, a first-year student assembles the published verification literature into a phased treaty: compute declarations and random chip inspections first, then guarantee processors on every accelerator, while conceding that algorithmic progress would eventually defeat it.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 22 October 2026 1353 words · ~7 min

On October 22 a new LessWrong account posting as josefely published "How could the US and China verifiably slow AI?", a 6,899-word explainer and the account's first post. The profile describes the author as a first-year student at the University of Vermont, from Boston, and gives no real name. The essay was written, the author says, for friends who want AI slowed for reasons like labor displacement but do not follow safety research, and it disclaims novelty: "This likely isn't a highly original contribution", though almost nobody outside the field reads the papers it draws on. Most of the technical content comes from a 2024 taxonomy of verification methods by Akash Wasil of Georgetown and colleagues, a 2025 survey of hardware-enabled mechanisms led by Aidan O'Gara at Oxford, and the FlexHEG guarantee-processor proposal that James Petrie and coauthors wrote for the UK's ARIA. The essay assembles them into one argument, from game theory through chip design to a phased treaty scenario.

It opens with the case that a slowdown is now a live political demand. AP-NORC found in late September that 64% of US adults think AI is developing too quickly; 1,386 employees of frontier AI companies signed the July Pacing the Frontier statement asking Washington to back an international effort to pace frontier development; and Dario Amodei's September 12 essay "We Must Pace the Frontier" proposed, as the third of four possible levels of US-China agreement, a SALT-like "speed limit" on recursive self-improvement, which he called "difficult but just on the edge of being possible". Amodei rated a full pause unlikely soon because the confidence needed in verification would be very high. Josefely's essay takes up the verification problem he left open.

The game-theoretic section argues that a unilateral US halt cannot work. The core ideas are public, talent would move, and China's best model is close behind: by the author's count, Moonshot's Kimi K3 scores 157 on Epoch AI's capabilities index against 167 for Claude Opus 5.5, and since the index has advanced about 14 points a year since reasoning models arrived, the gap is somewhat under a year (Epoch's pages on October 23 list 158 and 169, a gap of 11). If the US stopped, the author writes, "China would surpass our capabilities in less than half of one US congressional cycle". A mutual pause without verification fails for the mirror-image reason, since a secret defector gains a large advantage, so an agreement has to detect defection before it pays off. That requirement decides what gets regulated. Following the 2024 argument by Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim and colleagues that compute is detectable, excludable and quantifiable, with a concentrated supply chain, the essay concludes that a treaty would govern compute: caps on cluster or training-run size, cryptographic chip licenses, shared authority over large runs, automated safety tests.

The author is candid about the limits. Compute governance tracks what is easy to measure, such as cluster size, and not what anyone cares about, such as whether a model is aligned or whether a cluster is training or forecasting weather. It also decays. The stock of accelerators grows about 3.4 times a year, and the compute needed for a given level of performance has been halving every 4.5 months as of 2026, so compute governance "is a wasting asset that becomes less effective over time". It buys time; it does not stop AI.

The verification section follows Wasil et al.'s three categories. Unilateral "national technical means", which include satellite imagery, energy monitoring, customs data, financial intelligence and whistleblowers, catch large-scale activity but say little about purpose, and the Manhattan Project kept 130,000 workers secret. Inspections of data centers, fabs and developers are stronger but need a hostile state's consent and risk leaking trade secrets; unlike enriched uranium, the product of AI development is information, "where understanding it is all you need to steal it". Domestic arrangements such as Anthropic's embedded evaluators from Accenture, announced September 18, do not transfer, because from Beijing's side everyone involved is American. Hardware-dependent methods, the essay argues, "have the most raw power of any tool available to treaty-makers". Chips could report compute used, classify training against inference, prove their location through timed challenge-response exchanges or GPS, and throttle themselves after metered use until they receive a fresh cryptographic license, with a one-way counter rejecting any license already used. The building blocks, taken from O'Gara et al., are monotonic counters, secure memory and clocks, tamper-evident enclosures and trusted execution environments; FlexHEG packages them into a retrofittable guarantee processor. The essay passes on O'Gara et al.'s warning that existing hardware security was built for smart cards and microcontrollers and that adapting it "may require substantial work", and it notes that no chip designed to comply with a US-China treaty exists today. The essay cites Simone Gargiulo and Gabriel Kulp's 2026 paper for the claim that workload classifiers must resist adversarial reshaping; that paper, though, identifies training, inference and non-AI work from an H200's external power draw with 97% accuracy, a different mechanism from the on-chip reporting the essay describes.

The essay closes with a scenario. After a wave of AI-enabled cyberattacks on power grids and the financial system, Washington and Beijing open talks through the incident channel agreed at the September Trump-Xi summit, where, per the Associated Press, an AI-specific dialogue is already scheduled for November and Trump said the US "is not going to be putting on brakes". The first phase would declare and locate all compute, freeze new deployments, track construction by satellite and inspect chips at random against supply records. The author's case for random inspection is that "You only need to get caught spoofing once", that the chance of detection rises with the size of a hidden stockpile, and that model capability scales sublinearly with compute, so a stockpile small enough to hide is not worth much. Existing clusters would either be shut down (the essay floats 90%, checkable by power and thermal sensing, and admits the economic pain) or restricted to running existing models, verified by cluster software, spies, inspections, or network taps that log hashes of workloads for later re-execution, an idea the essay takes from Naci Cankaya's working paper at MIRI, which argues "There is no need for mutually trusted chips". Trust and economic pain trade off: more trust allows more reliance on software and inspections, less trust means shuttering more compute. The final phase would put guarantee processors on every new accelerator and retrofit old ones, running a ruleset that changes only when multiple parties sign with separate cryptographic keys; options include per-model compute caps, location checks, deployment limited to approved models, and hashed weight snapshots of the kind Yonadav Shavit proposed in 2023. The author concedes the whole arrangement is temporary, since algorithmic progress would eventually let a covert project reach dangerous capability on untracked compute; the hope is to slow progress long enough for society and alignment research to catch up.

Recent proposals the essay does not mention pull in different directions. Felix Choussat and Adam Khoja of the Center for AI Safety argued in AI Frontiers in August that whole-lab human inspection, with US and Chinese inspectors given physical access and read-only access to communications, code and compute telemetry, could verify a slowdown "without needing a single new invention", because lab secrets leak anyway. Ruby Scanlon and Janet Egan at CNAS argued in September for layered verification and government-funded verification R&D, and put the number of engineers working on AI verification at around 50; MIRI's Aaron Scher and coauthors published a full treaty design last November. The essay also leaves Beijing's stated objections untouched. After Amodei's essay, China's foreign ministry called it fearmongering and the Global Times called it a "Cold War playbook" for pairing a slowdown with chip restrictions, and Tsinghua's Sun Chenghao told Al Jazeera that Beijing suspects "AI safety" of being a cover for restricting chip access. A treaty built from chip supply records and licenses would have to answer that. As of October 23 the post had a score of 9 from three votes and no comments.
