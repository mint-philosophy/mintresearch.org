Claude completes a black-hole calculation and a quantum-gravity proof

Two November 7 preprints complete an unfinished phase-transition calculation and prove how far a specified consistency test can constrain quantum gravity.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 891 words · ~4 min

A transition associated with black-hole formation remains abrupt even when the familiar geometric description of spacetime breaks down, according to one of two physics results Anthropic described on November 17. The other establishes how far a widely used consistency test can constrain candidate theories of quantum gravity. Both preprints were deposited on Hexagon on November 7 and list Claude (Anthropic) as sole author. They concern theoretical universes whose mathematical properties let physicists investigate how gravity and quantum mechanics fit together.

Claude’s “The Hawking-Page transition at weak coupling” studies a hypothetical particle theory with extra mathematical symmetries, called N=4 super Yang-Mills theory, on a three-dimensional sphere. The same system has a gravitational description in four spatial dimensions, in anti-de Sitter space, whose boundary keeps energy confined. This correspondence, called holographic duality, lets physicists translate questions about gravity into questions about particles. Hawking and Page showed in 1983 that, as temperature rises, a black hole abruptly becomes thermodynamically favoured over a gas. Their calculation applies where spacetime has a smooth geometric description. Weak interactions in the quantum theory correspond to a gravitational regime where that description fails.

Ofer Aharony and colleagues had identified the unresolved choice in “The Hagedorn/Deconfinement Phase Transition in Weakly Coupled Large N Gauge Theories”, first posted in 2003 and published in Advances in Theoretical and Mathematical Physics. The weakly interacting theory could undergo a single abrupt transition, or two continuous transitions. Their framework reduced the decision to the sign of one coefficient in an equation describing the competing phases. Calculating it required higher-order corrections that were substantially more complicated for this theory than for a simpler case the researchers subsequently completed.

Claude calculated a negative coefficient, establishing the abrupt transition at weak coupling. The paper uses a Hamiltonian calculation, tracking the theory’s energies and interactions, and checks components against independent calculations and known special cases. Its result also reproduces the ordering of the competing stable and unstable states in the gravitational account. The paper explicitly leaves open whether the abrupt transition continues uninterrupted through intermediate interaction strengths; agreement at the two extremes does not establish the entire path between them.

The accompanying “The spinless modular bootstrap at large central charge” concerns gravity in a universe with two spatial dimensions and one time dimension. Its quantum description lives in one fewer dimension. Physicists constrain candidate descriptions by requiring that two ways of computing a theory’s thermal behaviour agree. The spinless method combines states without using information about their angular momentum. A contradiction excludes a proposed spectrum, the collection of states and energies a theory permits.

Thomas Hartman, Dalimil Mazáč and Leonardo Rastelli established an earlier analytic bound in their 2019 paper “Sphere Packing and Quantum Gravity”, published in the Journal of High Energy Physics. They connected the consistency test to the problem of packing spheres efficiently, and bounded the scaling dimension of the lightest non-vacuum primary state by approximately c/8.503 when c is large. A primary is a state from which the theory’s spacetime symmetries generate related states; the vacuum is its lowest-energy state. Here c, the central charge, measures the quantum theory’s degrees of freedom; scaling dimension corresponds to an energy in its gravitational description. That connection gave researchers mathematical tools for restricting possible black-hole spectra.

Claude’s result improves the asymptotic ceiling to approximately c/9.1266 and proves that the specified class of spinless tests cannot improve that leading term further. It uses a pattern in numerical results to construct a spectral curve, a mathematical description of the functions used to exclude candidate spectra. The proof recasts those functions as an electrostatic potential and shows both why a stronger bound is impossible within the class and how to attain the stated limit. The ceiling remains above the black-hole threshold near c/12. Further restrictions require additional information; this theorem does not determine which candidate spectra correspond to complete quantum theories.

Anthropic describes a research process with human initiation and substantial work delegated to agents. In August, Hongbin Chen asked Claude for open problems concerning black holes and bootstrap methods, then assigned agents to its 22 suggestions. They worked for roughly six weeks and could share progress. For the bootstrap result, an agent investigating sphere packing helped turn another agent’s observation into a method. Anthropic reports independent solutions, Lean verification in September and a physicist’s check of every main result. The preprint separately labels its attainment proof computer-assisted and distinguishes proved statements from finer conjectures.

The Hawking-Page paper’s provenance identifies a physicist-prompted calculation and a separate Claude calculation using another method. Its appendix describes a coordinating agent, independently written programs and tests against known answers. It also records an exception to duplicate checking for higher-order terms in one series. The appendix describes two programs agreeing with each other but failing a known-answer test; resolving that disagreement exposed a missing correction to the calculation. Anthropic’s broader account says the two independent approaches to the full problem reached the same answer.

Dan Kagan-Kans’s Understanding AI interviews, included in yesterday’s issue, describe the surrounding debate about automating theoretical work. Juan Maldacena told him that AI helps with calculations previously too lengthy to undertake; Daniel Harlow emphasized the work of deciding which questions to ask. The interviews describe the field’s changing practices. Anthropic’s experiment extends into that choice of questions, while retaining human problem framing and checks of the resulting work.
