Nvidia's sales team introduced Alibaba to Megaspeed's founder, The Information reports

The Information reports that Nvidia’s introductions helped the venture win Alibaba’s business and bank financing. Laiba Rehman questions the inspection provisions and delayed safeguards in the Chip Security Act.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 910 words · ~5 min

Nvidia’s sales team introduced Alibaba to Alice Huang twice over several months in 2022, helping the business that became Megaspeed secure a major customer and bank financing, The Information reported on November 17. Juro Osawa and Qianer Liu cite three people with direct knowledge of the introductions, which preceded US restrictions on exports of Nvidia’s most advanced chips to China. Huang was starting a business renting chips to cloud companies. Asked about Nvidia’s role, a spokesman said it works across the industry to “comply with the evolving rules regarding cloud services” while building infrastructure in approved regions.

Two of those sources describe how the introductions helped both companies. Alibaba was a large buyer of Nvidia’s A100 chip, and Nvidia wanted to retain its business and its foothold among Chinese customers. Huang was a newcomer to chip rental whom Alibaba executives did not know. Winning Alibaba’s business gave her venture credibility with banks and helped it obtain loans for Nvidia chips. The Information reports that Huang later served as Megaspeed’s managing director for eight months before relinquishing the role and formally cutting her ties.

A person directly involved in the financing told the reporters that several regional Asian banks had lent money for Nvidia-equipped servers before Megaspeed formally launched its rental business in 2023. Huang and her associates had pitched bankers a Southeast Asian datacenter business serving major customers such as Alibaba. One of the three directly knowledgeable sources says the banks’ compliance departments concluded that the arrangement would not violate US export controls if the restricted chips were not sold to China. The financing source says having Alibaba as a customer made lending easier. The article names no banks or loan amounts.

For documentary support, The Information cites corporate records in Malaysia and Singapore showing that Speedmatrix, Megaspeed’s Malaysian subsidiary, pledged assets in 2024 to a Singapore company ultimately owned by Alibaba. The records support a financial relationship; the account of Nvidia arranging the introductions comes from the reporters’ sources. One source says Nvidia introduced other companies to Alibaba for the same purpose, but the reporters could not identify them.

Nvidia’s August 2022 SEC filing confirms that Washington imposed an immediate licensing requirement on August 26 for exports of A100 and forthcoming H100 chips, and systems containing them, to China, including Hong Kong, and Russia. After the restrictions, one source told The Information, Nvidia sales staff again suggested that Megaspeed rent Alibaba capacity in Southeast Asia. The Information describes that offshore rental arrangement as lawful under the rules then in force, while reporting that some chips bought by Huang’s companies were physically diverted to China in breach of US rules. Its account does not establish that Nvidia’s sales staff knew about illegal diversion. The remote-compute restrictions covered November 16 address the separate question of Chinese companies using chips that remain overseas.

Nvidia says it ended its Megaspeed relationship in June 2025. According to CNN’s account of the November 16 Commerce-Justice statement, Commerce had investigated Megaspeed since 2024, requested Nvidia’s sales records in May 2025, and conducted an on-site check with Singaporean authorities the following month. DOJ’s criminal investigation was covered November 17; no charges had been announced. Megaspeed said recent reports lacked verifiable evidence and that Huang left in August 2025. Nvidia’s board created an export-control oversight committee on November 17.

In her November 18 essay, Laiba Rehman examines how the proposed Chip Security Act would detect diversion. She reads the version advanced by the House Foreign Affairs Committee on March 26. The committee text allows several mechanisms: on-site audits or inventories, attestations by a US-headquartered entity or its subsidiaries maintaining secure control of the chips, location checks through a trusted server, or other mechanisms Commerce approves. Inspections are consequently optional. Rehman objects that describing them as “periodic” does not require randomness or short notice, leaving room for arrangements in which firms could move equipment before inspectors arrive. The wording does not itself require a predictable schedule.

Rehman also compares the deadlines with the May 15, 2025 introduced bill. That version would have required location verification within 180 days of enactment. The committee substitute allows one year for its broader choice of safeguards, and extends the deadline for establishing diversion and tampering reporting requirements from 180 days to one year. Both are deadlines for action after enactment, not scheduled implementation dates. Rehman urges Americans to press Commerce to act sooner.

The provisions for enhanced safeguards also changed. The original required an assessment within one year and required Commerce to impose mechanisms it found appropriate within two years after that assessment. The committee text makes the initial assessment due within two years, followed by annual assessments for three years, and says Commerce “may” require enhancements within two years of an assessment. Rehman’s roughly four-year timetable describes how long adoption following the initial assessment could take. Adoption would remain discretionary; the fourth anniversary would not make enhanced safeguards mandatory.

The committee text also says the Act should not be construed to direct Commerce to require kill switches, geofencing or hardware modifications for location verification. Rehman wants mandatory records of exported chips’ locations and end users, which she argues could support future treaty verification. That record-keeping authority was already discretionary in the 2025 bill. She attributes the longer deadlines to assumed industry pressure, but does not establish that explanation. Her practical recommendation is to strengthen inspection and record-keeping requirements while pushing implementation ahead of the statutory ceilings.
