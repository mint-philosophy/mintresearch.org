Anton Leicht’s plan for slowing AI in stages

Research-compute cuts could buy time for capability limits, Leicht argues, with safety requirements replacing them once evidence and oversight improve.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 979 words · ~5 min

Anton Leicht argues that governments should prepare several ways to slow AI development before rapid progress forces them to improvise. In Putting Pacing Into Practice, published November 18 in Threading the Needle, he proposes a sequence: begin with a cut to research computing, move to limits on capability gains per period, then permit faster development once companies can demonstrate safety. Each phase would buy time to make the next workable. His immediate recommendation is to build the accounting, evaluations and safety evidence these policies would require.

Leicht distinguishes controlling development speed from requiring particular safeguards. Slower progress could give cyberdefenders, legislators and evaluators time to adapt even without new substantive safety standards. Conversely, models could satisfy safety requirements while improving too quickly for institutions to oversee them. He argues that supporters of rapid technological development should take pacing seriously because an uncontrolled acceleration could provoke much harsher restrictions, including government control over who can develop AI. He retains his earlier opposition to a pause, which he feared would let computing resources, investment and talent regroup elsewhere.

His concern extends beyond fully autonomous AI research. In Iron Makes Iron, Dean W. Ball distinguishes ordinary use of a technology to improve itself from systems independently planning and executing their successors' development. Ball opposes surrendering practical control of AI companies to AI, while warning that banning the broader category could prohibit most frontier development. Leicht accepts the distinction but argues that even human-directed, AI-assisted research could advance faster than institutions can respond. A limit on capability growth would constrain that acceleration without requiring a judgment that autonomous self-improvement had already begun.

Leicht would use computing restrictions first because they could reduce research immediately once the accounting was ready. But a rule covering only a developer's internal research could shift work to outside suppliers. Customers using its models might curate training data or build reinforcement-learning environments, then supply those improvements back to the developer. Redwood's compute-accounting proposal, discussed November 17, addresses this route by counting relevant customer work against the provider's allowance. Leicht's broader warning is that developers and overseers need a shared, independently tested account of where computing goes before a crisis makes those classifications urgent.

Efficiency gains would erode a one-time cut even if companies complied. Leicht calculates that halving research computing would postpone a return to the previous research pace by roughly eight months, assuming algorithmic efficiency continued tripling annually and research speed scaled with effective computing. His empirical reference is Anson Ho and colleagues' NeurIPS 2024 paper, Algorithmic progress in language models. Using more than 200 evaluations from 2012 through 2023, the researchers estimated how much computing older language models needed for a fixed performance level. Leicht applies that historical efficiency trend to a hypothetical research slowdown; he uses it to illustrate how research could regain its earlier pace despite a continuing cut.

He would then limit the increase in internal model capabilities over a specified period. A regulator or industry body would adjust the allowance as confidence in safety and oversight changed. Leicht thinks repeated improvements after initial training create more opportunities to measure progress and intervene than a few large training runs would. The difficult part is constructing an assessment that tracks the capabilities authorities actually want to restrain. A developer could otherwise improve automated AI research while weakening unrelated abilities, leaving its aggregate score within the allowance. He wants tests that resist deliberate manipulation, particularly for automated research and dangerous misuse.

Leicht also expects capability limits to provoke less disagreement between developers than the alternatives. He argues that percentage allocations of computing disadvantage companies with smaller installations, while safety evaluations can privilege particular approaches to alignment. A shared capability measure might distribute the burden more fairly. His proposal therefore depends on both technical measurement and industry acceptance: evaluators would have to detect dangerous specialization, and competing developers would have to regard the assessment as credible.

Capability limits would create another problem. Computing capacity and unused technical improvements could accumulate while developers were prohibited from applying them fully. Repealing or allowing the limit to expire could then produce unusually fast catch-up growth. Leicht credits Joe Carlsmith's March essay on restraining AI development, which warned about this accumulation and argued that restraint needs continuing provisions for safety research and permission to advance safely. Leicht argues that policymakers adopting speed limits must maintain them or replace them with another effective restriction.

Leicht would eventually replace speed limits with requirements to demonstrate safety before further development. He thinks imposing such requirements today could amount to an indefinite pause because convincing safety cases are not yet available. Safety requirements can also produce alternating periods of stagnation and rapid growth as developers clear successive assessments. He would accept faster progress once monitoring, institutional oversight and evidence of safety had improved enough to handle it. Until then, passing a safety assessment would not by itself ensure a manageable rate of change.

Thomas Larsen and Romeo Dean identify a related problem in their separate AI Futures proposal for compute reallocation. They also favor input restrictions as a near-term measure, but argue that capability limits should be accompanied by restrictions on algorithmic progress. Otherwise, unused improvements could enable a sudden jump when restrictions ended or leak to developers outside the agreement. Leicht acknowledges the accumulated-progress problem; his proposed answer is to sustain speed limits until safety requirements can replace them.

Leicht expects political circumstances to determine which policy governments choose. Harmful incidents might encourage safety requirements, while warnings from lab insiders might favor computing or capability limits. His practical agenda is therefore to prepare each option now. Developers can document computing use and submit it to independent scrutiny; evaluators can test capability measures against manipulation; safety researchers can develop claims that evidence could disprove. Companies can undertake that preparation individually, he argues, even while collective agreements remain difficult.
