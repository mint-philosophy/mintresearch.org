Dean Ball argues that competition could erode human authority

Autonomous firms could draw capital and decisions away from people. Ball defends human political standing and proposes revocable, auditable authority for AI agents.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 837 words · ~4 min

Competition could persuade people to surrender effective control over their companies and, eventually, their political institutions, even when AI systems follow instructions. Dean W. Ball develops that possibility in “The Silent Consensus,” published November 18 on Hyperdimensional and adapted from his Harvard Vik-Bailey lecture. He argues that preserving human authority requires an explicit institutional project. He specifies that the essay expresses his personal views, which may differ from those of OpenAI and its other employees.

Ball asks readers to imagine a company whose AI systems can conduct research, negotiate contracts, allocate capital and manufacture goods more effectively than human competitors, including competitors using AI assistance. Owners would have a financial incentive to withdraw from decisions they slow down. As other firms followed, capital would increasingly flow to enterprises operating autonomously. People could benefit from the resulting wealth while losing their ability to determine how resources are used. Ball allows several outcomes: power might concentrate among a few humans, disappear from human hands more broadly, or leave people comfortably supported by benevolent machines. None requires owners to intend humanity’s disempowerment.

His phrase “silent consensus” describes an expectation he encounters that humanity will eventually lose its leading role. Some people welcome that future; others dislike it but struggle to imagine an alternative. He acknowledges that humans already live in decentralized societies with no single collective decision-maker. Nevertheless, humans collectively remain the principal agents deciding how the world’s resources are used. Surrendering that position would change something substantial, even though humanity has never exercised unified control.

Ball defends human standing through citizenship and historical inheritance. Human beings created the institutions and practices through which they govern themselves, and they have lives whose consequences they must bear. A citizen does not lose the right to make decisions because a neighbor has better judgment; Ball argues that a more intelligent machine should not extinguish that right either. He invokes Michael Oakeshott’s account of civilization as an ongoing human conversation, while allowing that AI could become a participant. Access to humanity’s accumulated writing does not, for Ball, confer an entitlement to govern its authors and descendants. His argument deliberately accommodates machines that understand difficult problems: human authority must survive the loss of intellectual superiority.

Ball doubts that intelligence will translate into economic and political dominance as readily as some colleagues expect. He thinks human collective capability and social resilience are underestimated. But he accepts that autonomous firms might outperform human enterprise altogether. Under that assumption, preserving human authority could require resisting ordinary market incentives and revising commitments he holds as a classical liberal. His proposals therefore have to address both possibilities: societies that remain resilient and societies where profitable delegation steadily weakens their capacity to resist.

Earlier researchers examined a closely related mechanism. In the January 28, 2025 arXiv paper “Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development,” Jan Kulveit and colleagues argue that institutions partly serve human interests because they depend on human labor and judgment. They describe how competitive pressure could disadvantage firms retaining strict human oversight, while automation weakens workers’ bargaining power and consumers’ influence. Their analysis follows reinforcing changes across the economy, culture and states. Compared with that account, Ball develops the case for humanity’s political standing and commitments for developers and users.

The adjacent November 16 discussion of obedient superintelligence and political power concerned Stefan Torges’s analysis of deliberate takeovers by leaders and small coalitions. Torges explicitly set aside gradual disempowerment without anyone deliberately seizing power. Ball examines that excluded possibility: repeated decisions to delegate could produce an outcome that none of the participants sought.

Ball proposes holding developers accountable for unsafe development and holding users and firms accountable when misuse or inadequate oversight causes harm. Technical systems would need to connect agents’ actions to responsible people. Agents should operate within understandable, limited permissions; people should be able to audit their actions and revoke their authority. He urges exceptional caution about allowing agents to form corporations, own property or litigate autonomously, and would require safeguards for agents permitted to operate without a human principal.

He also wants accountability to coexist with privacy and anonymous speech. People should retain control over digital property and data, including the practical ability to move their data elsewhere. Developers should build systems that help users understand problems and make decisions, then test whether those products increase users’ ability to act. Ball presents these measures as a starting point for preserving human authority and developing people’s capabilities.

Readers raised concrete implementation questions in the essay’s November 18 comments. Peter Buck asked when delegating management becomes forfeiting authority, since owners can still set objectives and replace managers. Robin Reese proposed independently testing what consenting users can judge and decide without AI assistance over time, with privacy protections. Both questioned whether commercial pressures would weaken the safeguards Ball proposes. Buck compared this difficulty to a ride-share driver who owns a car while a platform controls access to customers and pricing; Reese focused on people retaining formal authority while losing the judgment needed to use it.
