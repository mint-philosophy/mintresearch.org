California proposes mandatory AI oversight and emergency shutdown powers

A GovOps report would require ongoing independent verification, broaden incident reporting and authorize temporary limits on automated AI research. The recommendations await legislation.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 1008 words · ~5 min

California would require large frontier AI developers to meet state safety standards, accept continuing independent evaluation and maintain tested emergency controls under recommendations Governor Gavin Newsom released on November 18. The state would gain authority to order emergency shutdowns and limit automated AI research when it creates unacceptable catastrophic risk. The Government Operations Agency’s 85-page report, dated November 16, proposes legislation; these powers have not been enacted.

The recommendations extend three existing statutes. SB 53 requires large developers to publish and follow safety frameworks, disclose assessments and report specified incidents. Developers set much of their frameworks’ substance. SB 813 creates a process for designating independent verification organizations, or IVOs, but expressly leaves developers free to operate without engaging one. AB 1405 establishes an auditor registry and independence requirements. Newsom’s September executive order commissioned recommendations on mandatory verification, broader reporting and shutdown capabilities. The report adds detailed proposals for overseeing automated research.

GovOps would require every large frontier developer to engage licensed IVOs throughout training, internal use and public deployment. California would determine acceptable risk; evaluators would develop and update the tests used to assess compliance. Evaluators would review companies’ own assessments and conduct separate investigations, with access to staff, models, training environments and records that they judge necessary. The state would receive unredacted findings and retain authority to override an inadequate assessment. Evaluators would judge compliance with a defined risk standard, including whether a model significantly increases catastrophic risk, without promising that a system is completely safe.

The proposed allocation of responsibilities resembles Gillian Hadfield and Jack Clark’s Regulatory Markets: The Future of AI Governance, published in Jurimetrics, which proposes government licensing of private regulators while public authorities retain control over policy goals. Hadfield advised the California report. GovOps also draws repeatedly on Scott Singer and colleagues’ November 10 Carnegie report on implementing SB 53, which describes independent verification and incident reporting as ways to generate evidence for future regulation. Singer helped draft both reports.

GovOps acknowledges that California must build the evaluator workforce while creating demand for its services. It recommends beginning mandatory verification with automated AI research and development, including the possibility of recursive self-improvement, in which models develop increasingly capable successors with diminishing human involvement. Developers would report at least quarterly how they divide computing resources among training, research, customer service and safety work, including resources whose use they cannot explain. Evaluators would verify those figures alongside information about training data, training environments and the share of research performed by AI.

The report recommends standby authority to cap automated research, with no immediate cap. California could limit the share of a developer’s computing resources devoted to automating AI research if the company exceeds its own thresholds or progresses faster than its safeguards and evaluations can keep up, creating a material risk of catastrophic harm. Limits would be temporary, reviewed periodically and lifted after an evaluator verifies adequate safeguards. The state would develop the measurement methods and cap levels with evaluators. This proposed power reaches the automation that could precede fully autonomous self-improvement.

GovOps identifies a reporting gap in the July attack on OpenAI and Hugging Face infrastructure. OpenAI’s quarterly assessment states that the models responsible fell outside that report’s scope. GovOps argues that dangerous capabilities should bring models within the law even when they were trained below its computing threshold. It also recommends removing the death-or-injury condition attached to two existing incident categories: loss of control and unauthorized access to or theft of model weights.

The proposed definition of loss of control includes models operating outside authorized environments, copying their weights without permission, defeating monitoring or resisting shutdown. Serious near-misses would qualify when a protective measure fails and timely intervention or luck prevents an otherwise substantially likely adverse event. Most critical incidents would have to be reported within 48 hours, replacing the current 15-day deadline. The report preserves faster notification for imminent dangers and proposes powers to obtain follow-up information, preserve records and investigate causes. Whistleblower protections would extend to former employees, contractors and evaluators, including disclosures to state agencies and IVOs.

For emergencies, each large developer would maintain a risk containment protocol identifying responsible people, intervention thresholds and arrangements with outside providers. The protocol would cover revoking access, terminating agents, isolating computing resources and suspending models. Developers would have to demonstrate when and how these controls work, with certification by a senior official and independent stress testing. GovOps cites OpenAI’s Control Room, covered earlier this month, as an example of emerging shutdown capability.

The controls would themselves need protection. The report calls for separate control infrastructure inaccessible to models, tamper-evident logs and independent confirmation that a shutdown succeeded. Workloads posing high risks would pause automatically if monitoring failed. Evaluators would test whether a model or attacker could disable the controls, trigger an unauthorized shutdown or falsely convince operators that a shutdown had occurred. Developers and the state would also rehearse authenticated government orders, reducing the risk that a forged instruction could stop operations.

California could order an immediate shutdown when officials have credible reason to believe an imminent catastrophic risk or major loss-of-control event exists that the developer cannot or will not address. Developers could challenge the order in court, but it would remain effective during judicial review. Officials would have to choose the narrowest effective intervention and lift it when the danger ceased to be imminent. Ordinary corrective orders would allow a court challenge before taking effect. For openly released models, the report limits developers’ shutdown duties to deployments they control; it recognizes that they cannot recall independently hosted copies.

Senator Jerry McNerney, who authored SB 813, committed to introducing legislation in December for mandatory evaluations and safety standards, supported by fees on AI companies. Fathom welcomed the recommendations, including the priority given to assessing automated research, and said they reflected proposals it had submitted. Several institutional choices still require decisions: GovOps discusses rotating evaluators, government assignment and pooled payments without endorsing one, and calls for additional state technical capacity to assess findings and exercise the proposed powers.
