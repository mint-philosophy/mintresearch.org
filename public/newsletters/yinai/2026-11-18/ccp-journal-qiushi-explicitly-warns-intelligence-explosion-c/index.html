Qiushi links survival risks to international AI governance

Qing Yuan examines why shared safety principles struggle to become compatible national rules.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 419 words · ~2 min

Qing Yuan warns that an intelligence explosion could produce goals incompatible with human expectations and threaten human survival. The November 16 Qiushi essay “How to Respond to the Practical Challenges of Global AI Governance” connects that possibility with risks crossing borders faster than governments can respond.

Qing argues that changing capabilities defeat static assessments, while incompatible audit standards, risk categories and data rules obstruct cooperation. Countries lacking computing and skills also lack influence over rulemaking. He advocates using AI for risk detection and response, alongside more compatible regulatory standards.

His proposed cooperation starts with less contentious applications such as disaster relief, building trust while preserving national regulatory choices. Human control and continued innovation are both requirements. This is an argument in the Party journal, without new binding obligations.

Nathan Calvin’s November 18 response explains why the warning attracted attention. Calvin has sometimes doubted whether Chinese discussion of AI safety addresses the concerns of Western safety advocates. He distinguishes protecting Party security from preventing existential catastrophe or the loss of all human control, and finds this passage unusually explicit about the latter. His judgment concerns the language he has encountered; it does not establish the first Chinese recognition of such risks.

China’s official Global AI Governance Initiative, published by its foreign ministry in October 2023, already called for AI to remain under human control. It proposed testing and assessment according to risk levels, with developers making systems predictable and capable of being inspected, monitored and traced. The initiative paired those technical requirements with respect for national sovereignty and different domestic policies. It supported discussions about an international governing institution within the United Nations and assistance to developing countries. It also opposed supply-chain barriers and exclusive groups that obstruct other countries’ AI development. Safety cooperation and access to AI were joined in the same diplomatic proposal.

China also participated in the November 2023 Bletchley Declaration, which recognized potentially catastrophic harm from misuse and unintended failures of control. Its signatories called for shared scientific understanding and risk-based national policies, allowing approaches to differ under domestic law. Bletchley assigned developers of unusually powerful systems particular responsibility for testing and evaluation, alongside transparency about dangerous capabilities. It therefore established an earlier international basis for cooperation on control risks without requiring identical regulatory systems.

As policy analysis, this comparison suggests that Qing’s proposals depend on further agreements about evidence and enforcement. Governments could endorse evaluations while disputing acceptable deployment thresholds or recognition of foreign audits. Shared principles alone leave those practical decisions unsettled.
