AI Futures sets out how auditors could enforce research compute caps

Their reallocation plan specifies how auditors would check workloads and follow outsourced research, with a model-dependent estimate of extra time before advanced AI.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 November 2026 1028 words · ~5 min

Romeo Dean and Thomas Larsen propose giving independent auditors access to frontier AI companies' computing records, then limiting the share spent developing more capable models. Their November 17 proposal, “Pacing AI via Compute Reallocation,” was republished by the AI Futures Project on November 18. They roughly estimate that a 15% research allocation cap starting in January 2027 could delay an intelligence explosion by a year, allowing more time for safety research and government preparation. Their proposal specifies how to account for workloads, expose misclassification and detect research concealed as customer use.

The scheme incorporates Alek Westover and Nathan Sheffield's Redwood classification, which we covered on November 17. Computing would count toward the capabilities allowance by default, with exemptions for qualifying customer services, idle hardware, narrow products and specified safety activities. Eli Lifland's November 10 disclosure proposal explained why auditors must check both reported workloads and computing hidden from their view. The AI Futures Project had already proposed minimum allocations for customers and transparent safety research in August. Dean and Larsen now develop the accounting and enforcement arrangements needed to make such allocations credible.

Companies would log each workload's hardware and duration, with records appropriate to the work. Research entries would identify owners, code and datasets, including connections to jobs launched by AI agents. Inference entries would record the model, customer or internal account, token usage and whether outputs enter training. Transcripts would be retained where zero-data-retention commitments permit. Human reviewers and AI classifiers would then determine which workloads count toward the research cap.

The accounting appendix addresses a way companies could exploit differences between chips. Counting nominal computing operations could let them assign less useful hardware to exempt work while reserving better chips for research. The authors suggest converting chip-hours into a common unit using the companies' own assessments of chip value, publishing the conversion ratios and checking them against rental prices. Public disclosures, potentially every two weeks, would include allocation totals, methods, unresolved discrepancies and descriptions of sampled exempt projects. Projects using more computing would have a higher chance of being sampled.

Ordinary audits would reconcile reported chip-hours with compute contracts and datacenter capacity. Auditors would inspect workloads, review logging software, submit test jobs and independently check classifications. They would also examine subsidized contracts with large customers, unusually concentrated spending on exempt research, and confidential accounts from employees. Dean and Larsen think these checks could establish useful transparency today. Imposing a restrictive cap would create stronger incentives to conceal work, requiring more intrusive verification.

Their attack analysis considers unreported hardware, falsely idle GPUs, concealed efficiency improvements and research passed through external customers. Companies could subsidize a partner's model use, arrange access to its research and present the computation as ordinary customer service. The authors estimate that ordinary audits could miss capabilities work equivalent to 55% of total computing under an organized deception scenario. They assume leadership can enlist roughly 30 conspirators before whistleblowing becomes too likely, and discount diverted computing when it would be less useful than normal research. These figures express their judgments about possible evasion.

Under stronger auditing, inspectors would run software they control on company machines, collecting GPU records with exclusive or nearly exclusive access to the logging system. Power measurements and replayed workloads could test whether the reported activity matches actual computation. The authors also propose occasional checks of GPU memory, while noting that their technical feasibility is uncertain, especially for customers promised that their data will not be retained. Customer identity checks would make it harder to disguise employees as outsiders. Audits of major external users, supported by investigations of money flows and the provenance of purchased code and data, would address research outsourced to partners. The appendix suggests that sufficiently large customers should either avoid AI research, follow the disclosure regime themselves or have their usage counted against the provider's allowance.

With stronger measures, the authors estimate that undetected research could fall to the equivalent of about 6% of total computing even under leadership-backed deception. Their calculation assumes technically knowledgeable auditors and six months to implement the measures, followed by six months of operation. Auditors would also need to keep revising the classification rules: companies could favor nominally exempt safety projects that help improve general capabilities. The proposed checks include attempting to use those research results to improve models, then revising the exemptions or counting the corresponding work against the cap.

The worked slowdown calculation uses a 20% cap plus six percentage points of evasion. Against the authors' estimate that Anthropic currently devotes about three-quarters of its computing to capabilities research, that would cut research computing roughly threefold. Their model variants delay milestones for superhuman AI researchers and systems exceeding top human experts by four to nine months. The results depend on how much computing constrains research alongside human and AI labor, and how quickly AI improves the research process. If computing capacity keeps growing at the same rate, progress eventually returns toward its previous pace, with milestones reached later. The one-year estimate for the stricter 15% cap should be read alongside those assumptions.

Dean and Larsen favor limiting research inputs because delaying final training runs alone lets companies accumulate better algorithms. When training resumes, those improvements could produce a sudden increase in model capability; other developers might also acquire and use them. They would begin with voluntary disclosures and independent audits, followed by stronger verification. Binding disclosures or state and federal regulation could proceed alongside that pilot, eventually expanding into an international regime. They argue that public comparisons would reward companies for demonstrable restraint without requiring agreements among competitors on allocation levels.

The authors also argue that slowing US development could allow time to improve security and negotiate with China, while reducing the capability of models available to steal. In a follow-up response on LessWrong, Ansel disputes the inference from theft risk: better security might preserve a lead, and more capable models might help provide it. Ansel also suggests that a unilateral slowdown could encourage China to delay negotiations. Dean and Larsen present reallocation as a temporary measure; they expect the additional time to be used to establish a more durable system of international governance.
