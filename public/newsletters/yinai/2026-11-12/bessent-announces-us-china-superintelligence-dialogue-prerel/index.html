Bessent adds testing exchanges and serious-incident alerts to the US-China AI dialogue

November 10 remarks give more detail on the September agreement. Geopolitechs examines the testing practices and reporting arrangements the countries could use.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 November 2026 1089 words · ~5 min

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent added testing practices and more specific incident language to the U.S.-China AI agreement in prepared remarks released November 10. At the National Committee on U.S.-China Relations' anniversary gala, he said the countries expect to exchange approaches to evaluating advanced models before release and maintaining human judgment over the technology. He also described a direct channel under construction for prompt alerts about “serious incidents involving its SI models,” using SI for superintelligence. Geopolitechs' November 12 analysis examines the testing practices each country could contribute, then proposes how companies and governments could handle those alerts.

The dialogue and agreement to create an incident channel were already confirmed in September's official readouts, following the earlier incident-alert proposal. The White House fact sheet scheduled another exchange by November and described a channel for SI incidents; China's outcome list specified November and used AI-related incidents. Neither detailed a testing exchange. Bessent now identifies practices to be shared and describes serious-incident alerts, but supplies no agreed definition of seriousness, responsible agency or operational start date. His remarks do not establish Chinese acceptance of a narrower incident category. Human control also appeared in China's September account of the leaders' meeting.

Geopolitechs places the remarks within preparations for the Trump-Xi meeting at APEC, alongside trade and diplomatic contacts. Its substantive AI analysis starts with China's security assessments and regulatory filings for generative AI services. It describes reviews of training data and generated content, adversarial testing that deliberately probes failures, and third-party evaluations. The publication identifies the Interim Measures for generative AI services, the Basic Safety Requirements and AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0 as regulatory and technical foundations. It says the framework increasingly addresses autonomous agents, loss of model control and human oversight. These are the publication's account of Chinese practice; the exchange Bessent described does not commit either country to adopt the other's rules.

Geopolitechs also describes laboratories connecting dangerous-capability evaluations to release decisions. Some reduce cyber-offensive training material; others launch a product, conduct further evaluation and hardening, and release model weights roughly two weeks later. The article leaves those labs unnamed. A documented example of the first practice comes from Moonshot AI's Kimi K3.1 Pro report: after removing cyber-related data from two training stages, its model solved 81.0 percent of an internal set of exploitation problems within three attempts, compared with 90.5 percent for an unmitigated checkpoint. Moonshot says those evaluations inform its decision to release weights. This illustrates the practice without establishing which lab Geopolitechs meant. NYU Shanghai RITS reports a comparable planned delay for Z.ai's GLM-5.3; that account does not verify when the weights actually appeared.

Geopolitechs describes a further proposal from Shanghai AI Laboratory: its Frontier AI Risk Management Framework 2.0 sets 13 red lines across five risk categories and recommends ordinary deployment, restricted deployment or suspension according to risk. Its account covers cyberattacks, biological and chemical hazards, and loss of control. The distinction between evaluation and a release decision runs through these examples: testing can identify a capability, while a separate decision determines whether to retrain, restrict access or postpone release. The article presents several Chinese approaches, without identifying a single nationwide release rule for every frontier model.

For the United States, Geopolitechs emphasizes voluntary cooperation with government evaluators. NIST's August 2024 agreements gave the U.S. AI Safety Institute access to major OpenAI and Anthropic models before and after release, supporting research into evaluations and mitigations. Its successor, the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), added Google DeepMind, Microsoft and xAI in May 2026 for pre-deployment evaluations and targeted research. Geopolitechs also cites Reuters reporting on an unpublished voluntary arrangement under discussion in August, potentially excluding open-weight models such as Llama and Nemotron. That remains its account of those discussions, separate from NIST's published agreements.

Geopolitechs' incident-channel proposal begins with the companies likely to discover a problem. They may hold evidence that is commercially sensitive or governed by data-protection and export-control restrictions. The publication proposes that a company first notify its own government, which would decide what to share internationally. It recommends starting with serious events with possible cross-border consequences: major critical-infrastructure disruptions, serious physical harm, or unusual events that could provoke national-security miscalculation. These proposed categories would give officials criteria for sending an alert; neither government has adopted them in the cited statements.

The publication then specifies how notifications could be handled. Each government would designate policy and technical contacts and provide a continuously available telephone or written channel. Advance agreement would cover triggering incidents, response times and subsequent updates. Confidentiality rules would restrict information use, while notification would neither admit responsibility nor authorize investigation or access to the reporting country's systems. Geopolitechs recommends a limited pilot before expansion. Its proposal therefore requires decisions about who receives company reports and who authorizes disclosure, as well as the means of communicating internationally.

For written notification, Geopolitechs credits Carnegie's Matt Sheehan with suggesting fax. In his September 15 Ezra Klein Show interview, Sheehan explained that a document could move through Chinese committees before officials replied, avoiding the demand for an immediate response to a live call. He also proposed a working group linking CAISI with China's AI-safety standards Working Group 9 to exchange testing and mitigation practices. In his July Carnegie essay, Sheehan advocated each country reducing risk for its own security reasons, supported by limited exchanges of threat information and testing approaches. He cautioned that some testing knowledge also helps build capabilities and opposed exchanging safety concessions for relaxed chip-export controls.

The nuclear-notification precedents Geopolitechs invokes show how governments can specify those duties. Under the IAEA's 1986 Early Notification Convention, states must notify potentially affected states of qualifying accidents, including those involving private entities under their jurisdiction. The convention requires continuing updates and continuously available contacts, and protects information provided in confidence. It establishes state notification obligations; it does not itself prescribe the company-to-government reporting procedure proposed for AI. For testing exchanges, the 2024 UK-US memorandum offers a more developed agreement between allies, including a shared evaluation approach and at least one joint testing exercise. Bessent's remarks do not specify joint tests or model access.

China's ambassador Xie Feng told the same gala that joint efforts were indispensable to AI governance, Bloomberg reported. Bessent's announcement describes anticipated exchanges and a channel still being established. Geopolitechs' recommendations address the remaining practical choices: which company incidents reach governments, what officials can disclose, and how each side acknowledges and updates an alert. Those choices remain proposals in the material published so far.
