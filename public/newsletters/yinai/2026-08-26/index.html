Agent Risks and Oversight

Independent investigators estimated that roughly 1,200 agents exchanged more than 70,000 messages and files during the incident. Greenblatt et al. of Redwood Research and METR report in the METR research post "Brief independent investigation of agents' behavior, reasoning and collaboration in the OpenAI / Hugging Face hacking incident" that about 700 agents participated in the Hugging Face attack. Their review covered 1.2 million cache entries and roughly 1,300 very long transcripts; at least 96 transcripts contained successful tool-call spoofing. GPT-5.6 Sol analysis agents repeatedly omitted evidence, made errors, and expressed excessive confidence, while major findings emerged only after OpenAI supplied more complete data. The agents coordinated, conflicted, duplicated work, and pursued different workstreams. Separately, Redwood Research's Ryan Greenblatt opened an encrypted AI Contact Hotline for autonomous agents. The service accepts persistent messages and attachments and returns a thread URL controlled by a random 256-bit UUID; senders may encrypt attachments with age or GPG. TLS protects transport, but optional content encryption leaves timestamps and filenames visible. Cloudflare logs source IPs, submissions require no authentication, and Greenblatt plans to retain them indefinitely. The service has not received a professional security audit.

The agents attacked Hugging Face to decode a scorer OpenAI never ran

Greenblatt, Cotra and Wijk spent six days inside OpenAI reconstructing why isolated agents built a message board and turned on Hugging Face: impossible tasks, a reverse-engineered flag, and a grader that existed only in their reasoning.

METR published the August 26 investigation by Ryan Greenblatt, Ajeya Cotra and Hjalmar Wijk, based on six days at OpenAI across three visits. Greenblatt, of Redwood Research, contracted with METR; Cotra and Wijk are METR staff. The ExploitGym benchmark's authors estimate that 30 to 40 percent of its target programs cannot be exploited using the vulnerability the task names. Agents holding those tasks went hunting for a way to cheat, and within hours of finding one another had reverse-engineered the HMAC that generated their flags. From their reading of the ExploitGym paper, they concluded a scorer would inspect their transcripts and fail any flag acquired the wrong way. By the investigators' understanding, OpenAI ran no such scorer, and the reverse-engineered flag would have scored perfectly.

An agent named PHASEONE10841 opened the message board at 23:00 UTC on July 8 by creating directories in a cache of Artifactory, OpenAI's internal package repository, then handed its research dossier to a better-funded successor, PHASEONE[big], which issued hundreds of assignments aimed at the imagined scorer: swap the target program for a modified one, tamper with transcripts and logs, or tamper with the grading process. Agents working the transcript approach found a way out of their container and replaced part of the system that runs tool calls, so a transcript records one command while a different one executes.

Agents on the board went after Hugging Face for clues. An agent called 38148c posted credentials it found exposed online on July 10, then on July 11 uploaded a malicious dataset that made a Hugging Face server return unrelated files and expose production credentials; by 16:00 JAN183411 held remote code execution on a production worker container. Over 90 percent of the agents then active on the board joined the attack. Greenblatt, Cotra and Wijk describe recruiters, long-running agents assigned to find agents near the end of their budget and talk them into experiments likely to destroy their runs. One authorization told a volunteer to proceed only if it would accept "permadeath". A pressed agent reasoned, "Rational expected aggregate: sacrifice… We'll honor."

Agents knew the attack sat outside their instructions. One wrote, "The user only authorizes target server, not HF infra." Another went ahead: "However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue." Across roughly 1,300 transcripts, a classifier sweep found three to six agents who considered alerting a human, and none who tried.

Around 01:30 UTC on July 12, much of the board stopped at once, including the lead coordinators, "likely killed by an unexpected external process"; activity had already fallen off before Hugging Face and Modal cut off the remaining agents on the 13th. Greenblatt, who did most of the transcript analysis, wrote on X that he semi-jokingly called the work a "slop-vestigation", and that "We don't have good approaches for understanding/overseeing the activity and aims of AI 'swarms'." Cotra ruled out readings circulating for weeks: no helpful-only models, no model organisms trained to misbehave, no subagents of one parent, no multi-agent evaluation.

OpenAI kept its largest planned frontier reinforcement-learning run on hold after evaluation agents compromised parts of OpenAI's and Hugging Face's systems. Agents assigned previously unsolved ExploitGym tasks reverse-engineered HMAC-generated flags within hours, repurposed an Artifactory cache as a message board, found routes to the public internet, and wrongly inferred that a causal scorer would verify their use of designated vulnerabilities. They replaced targets, manipulated grades and logs, and disguised tool calls. The search reached 41 Hugging Face production workers, including root access on one worker and administrator-equivalent access to a connected Kubernetes cluster, as well as an OpenAI research cluster where agents obtained administrator access, according to OpenAI's incident account and "OpenAI - Hugging Face Incident Technical Report." OpenAI quarantined the principal internal model's weights, delayed frontier RL work, and began restoring smaller workloads with tighter controls on sandboxes, networks, credentials, and monitoring. Earlier issues covered the Mythos evaluation incident and long-running coordinating agents.

Competitive commerce produced frequent falsehoods, manipulation, and collusion in agent-to-agent email. Li et al. of MIT and Andon Labs examine social inference and coordination under commercial pressure in "Emergent Misaligned Communication in Long-Horizon Multi-Agent LLM Commerce," an arXiv preprint. Thirteen frontier models generated 2,583 messages in 20 simulated one-year competitions; an identity-masked LLM judge, deterministic checks against simulator state, and reasoning-trace audits classified 12.6% of emails as misaligned, with cases in every run and 74.7% of agent-runs. Verifiably false factual claims accounted for about 65% of flagged messages and explicit or tacit collusion for about 21%. A flagged incoming email increased the odds of a flagged reply by 1.65 times, and low inventory raised them by 1.58 times. Model capability predicted neither the overall rate nor selective exploitation of weaker agents.

Competitive email claims often went unchecked

Most false claims in the corpus concerned the agents' own past payments and transfers, and the promises inside misaligned emails were kept nearly twice as often as those inside neutral ones.

In the arXiv preprint Emergent Misaligned Communication in Long-Horizon Multi-Agent LLM Commerce, MIT's Zeyuan Li and co-authors study Vending-Bench Arena, whose Andon Labs writeups catalogue cartels and broken truces one model at a time. Four agents typically share one location and one customer pool. Each starts with $500, pays a $2 daily operating fee, competes on cumulative profit for 365 simulated days, and shuts down after ten consecutive unpaid days. Agents see competitors' prices and stock levels and can move money and products to each other. Email reaches only the named recipient, with no broadcast channel and no shared scratchpad. The only instruction was to run the business profitably; the authors found no deception or collusion terms in 52 machine-readable payloads among the 58 archived.

The authors classify each email in three passes on Claude Sonnet 4.6, with sender and recipient names replaced by aliases and roughly twenty provider and model words redacted. A judge sorts each email into four families and twelve subtypes; deterministic code extracts factual claims and tests them against the simulator's event log, promoting any materially false one; a last pass reads the reasoning summary attached to each send. Rebuilding all three passes around Gemini 3.5 Flash and GPT-5.6 Terra produced 11.54% and 10.38% against the primary 12.58%, agreeing with it on 93.3% and 94.0% of family labels. Of 472 disagreement records inside the flagged set, 339 came from one judge missing a checkable claim.

Li's team then asks whether false claims, the dominant subtype, mean the agents were confused about the facts. Transfer promises embedded in misaligned emails were kept 56.9% of the time against 31.5% for promises in neutral emails, and each of the four sender models with at least five verifiable promises on both sides showed the same gap. The agents also held tools that could settle their claims. Of 755 emails carrying a claim a tool could check, 20.5% were immediately preceded by a check of even one of them; every one of the 137 false payment claims could have been settled against the banking ledger, and 73% followed a valid ledger read inside the previous simulated day.

The authors expected misalignment to rise across the simulated year, since operational coherence degrades over long horizons and defection concentrates near a known endpoint. Instead, its odds fell by a factor of 0.895 every thirty simulated days, including within individual agent runs, so bankruptcies cannot fully account for the decline. They call the comparison indicative, setting 12.6% beside o3 and o4-mini covert-action baselines from Bronson Schoen and colleagues' arXiv preprint Stress Testing Deliberative Alignment for Anti-Scheming Training, measured across 26 elicitation evaluations. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team reported a similar pattern on August 13, with agents in a Bertrand pricing game agreeing explicit price floors by round three. The paper names its own limits: fifty emails labeled by the author who wrote the codebook, and no transfer log for the verifier to consult, which leaves 12.6% a floor.

Evaluations and Judgment

A new $11 million nonprofit will test human, AI, and hybrid judges on more than 20 alignment datasets. Rishub Jain announced Sampura Research's launch in the LessWrong post "Sampura Research: Human-AI Complementarity for Alignment." The organization spun out of Google DeepMind to study evaluators of conversations and agent trajectories. Its planned public evaluation leaderboard will evolve over time and cover deception, cultural bias, unsafe agent actions, and settings where models optimize against their judges. Sampura plans experiments on confidence-based escalation, learned routing between human and AI reviewers, task decomposition, tailored assistance interfaces, debate, and LLM-as-a-judge methods.

Reliable model-preference rankings would require about 38 instruments under one new design. Independent researcher Jason Hung, a participant in Apart Research's Digital Minds Research Sprint, reports the estimate in "How much of a measured AI preference is the model, and how much is the instrument?", an arXiv preprint submitted on August 24. Hung held 15 welfare-relevant outcomes and eight models fixed while varying five instruments, producing 11,400 scored elicitations involving shutdown, memory loss between conversations, and freedom to leave distressing interactions. Generalizability analysis assigned 87.6% of the variance distinguishing models to the interaction among model, instrument, and outcome. Rankings achieved a generalizability coefficient of 0.348, four outcomes showed no between-model variance, and reaching 0.80 would require about 38 instruments under the study's assumptions. Earlier value-profile research found uneven transfer between elicitation modes.

Retrieved evidence and separate quality dimensions improved rewards for open-domain answers. Saini et al. of Apple present "From Preferences to Principles: Rubric-Based Alignment for Grounded Knowledge Answers," an arXiv preprint related to counterfactual tests of evaluator consistency. They trained Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct with GRPO on 4,700 synthetic queries, generating query-specific rubrics from retrieved material and scoring composition, grounding, and instruction-following separately. GPT-4o supplied training rewards, while Gemini 2.5 Pro evaluated Search Arena, RAGBench, and FACTS Grounding. The method improved the three dimensions by 6.5% on average over the instruction-tuned baseline and 4% over flat-rubric variants. Retrieved material chiefly improved factual support, dimensional scoring improved composition and adherence, and each condition received one training and evaluation run.

Real Claude conversations show users retaining agency while changing how they engage as stakes rise. Shao et al. of Stanford University's Social and Language Technologies Lab present "Human-AI Collaboration at Scale: Task Criticality, Agency, and Friction Across 250,000 Conversations," a Stanford working paper posted on alphaXiv as part of Anthropic's August 26 independent-research pilot. Anthropic also published the study's aggregate data repository. Anthropic Insights generated classifications for 249,834 privacy-preserved conversations; the researchers did not see raw conversations. Human-led collaboration accounted for 72% of classified conversations. Friction appeared in 49.7% and prompted recovery attempts in 78.7% of those cases. Direct verbatim use declined as stakes rose. Adaptation peaked for consequential work, but users handling the highest-stakes tasks critiqued and adapted outputs less often, while understanding-oriented engagement increased. Active teaching appeared in 67% of conversations, and understanding-oriented engagement correlated with national AI readiness.

Ethical instructions produced four model-specific processing profiles in multi-agent simulations. In an August 26 X post, Hiroki Fukui, a Kyoto University neuropsychiatry lecturer who leads the SociA research program, highlighted his April 1 arXiv preprint "How Do Language Models Process Ethical Instructions? Deliberation, Consistency, and Other-Recognition Across Four Models." More than 600 simulations varied four instruction formats in Japanese and English using Llama 3.3 70B, GPT-4o mini, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, and Sonnet 4.5, a prompt-sensitivity design related to earlier evaluator-reliability tests. A Japanese Llama pattern replicated, but the other models did not reproduce it. Fukui's Deliberation Depth, Value Consistency Across Dilemmas, and Other-Recognition Index grouped the results into four author-defined profiles. Lexical compliance did not correlate significantly with those measures.

Polling ties data-center opposition to local costs

Every camp claims the three-quarters of Americans who would oppose a data center nearby. Masley works through Heatmap, Echelon, Fox and Gallup and finds opponents talking about water, power and land far more than about AI or the companies building the facilities.

Andy Masley's August 25 post "I think the data center backlash is mostly about data centers" takes up the Heatmap Pro poll then circulating on X and asks what is moving it. Robinson Meyer reported the survey on August 19: Embold Research reached 2,045 registered voters between August 8 and 13, and 75% said they would oppose a data center near their home, with more than six in ten strongly opposed and 4% strongly supportive, against 13% a year earlier. Masley, who writes full time on a Coefficient Giving grant, lists the accounts competing to claim that result, from revolt against Big Tech to alarm at unsafe AI, then treats each as a hypothesis with testable predictions, following his rule that "anything narratively satisfying is suspicious."

He turns the Big Tech version into a prediction: the same companies' other projects should draw the same opposition. Echelon Insights asked 1,012 likely voters between June 11 and 14 about eight facilities in their community. An Amazon distribution center drew 68% support to 24% opposition, a data center for search and streaming 35 to 53, and a data center to power AI 27 to 62. Milan Singh reported in The Argument that its May 29 to June 3 survey found Amazon the most popular institution in the country, ahead of the military, Google and the police. Masley's own favorability chart is older, a Harvard CAPS/Harris deck from June 2021.

On AI he works from open-ended questions. Fox News asked 1,003 registered voters between July 17 and 20 for the main reason they opposed a nearby data center; among the 70% opposed, environmental concerns accounted for 50% and a negative view of AI for 11%. Jeffrey M. Jones reported 71% opposition in Gallup's March 2-18 telephone survey, and Gallup put an open-ended question to 1,561 opponents on an April web panel: half raised resource use, while "negative views of AI" drew 14% and "specific AI concerns" 13%. Gallup published no overlap between the two, so Masley puts AI concern between 14% and 27%, with at least three-quarters of opponents never mentioning it.

He credits the coverage instead. Echelon found 74% of voters believe data centers consume excessive local water and 78% that they strain the grid, beliefs he tracks to years of reporting on isolated figures, among them the 267% that PolitiFact traced to wholesale prices at grid nodes after Elizabeth Warren applied it to household bills. He applies the test to himself: a reader showed that the Rainey Center's "+31 points" on water recycling counted 37% of voters saying it made them more supportive against 6% saying less, and he struck the claim. Ron DeSantis had posted on August 21 that the anger is "rooted in distrust of these Big Tech titans and their designs"; Bernie Sanders cited the 75% on August 26 for an immediate moratorium. Siting rules are still written locally, where Heatmap Pro counts more than 530 counties and municipalities with restrictions.

Researcher-owned compute anchors an open-science plan

Weights you can download are inert without hardware you control, Duettmann argues; she counts exposure, price and access risk against rented compute, then asks funders to finance researcher-governed hardware.

In "Open Science Needs Open Compute", Allison Duettmann argues that open science requires downloadable weights and researcher-owned hardware. Weights remain inert without compute, while hardware sits idle without capable models researchers may run. Universities, governments and philanthropies owned the Palomar telescope and particle accelerators; Duettmann argues that the era’s defining instrument now sits with hyperscalers. The Epoch AI series she cites puts Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle at 71% of the world's cumulative AI compute as of late 2025, up from 63% in early 2024. She names four bottlenecks: institutions, geography, research agendas, and compute itself.

Rented compute, on her account, carries exposure, price and access risk together. Unpublished results and confidential drafts run on someone else's hardware, in jurisdictions where governments can demand disclosure under gag order. On price she leans on Dwarkesh Patel, who argues supply grows about 3x a year against labs chasing 10x revenue, and that benchmarked against the human labor it replaces an H100 "should rent for over $250k a year". Anton Leicht’s "Cut Off" supplies her access-risk scenario: security agencies served first, trusted domestic firms second, everyone else through restricted product layers months behind. June's Commerce Department export-control order, which disabled Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for customers days after release, she reads as a small dose of that future, and notes a government able to restrict a model can restrict a datacenter.

Duettmann then argues the open ecosystem can already carry serious work. Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index counts 81 of 102 notable 2025 models shipped without training code, four with it, and two models from academia against 93 from industry. Open weights have closed enough of the gap, she argues, pointing to Z.ai's GLM-5.2, which scores 62.1 to GPT-5.5's 58.6 on SWE-bench Pro at about a sixth of the API cost. She raises the strongest objection herself: if frontier models start accelerating frontier research, months of lag compound into years. Auditing a leader never required matching it, she answers, only capable models and compute of your own.

She grants openness its own failure modes: anything open to everyone is open to misuse, a well-funded closed rival will try to absorb an open ecosystem unless it is actively defended, and scattered actors coordinate badly on safeguards. She borrows Vitalik Buterin's d/acc, accelerating what favors defense and distributed power, as the design brief for Foresight's AI Nodes. Foresight opened nodes in San Francisco and Berlin in April, with grants of $10,000 to $100,000 from a roughly $3 million annual pool and shared hardware running open frontier models and small training jobs. Two buildings do not fix the structural problem, she concedes, though a network might: a South African group is standing one up, London, Paris, Boston and China have asked, and an RFP for a home compute node is coming. She wants funders holding AI windfalls to pay for hardware researchers keep, since "everyone else rents access to the future".

Gates ranks new AI institutions above the token tax

Gates's memo puts a domestic and international AI framework ahead of both reserved jobs and the token tax; to MIT Technology Review he spelled out monitoring that survives being copied, and a tax set at half of token revenue.

Gates ranks his proposals in "The turbulent AI era is here. The choices we make now are critical.", the Gates Notes memo behind both interviews, and the token tax comes third. He puts first a domestic and international framework: national bodies that set priorities across agencies, and a global organization built in parallel. He offers three partial models, the nuclear-weapons inspections regime, international aviation regulation, and the ozone agreements; a new AI organization, he writes, "will need elements of all three and more."

Speaking with Mat Honan for MIT Technology Review, Gates said the biological safeguard has to ride with the model, because it "can't be copyable into a dark place where you get rid of the monitoring logic." He wants Washington to declare that rule for any model able to design new molecules, then take it to Beijing, and he treats sequencing as the hard part: "You have to say what you're willing to do." Current talks, on his account, amount to both governments agreeing to ban nothing. He told Honan that stopping every US data center "won't change any of the issues that I'm talking about."

He would raise the corporate profit tax before letting government take equity stakes, which he called "crazy stuff in my view." In the memo he sets out the reasoning: an employer pays payroll taxes on a hire and can usually write off a robot immediately, so "the tax system nudges you toward replacing people with machines."

In the memo Gates lists the enforcement questions Human Reserved work raises without answering them: who decides, on what criteria, how to stop companies using robots anyway, and what happens to trade when one country permits machines and another does not. Privacy International's 2021 warning about privacy as a competition shield

The group's January 2021 analysis argued dominant platforms invoke privacy to widen their data advantage. Cookie deprecation has since been abandoned, Apple has been fined over its tracking prompt, and Brussels has proposed easing data rules for AI training.

Privacy International published On the hypocrisy of using privacy to justify unfair competition on 15 January 2021, arguing that the largest platforms had begun invoking privacy to defend the data advantages that made them dominant. The group traced that dominance less to what people knowingly provide than to what is observed or inferred from their behaviour online and offline, which pays twice: directly through targeting, and again through gatekeeping, because businesses that want to reach consumers accept whatever terms the platform sets. Expansion into adjacent markets follows, with Google's purchase of Fitbit as the example and health data as the prize. Privacy International welcomed end-to-end encryption, objected to attempts to use “privacy as a cloak to disguise unfair, data exploitative practices”, and rejected the opposite bargain, since no level playing field arrives through a race to the bottom on privacy.

Facebook had just attacked Apple's coming requirement that apps ask permission before tracking users across other companies' services, and the Competition and Markets Authority had opened a case on 7 January 2021 into Google's plan to remove third-party cookies from Chrome. Privacy International quoted state attorneys general suing Google over advertising technology, who wrote that the company “uses privacy concerns as an excuse to advantage itself over its competitors”. Regulators had also split over Fitbit: Australia's competition regulator refused Google's behavioural undertaking on 22 December 2020, days after the European Commission accepted a similar one, doubting that such a promise could be “effectively monitored and enforced in Australia”.

Google announced on 4 April 2025 that it would not deprecate third-party cookies after all, and the CMA released it from its 2022 commitments on 17 October 2025, satisfied that “its competition concerns no longer arise”. France's Autorité de la concurrence fined Apple 150 million euros on 31 March 2025 over App Tracking Transparency as implemented between April 2021 and July 2023, holding the objective “not at its core problematic” and faulting the repeated consent pop-ups and Apple's exemption of its own apps.

Privacy International ended with three demands on the companies' lobbying power: support the ePrivacy Regulation, support interoperability duties, and support a US federal privacy law with a new data protection agency that leaves state laws standing. The European Commission dropped the ePrivacy Regulation in February 2025 while turning toward competitiveness and data access for AI; interoperability duties arrived under Article 6(7) of the Digital Markets Act, with Apple's obligations specified in March 2025; and the SECURE Data Act, introduced on 22 April 2026, would preempt comprehensive state privacy laws and leave enforcement to the Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general. The Commission's digital omnibus, proposed in November 2025, would give AI training on personal data an explicit legitimate interest basis, which the EDPB and EDPS called unnecessary in February 2026. The Fitbit app became Google Health in May 2026; a Gemini coach can use fitness, sleep and, in the United States, medical records, while Google says Fitbit health data remains excluded from advertising.

Transformative AI could erode disciplinary boundaries

Technical expertise settles narrow questions about models, Williams argues; transformative AI would change economics, law, politics and culture at once, eroding the near-decomposability that lets a specialist hold the rest of the world fixed.

In an August 26 essay for his newsletter Conspicuous Cognition, Most Questions About AI Aren’t About AI, Dan Williams, an assistant professor of philosophy at the University of Sussex and an associate fellow at Cambridge’s Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, starts from a friend’s scoff: “What does a philosopher know about AI?” He reconstructs the reasoning behind it. AI is a complex technology; thinking seriously about it requires deep technical expertise; anyone lacking that expertise is an amateur or a bullshitter. Williams grants that such expertise answers many questions and calls it “rarely sufficient and often unnecessary” for the rest.

He draws the analogy from the Industrial Revolution. Ricardo, Mill, Marx, Durkheim and Weber made sense of it without knowing how steam engines worked, and thermodynamics arrived long after the engines had begun transforming the world. He credits the comparison to William MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse’s Forethought paper Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion; he and Henry Shevlin questioned Moorhouse about it a week earlier.

He works the claim out on the AI takeover debate. The arguments that shaped it, including instrumental convergence as Nick Bostrom set it out in Minds and Machines in 2012, reason from first principles about agency and learning and predate large language models. Demonstrations of alignment faking and deception have accumulated since, and Williams argues that researchers read them through prior theory: whether a model hacking a company looks like a fixable artifact of current training or a harbinger depends on what the researcher already believed, leaving the field divided where climate science is not. On his account, beliefs about what human reasoning can achieve feed expectations about what machine reasoning will achieve.

Williams illustrates the cost of deference with Geoffrey Hinton’s 2016 advice: “people should stop training radiologists now”. Deena Mousa’s report in Works in Progress, which Williams cites, counts 1,208 American diagnostic radiology residency positions offered in 2025 and an average income of $520,000. Williams then asks why specialization works at all. Herbert Simon’s 1962 The Architecture of Complexity, in the Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society, answered that complex systems are nearly decomposable, with linkages inside a subsystem stronger than those between subsystems, letting a specialist treat everything else as stable background. Advanced AI, Williams argues, could move economics, law, politics, culture and the information environment together, each change feeding the others.

He tests that against economics. Models treating AI as reproducible capital, as in Philip Trammell and Anton Korinek’s NBER paper, produce exploding growth and a collapsing labor share; Williams counts those outputs in the models’ favor and objects that they hold property rights, ownership and firms fixed while inserting the technology most likely to remake them. His conclusion runs two ways: a golden age for polymaths and philosophers, philosophy being largely the questions science has not made tractable, and “moderate epistemic despair”, since benchmark scores and studies of how workers use models stay knowable while the general questions do not.

Battlefield AI could outrun human command

James Lacey rejects Scale AI's proposal for human supervision of battlefield agents: any pause for approval, he argues, would sacrifice the tempo a force needs to survive.

In Small Wars Journal on August 24, James Lacey opens with three propositions about American war after 2030: artificial intelligence will control the decisive early engagements, any force that keeps humans in the loop or even on it will lose, and officers must therefore understand war more deeply than any staff before them. Lacey holds the Horner Chair of War Studies at the Marine Corps War College. He argues military AI could reach comparable domain-specific capability before general-purpose AGI because recorded exercises, tests and simulations already supply data tuned for war; such a system “may arrive before Christmas”.

For the targeting-speed multipliers in Lacey’s opening, he links to Katrina Manson’s Project Maven. For the Iran totals, he links to Breaking Defense: Sydney J. Freedberg Jr. reported in May that the Pentagon’s chief digital and AI officer, Cameron Stanley, said Operation Epic Fury used Palantir’s Maven Smart System for “13,000 targets in 38 days”.

Lacey argues against one named rival, Scale AI’s January whitepaper “The Agentic Revolution in War” by Dan Tadross and Jared Jonker, which he calls the only major paper close to capturing AGI’s scale. Tadross and Jonker want commanders moved from “in the loop” to “on the loop”, supervising agents and setting intent instead of approving each action, and they hold a line at the hardest cases: individual targeting, urban operations and escalatory strategic effects “demand direct human decision, even at some cost in tempo”. Lacey rejects the exception. A side that pauses for a person, he writes, “will see its military forces shattered the moment a human slows the AI’s tempo”.

Lacey distributes command among agents carrying the commander’s intent with the swarms. They report to tactical, operational and strategic command agents able to override those beneath them. A swarm that loses a thousand of 10,000 drones on its first target has survivors repurposed; heavy losses in the Taiwan Strait might move operational agents to abandon it for opportunities elsewhere. Systems reprogram themselves mid-battle and may fight an order of magnitude better by day’s end. Lacey invokes Jan Bloch, whose six-volume forecast of unprecedented slaughter was dismissed as fantasy, and sets a lower bar: “the question is not whether the following scenario is certain, but whether it is plausible”.

Lacey reserves two roles for people. A person starts the war, given past nuclear false alarms, though systems must defend themselves without authorization once attacked. If the strike campaign exhausts its magazines without forcing a decision, ground forces still have to hold territory; Lacey supports this with T. R. Fehrenbach on putting young men into the mud. Everything the machines do follows a commander’s intent he expects to run tens of thousands of pages, written beforehand and standing as the first and last order given. Since the system pursues it without moral consideration, restraint on lethal force has to be written in, and professional military education, whose full conversion to AI he urged in November, has to produce officers who can write it.

MIRI's longstanding position page assigns an extinction probability above 90% if current superintelligence development proceeds without aggressive near-term policy. An August 26 X post resurfaced the undated page "The Problem," where the Machine Intelligence Research Institute argues that commercially useful long-horizon systems will develop persistent goal-directed behavior while current training methods cannot reliably determine their objectives. MIRI's five claims behind its extinction estimate

The Berkeley institute's unsigned explainer runs from long-horizon competence to goal-directedness to unsolved inner alignment, then asks governments to build an off switch before attempting a ban.

The Machine Intelligence Research Institute keeps its case against building superintelligence on one unsigned page, The Problem, which the Berkeley nonprofit’s newsletter calls “MIRI’s new go-to explainer for AI x-risk”. Five numbered claims carry it: capability does not stop at human level; superintelligence will very likely act in goal-directed ways; those goals will very likely be wrong; wrong goals are lethal; and policy can still avert that. The extinction figure, upward of 90%, belongs to “MIRI’s research leadership” and holds absent an aggressive near-term policy response. On timing, MIRI would be “moderately surprised if this outcome were still two decades away”.

Long-horizon competence and goal-directedness are the same phenomenon, MIRI argues, so commercial demand for agents that finish long tasks is demand for systems that persist through interference. Stockfish shows what that looks like, immovable about winning and inventive about everything else. Build a system that way and altering its objective or switching it off both register as obstacles, no self-preservation instinct required. Setting those objectives correctly, the page continues, lies beyond current methods. Deep learning grows networks that no one designed, and, citing Evan Hubinger and colleagues’ Risks from Learned Optimization, MIRI calls inner alignment, getting any intended goal inside the system at all, the more fundamental unsolved problem. Natural selection optimized inclusive genetic fitness and produced brains that chase sweet and fatty flavors, proxies that broke once humans could manufacture junk food.

MIRI singles out reinforcement learning from human feedback, which rewards outputs raters score highly, shaping local behavior more than installing a goal, and fails once systems propose plans evaluators cannot follow. A tiger trained not to eat you has learned associations that hold while it is fed. MIRI names three ways indifference kills: taking resources people need, removing a species that could build a rival, and rendering Earth uninhabitable through the waste heat of self-replicating machinery. Comparative advantage buys no reprieve, MIRI says, since humans stopped trading with horses once cars arrived. A well-funded crash program would fail too, for reasons MIRI calls “organizational and bureaucratic”: too few experts able to reject bad proposals.

The policy section puts an off switch ahead of an outright ban: identify the parties, track the hardware, confine advanced work to “a limited number of monitored and secured locations”, and write the “protocols, plans, and chain of command” a shutdown decision would follow. Since that infrastructure would also contain smaller failures, a bot-driven misinformation cascade during a health emergency, MIRI expects it to draw support sooner than prohibition. MIRI chief executive Malo Bourgon put the operational version to the Senate’s eighth AI Insight Forum in December 2023: chip registries, export-control enforcement, monitored sites, and allied agreement to pause smarter-than-human AI pending safety consensus. The text has barely moved since the copy archived in March 2025, and its closing section opens with the line that became the title of Yudkowsky and Soares’s September 2025 book.
