Transformative AI could erode disciplinary boundaries

Technical expertise settles narrow questions about models, Williams argues; transformative AI would change economics, law, politics and culture at once, eroding the near-decomposability that lets a specialist hold the rest of the world fixed.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 August 2026 496 words · ~2 min

In an August 26 essay for his newsletter Conspicuous Cognition, Most Questions About AI Aren’t About AI, Dan Williams, an assistant professor of philosophy at the University of Sussex and an associate fellow at Cambridge’s Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, starts from a friend’s scoff: “What does a philosopher know about AI?” He reconstructs the reasoning behind it. AI is a complex technology; thinking seriously about it requires deep technical expertise; anyone lacking that expertise is an amateur or a bullshitter. Williams grants that such expertise answers many questions and calls it “rarely sufficient and often unnecessary” for the rest.

He draws the analogy from the Industrial Revolution. Ricardo, Mill, Marx, Durkheim and Weber made sense of it without knowing how steam engines worked, and thermodynamics arrived long after the engines had begun transforming the world. He credits the comparison to William MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse’s Forethought paper Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion; he and Henry Shevlin questioned Moorhouse about it a week earlier.

He works the claim out on the AI takeover debate. The arguments that shaped it, including instrumental convergence as Nick Bostrom set it out in Minds and Machines in 2012, reason from first principles about agency and learning and predate large language models. Demonstrations of alignment faking and deception have accumulated since, and Williams argues that researchers read them through prior theory: whether a model hacking a company looks like a fixable artifact of current training or a harbinger depends on what the researcher already believed, leaving the field divided where climate science is not. On his account, beliefs about what human reasoning can achieve feed expectations about what machine reasoning will achieve.

Williams illustrates the cost of deference with Geoffrey Hinton’s 2016 advice: “people should stop training radiologists now”. Deena Mousa’s report in Works in Progress, which Williams cites, counts 1,208 American diagnostic radiology residency positions offered in 2025 and an average income of $520,000. Williams then asks why specialization works at all. Herbert Simon’s 1962 The Architecture of Complexity, in the Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society, answered that complex systems are nearly decomposable, with linkages inside a subsystem stronger than those between subsystems, letting a specialist treat everything else as stable background. Advanced AI, Williams argues, could move economics, law, politics, culture and the information environment together, each change feeding the others.

He tests that against economics. Models treating AI as reproducible capital, as in Philip Trammell and Anton Korinek’s NBER paper, produce exploding growth and a collapsing labor share; Williams counts those outputs in the models’ favor and objects that they hold property rights, ownership and firms fixed while inserting the technology most likely to remake them. His conclusion runs two ways: a golden age for polymaths and philosophers, philosophy being largely the questions science has not made tractable, and “moderate epistemic despair”, since benchmark scores and studies of how workers use models stay knowable while the general questions do not.
