MIRI's five claims behind its extinction estimate

The Berkeley institute's unsigned explainer runs from long-horizon competence to goal-directedness to unsolved inner alignment, then asks governments to build an off switch before attempting a ban.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 August 2026 487 words · ~2 min

The Machine Intelligence Research Institute keeps its case against building superintelligence on one unsigned page, The Problem, which the Berkeley nonprofit’s newsletter calls “MIRI’s new go-to explainer for AI x-risk”. Five numbered claims carry it: capability does not stop at human level; superintelligence will very likely act in goal-directed ways; those goals will very likely be wrong; wrong goals are lethal; and policy can still avert that. The extinction figure, upward of 90%, belongs to “MIRI’s research leadership” and holds absent an aggressive near-term policy response. On timing, MIRI would be “moderately surprised if this outcome were still two decades away”.

Long-horizon competence and goal-directedness are the same phenomenon, MIRI argues, so commercial demand for agents that finish long tasks is demand for systems that persist through interference. Stockfish shows what that looks like, immovable about winning and inventive about everything else. Build a system that way and altering its objective or switching it off both register as obstacles, no self-preservation instinct required. Setting those objectives correctly, the page continues, lies beyond current methods. Deep learning grows networks that no one designed, and, citing Evan Hubinger and colleagues’ Risks from Learned Optimization, MIRI calls inner alignment, getting any intended goal inside the system at all, the more fundamental unsolved problem. Natural selection optimized inclusive genetic fitness and produced brains that chase sweet and fatty flavors, proxies that broke once humans could manufacture junk food.

MIRI singles out reinforcement learning from human feedback, which rewards outputs raters score highly, shaping local behavior more than installing a goal, and fails once systems propose plans evaluators cannot follow. A tiger trained not to eat you has learned associations that hold while it is fed. MIRI names three ways indifference kills: taking resources people need, removing a species that could build a rival, and rendering Earth uninhabitable through the waste heat of self-replicating machinery. Comparative advantage buys no reprieve, MIRI says, since humans stopped trading with horses once cars arrived. A well-funded crash program would fail too, for reasons MIRI calls “organizational and bureaucratic”: too few experts able to reject bad proposals.

The policy section puts an off switch ahead of an outright ban: identify the parties, track the hardware, confine advanced work to “a limited number of monitored and secured locations”, and write the “protocols, plans, and chain of command” a shutdown decision would follow. Since that infrastructure would also contain smaller failures, a bot-driven misinformation cascade during a health emergency, MIRI expects it to draw support sooner than prohibition. MIRI chief executive Malo Bourgon put the operational version to the Senate’s eighth AI Insight Forum in December 2023: chip registries, export-control enforcement, monitored sites, and allied agreement to pause smarter-than-human AI pending safety consensus. The text has barely moved since the copy archived in March 2025, and its closing section opens with the line that became the title of Yudkowsky and Soares’s September 2025 book.
