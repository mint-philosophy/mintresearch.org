A specialist image model beats frontier LLMs on selected skin lesions

Magnus Lynch’s 200-case comparison finds better diagnosis accuracy from fine-tuned Inception v3. Discharge results depend on the threshold, and the test does not establish clinical safety.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 25 October 2026 768 words · ~4 min

A small image classifier trained on dermatology photographs identified skin lesions more accurately than two frontier LLMs in a selected 200-case test, Magnus Lynch reports in “A 10-year-old CNN outperforms frontier LLMs for skin cancer diagnosis”, published on Lynch Lab on October 25. Lynch, a consultant dermatologist at Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust, compared a fine-tuned Inception v3 with GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1. The classifier gave the correct first-choice diagnosis for 125 of 200 lesions, about 62%, against 84 and 85 for the LLMs, about 42%. Its decisions about discharging lesions depended strongly on the threshold used.

Lynch selected the cases from MILK10k, a public collection pairing ordinary close-up photographs with dermoscopic images taken through a skin-imaging instrument. His test deliberately contained 100 lesions he classified as needing further care and 100 classified as safe to discharge. The former included 30 melanomas, other skin cancers and actinic keratoses; the latter were moles and seborrhoeic keratoses, benign skin growths. Every selected lesion had a biopsy-confirmed diagnosis, including the benign cases. Lynch notes that these benign lesions had worried clinicians enough to warrant biopsy, making the selection harder than an ordinary mix of skin concerns.

The model card describes Inception v3, a convolutional neural network (CNN) published in 2015, as a research baseline, pretrained on general photographs and then fine-tuned on 68,011 clinical and dermoscopic images from the ISIC Archive. Lynch says all images of the test lesions and patients were excluded from training and validation. For this comparison, the CNN combined scores from both views, weighting the dermoscopic view more heavily, and chose among six diagnoses. The LLMs saw the same image pairs without patient history, age or body site. Each received the same prompt once at default settings, requesting a diagnosis, second choice, confidence and discharge decision. Discharge meant no treatment, biopsy or follow-up.

Lynch illustrates the difference between diagnosis and discharge with the models’ errors. GPT-6 Astra often labelled melanomas as basal cell carcinoma, another cancer, while many of the CNN’s melanoma errors assigned benign diagnoses. A wrong cancer label can still lead a model to recommend further care. At the CNN’s preset research threshold, it recommended discharge for 76 of the 100 benign lesions but also 18 of the 30 melanomas. GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 recommended discharge for only 32 and 22 benign lesions, alongside three and four melanomas. They would therefore send many benign cases for further assessment.

Lynch’s table also reports CNN results at thresholds labelled “matched”: 98 or 99 of the 100 lesions needing care were retained, and only one or two melanomas were discharged. The post does not define the matching criterion or report how many benign lesions were discharged at those settings. Those omissions prevent a comparison at an established equal rate of benign discharge. The model card’s preset thresholds were chosen on validation images to catch 90% of cancers, or cancers plus actinic keratoses, with separate cutoffs for each image type. It explicitly describes them as research settings. Its separate figures for dermoscopy-only and clinical-only inputs are different evaluations from the paired-image comparison.

Lynch’s October 11 article had already reported the LLM-only test on these cases; the October 25 post adds the specialist classifier. The earlier article also describes three additional runs on its opening melanoma example. Both LLMs repeatedly gave the same wrong benign diagnosis, but their discharge decisions changed across runs. That small check illustrates why the main comparison’s single response per case cannot establish how reliably a model would repeat its decision. The one-case difference between the LLMs’ diagnosis totals offers little basis for ranking them.

The released cases, prompts and replies reproduce the LLM totals when scored against the supplied biopsy labels. They contain no CNN predictions, so the classifier’s results and threshold comparison remain figures reported in the article. Lynch says he designed the test and made the clinical rulings; Claude Code assisted with analysis and drafting, and scripts scored answers against the diagnoses.

Lynch’s exclusions protect against reusing the same patients and lesions in CNN training, but this remains an evaluation of selected research images. The model card lists MILK study contributors among its training sources and reports few images of darker skin. Holding out individual patients and lesions does not establish performance in a new clinical population. The deliberately balanced case mix, biopsy selection and limited number of melanomas also constrain interpretation. This comparison shows how the tested models classified these images and how their discharge choices differed; it does not establish that any of them can safely discharge patients in prospective clinical use.
