Anthropic could publish IPO filing Monday, with cash flow and control unresolved

Nick Wingfield previews the disclosures he wants from a filing that could appear October 26: cash flow, cloud-partner relationships, revenue sources, control and shareholder sales.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 25 October 2026 784 words · ~4 min

Nick Wingfield writes in The Information’s October 25 newsletter that Anthropic could make its IPO registration statement public as early as Monday, October 26. Weeks of reporting from draft filings and investor meetings have already disclosed substantial financial and governance details. Wingfield asks colleagues Jason Dean and Laura Mandaro what they still want to learn: how Anthropic’s growth translates into cash, how its relationships with cloud companies work, and who would exercise control after the listing.

Wingfield begins with cash flow and the path to profitability. Anissa Gardizy’s October 20 Wall Street Journal report, based on a draft prospectus, put first-half 2026 revenue at $16.3 billion, up from $1.1 billion a year earlier. Its $80.7 billion net loss included $72 billion in accounting charges from changes in the value of convertible notes and other financing instruments. We covered the reported loss and computing obligations on October 21. That net-loss figure alone does not establish how much cash the business consumed; Wingfield wants cash-flow figures and operating measures that explain its financial trajectory.

The Information also wants to connect Anthropic’s spending with the investments made by its largest partners. Google and Amazon supply computing capacity and hold stakes in the company; Amazon also resells its models. Dean and Mandaro want to know how much Anthropic pays each to run its AI, how much each has invested, and how much those stakes have appreciated. Those questions distinguish payments for computing from investment returns. The value of an equity stake, a commitment to purchase capacity over several years and a payment made during a reporting period describe different parts of the relationship.

Wingfield’s revenue questions concern both customers and sales channels. The Journal reported that two customers supplied about one-fifth of first-half 2026 revenue. Wingfield wants more recent concentration figures and a breakdown of how customers buy Claude. He says API sales, through which developers put models into their own products, have historically supplied most revenue and can fluctuate as developers change their preferred model. Claude subscriptions and sales through resellers such as Amazon, which takes a share of revenue, warrant separate attention. Customer concentration identifies dependence on particular buyers; channel figures describe how sales reach the company.

Reuters’ September account of the confidential prospectus, which we covered at the time, already described the proposed founder voting majority. A majority of the seven co-founders would direct a single Class F share through a Founder LLC, carrying 50.1% of voting power over key corporate matters. Class F and Class A holders would elect Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei and a third director; the Long-Term Benefit Trust would elect four directors. The reported arrangement therefore combines founder control of shareholder votes with a separate body’s power to choose most of the board.

Anthropic’s 2023 explanation of the Trust describes why it created that separate authority. The company wanted directors to consider people affected by AI who have no financial stake or contractual relationship with Anthropic. A special Class T share gives the Trust director-election and removal powers, with its board representation increasing over time. Anthropic also described provisions allowing sufficiently large shareholder supermajorities to change the Trust without trustee consent. These are earlier features of its governance experiment; the IPO would bring them alongside the proposed founder voting arrangements.

Wingfield asks how the different sources of authority would work together. He wants to know whether Dario Amodei has special power over the other founders, and how disagreements between directors appointed by the Trust and those appointed by founders would be resolved. He also reports that Anthropic has planned an employee share class for tie-breaker votes. The existence of that proposal leaves questions about which decisions employees could influence and when their votes would count.

New York City Comptroller Mark Levine raised a related accountability concern in his October 14 letter to Amodei. Citing reports of concentrated founder and trustee control, he urged Anthropic to pause its IPO until it addressed safety and governance concerns. He asked for specific audits and controls, their intended scope and an implementation timetable. His request concerns the safeguards accompanying control, beyond the allocation of voting rights.

Wingfield closes with the terms under which existing shareholders could sell. The Information has previously reported that Anthropic considered extending lockups, the period when holders are barred from selling, beyond the usual 180 days while allowing selected shareholders to sell at the IPO. Such secondary sales would let existing holders receive proceeds at listing. Wingfield contrasts the proposal with this year’s SpaceX and Cerebras IPOs, which he says allowed no secondary sales at listing. He wants the filing to specify how long most investors must wait and which investors or employees could sell sooner.
