China links some AI project reviews to employment risk

The October 23 opinion also calls for cautious adoption in labor-intensive industries and encourages employers to explore advance notice and transition support.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 25 October 2026 720 words · ~4 min

China's State Council General Office has directed officials to assess employment risks when reviewing certain AI application projects and to manage the pace of AI adoption in labor-intensive industries. Its opinion on AI and employment was signed on October 16 and published on October 23 with State Council approval. Xinhua's English account summarizes 18 tasks covering job creation, training, worker protection and monitoring. The Chinese document gives more specific directions to provincial governments, ministries and other State Council agencies.

Point 6 prioritizes AI applications in jobs where labor is scarce or conditions are hazardous. It asks officials to take a cautious approach to the scope, intensity and pace of adoption in labor-intensive industries, to reduce disruption to workers. It also encourages companies to explore an unemployment buffer period during large-scale AI deployment, combining advance notice to affected workers with help moving to other jobs. The text presents this as something employers should explore; it sets no compulsory notice period.

Point 7 connects assessment to project review. When an AI application project in a field subject to market-access rules could cause substantial employment disruption, the opinion calls for an employment-risk assessment during project review. Relevant departments are to coordinate support for affected workers' transitions. For projects in other fields, it calls for assessments suited to the circumstances. Point 7 does not identify the sectors covered by this review-stage provision, define how much disruption triggers it, or specify the assessment procedure.

For workers displaced by AI, the document directs agencies to provide eligible people with vocational guidance, training, job recommendations and support for starting businesses. Insured unemployed workers are to receive benefits promptly and in full under existing rules. The worker-rights provisions also ask authorities to regulate AI use in human-resources management and act against algorithm-enabled hiring discrimination, fraudulent recruitment and violations of privacy and rest rights. Employers are to uphold existing rights to pay, leave and occupational health and safety as work increasingly involves collaboration with machines.

The social-security measures encourage flexible workers and people in new forms of employment to enroll in the urban employee basic pension scheme. They also call for expanding the occupational-injury protection pilot for workers in new forms of employment. A separate pay provision asks companies to develop remuneration systems suited to AI-sector workers and recognize the value of knowledge and skills. These measures refer to existing insurance arrangements and wage guidance; the opinion specifies no new benefit amount or compensation payment for AI displacement.

Alongside these protections, the government wants AI to generate employment. It encourages eastern regions to develop core AI industries and central and western regions to accommodate activities such as data labelling and smart-terminal manufacturing. Qualifying firms can receive support through existing job-stabilizing and job-expanding loan programs, while government investment funds are to support AI businesses on market terms. Training provisions encourage participation by graduates, migrant workers and unemployed people, closer collaboration between schools and companies, and more public AI courses on national learning platforms.

Officials are also to strengthen the information used to assess displacement. The document calls for sharing industry and employment data across departments, developing regional and sector monitoring models, and conducting a special survey that follows AI adoption, jobs created or replaced, and changes in demand for skills. Universities and research institutes would host AI employment research centers using existing resources. Point 18 brings AI employment risks within the mechanism for preventing large-scale unemployment, with contingency planning and joint responses to labor-relations risks.

Several commitments precede this document. The State Council's August 2025 AI+ opinion already called for AI employment-risk assessment, training, and deployment in jobs with labor shortages or hazardous conditions. Its June 2026 employment-first plan called for employment-impact assessments of major policies and projects and improved early warning and responses to AI-related employment risks. The October opinion adds more specific instructions on pacing adoption in labor-intensive industries, exploring a transition period, and assessing employment effects during certain project reviews.

Human resources and social security departments and development and reform departments are to coordinate implementation at each level of government. Local authorities and relevant departments are told to adapt implementation to their circumstances. The opinion's 18 tasks contain no numerical targets, implementation deadlines, spending totals or specific penalties. The transition-period proposal and project assessments therefore still leave practical details for employers and the responsible departments to determine.
