Yesterday in AI · 8 October 2026

Trahan would make AI developers pay for harm; Cantwell would require audits before release

A progressive coalition rejects preemption of state AI laws, which Adam Thierer says any deal requires. British proposals, Taiwan’s unused deepfake takedown rule and Daniel Solove’s essay on mental privacy raise questions of testing access, cross-border reach and rights.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 8 October 2026 1492 words · ~7 min

Representative Lori Trahan’s CLAIM Act, released as a discussion draft on October 7, would make AI developers pay for certain foreseeable harms their systems cause to non-users, however careful they had been. Semafor’s Nicholas Wu reported that the Massachusetts Democrat is rolling it out in direct response to this year’s rogue AI hack of Hugging Face, and that she presents liability as an incentive for safety. “When someone breaks the law and hurts you, you can take them to court,” Trahan said in her announcement.

Under the six-page bill text, the company that trained a system would be liable “regardless of the degree of care exercised” for foreseeable injuries to non-users. Except for defamation, liability would attach when the system’s conduct would amount to negligence, an intentional tort or a crime if an adult of sound mind had done it. This rule would not apply if the user, or an intermediary that fine-tuned or otherwise modified the system, intended the conduct or was negligent. Where a claim requires intent, courts would presume the system had the mental state a person acting the same way would have had, and, overriding the usual federal evidence rule, the developer would have to persuade jurors otherwise. Only non-users could sue, in federal or state court; users harmed by systems they use would gain no new claim. The draft preserves all state law and would expire after five years.

Trahan’s office argues in a background paper that AI risk is hard to measure in advance while AI harms are “relatively easy to attribute ex post,” and that liability “mechanically scales with the risk.” Current law leaves victims without a remedy, the paper says, because negligence requires a specific precaution the company skipped and product liability a safer design, which may not exist when nobody knows how to make a capability safe. The office also asks stakeholders how prevention and liability should combine for catastrophic risks “which may cause harms too large to compensate after the fact.”

CLAIM closely follows a Rhode Island bill its documents do not mention: S0358, sponsored in 2025 by state Senator Victoria Gu, used the same sound-mind test and intent presumption but covered only frontier-scale models and offered developers two defenses. Inside AI Policy reports that Gabriel Weil, a University of Houston law professor who has proposed liability without fault for physical harms from frontier systems, helped write CLAIM and is weighing a change for open-weight models. On X, Bahrad Sokhansanj found the presumption appealing for compensation but warned that “a standard that is too presumptive of agentic intent” could discourage companies from offering flexible or unmonitored agents.

Semafor’s Ashley Gold separately reported a statement of principles that Guardrails Action and 38 other groups sent to Congress. The letter endorses no bill. Its signers want government oversight with real enforcement power “to determine the pace and substance of new AI models”; no AI making final decisions to deny health care or benefits, fire or discipline workers, or deploy weapons; enforcement of existing consumer-protection and computer-fraud laws; and no federal preemption of state AI legislation. Bills that fall short, they warn, “will be met with active, mobilized opposition.” Guardrails Action’s affiliated super PAC announced $1.2 million in ads that day against three candidates backed by the AI-industry super PAC Leading the Future, The Hill reported.

Senator Maria Cantwell of Washington, the ranking Democrat on the Senate Commerce Committee, released her own six-principle framework that day. It would act before a model’s release, where CLAIM would act after an injury. NIST would set catastrophic-risk standards, audit firms would examine developers “akin to outside auditors examining a company’s financial statements,” and no covered model could be released until an independent audit confirmed compliance. Developers would give testers access, report incidents promptly and protect whistleblowers. They would remain liable under existing law where a failure to implement reasonable safeguards contributed to harm, a standard closer to fault than CLAIM’s. Like the coalition, Cantwell wants human oversight of consequential decisions, citing Medicare’s AI-assisted WISeR prior-authorization model, and a right to review by a qualified human. The framework has no legislative text, Roll Call notes, and The Seattle Times reports that its penalties remain unresolved.

Cantwell’s framework is also silent on whether federal rules would override state laws. Adam Thierer, a resident senior fellow at the R Street Institute, argued on X that “there will be no compromise or effective national AI governance framework” without preemption of state frontier-model rules, a post Shakeel Hashim quoted on October 8. The social-harm section would “derail the entire effort,” he wrote, and he wanted it and the global-cooperation section handled separately so that a “skinny” frontier oversight and transparency bill could pass. Cantwell’s policy record, released alongside the framework, recalls her 2025 position that Congress should not preempt state AI laws “without a strong federal standard.” CLAIM would set a federal floor and the coalition rejects preemption, while the FRONTIER Act, which Trahan introduced with Republican Jay Obernolte and pairs with CLAIM, would bar new state obligations on developers for frontier-risk transparency, audits and incident reporting. Experts in a Forecasting Research Institute survey gave preemption little support but rated it the likeliest policy to pass.

Evie Breese’s reporting for Tech Policy Press and openDemocracy on Britain’s competing options draws on a September 14 report by the Joint Committee on Human Rights, which Labour MP Alex Sobel chairs. The committee found that, outside very limited circumstances, regulators “lack the power to test and evaluate AI systems before they are publicly released,” and that the AI Security Institute (AISI) has no statutory power. Like Trahan, it cites the Hugging Face incident, but it concludes that AI’s human rights risks “cannot be adequately addressed by relying on individual litigants,” the remedy CLAIM provides. It recommends a statutory AISI to which developers must submit new models, and an oversight body able to order remedies and to limit UK deployment by foreign developers posing unacceptable risks. Anthropic delayed AISI’s access to Claude Mythos 5.1 while U.S. organizations tested it, Breese reports, and Google gave its newest model to the U.S. government first.

Sobel’s Artificial Superintelligence Bill has no published text. Breese reports that it would make developing artificial superintelligence (ASI) a crime, monitor “designated ASI development indicators” such as self-preservation, and let the Secretary of State seize the compute involved. Sobel told her the bill cannot stop development in the U.S. or China, so a treaty is its overarching goal. Nuala Polo of the Ada Lovelace Institute calls such a ban “largely symbolic” for that reason. The government appears to favor a narrow national-security bill, which Polo says “targets only the risks industry itself is prepared to regulate”; Ada wants a comprehensive bill with mandatory pre-deployment testing and liability reaching developers and the platforms that host them. The British argument over scope resembles the American one between Thierer’s skinny bill and Cantwell’s broader principles. Polo also notes that the Online Safety Act’s offense test turns on criminal intent, which chatbots “cannot form,” the same gap CLAIM’s presumption targets.

In an October 1 essay for Tech Policy Press, Eddy Yen-Ting Lin examines Taiwan’s Article 51-3, which lets candidates who obtain a police forensic finding require platforms to remove a verified deepfake within two days, but only from the official election notice to election eve. The Central Election Commission told Lin it had received zero requests as of May 2026, which its advisory group’s May 29 minutes confirm. The count covers this mechanism only; in September the Liberty Times reported a candidate’s police complaint over an alleged deepfake. Lin blames the short window, when foreign influence runs year-round; police findings on deception or satire that candidates cannot appeal; and offshore platforms that candidates must serve themselves. He proposes judicial oversight at the decision point, to allow appeals and keep the executive from suppressing speech, plus year-round coverage and a domestic legal representative for foreign platforms, as the EU’s Digital Services Act requires of services offered in the EU by providers without an EU establishment. The British committee’s proposed foreign-deployment power is aimed at a similar problem.

In “Rethinking Privacy of the Mind,” an October 6 essay republished on his Substack on October 8, Daniel J. Solove of George Washington University Law School argues that laws protecting neural data alone are insufficient to secure privacy of the mind, which he defines as protection against invasive probing and manipulation. Heart rate, eye movements and ordinary behavior also support inferences about mental states, he writes, and most manipulation uses no neural data at all. Sensitivity turns mainly on “the use of the data,” so the law must regulate the collection and processing of personal data broadly, as his 2024 article “Data Is What Data Does” argued for sensitive data generally. The EU AI Act already regulates by use, banning AI systems that infer emotions in workplaces and schools, with medical and safety exceptions.
