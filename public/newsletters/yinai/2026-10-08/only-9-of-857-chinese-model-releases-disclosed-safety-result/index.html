Nine of 857 Chinese model releases carried safety results at launch

SemiAnalysis’s release census and two studies of public statements find sparse disclosure and limited demands on frontier developers. Primary documents qualify its DeepSeek counts and its account of geopolitical motives.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 8 October 2026 1429 words · ~7 min

Only nine of 857 Chinese model releases had developer safety results available at launch, Mark Chen, Doug and Dylan Patel report in SemiAnalysis’s October 8 Beijing Will Not Pace the Frontier: China’s Speed-First AI Safety Regime. Their three datasets examine what developers disclose, what their leaders say and how experts influence regulation. They respond to the debate over Dario Amodei’s September 12 pacing proposal, covered here last month: Trump invokes China as a reason to accelerate, while Amodei argues that preserving America’s lead could create room to slow down.

The census covers identifiable releases from 2021 through September 15, 2026 by four large cloud and internet companies (ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu) and five startups (DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax, StepFun and Zhipu, now Z.ai). The authors searched model cards, release notes and technical reports for numerical results or substantive findings on harmful outputs, jailbreaks, privacy, refusals or dangerous capabilities. Generic assurances of safety training did not qualify, and a flagship’s results did not carry over to other sizes or snapshots. Failure to find a disclosure establishes nothing about unpublished testing.

They counted 31 releases with developer results: nine at launch, 16 later and six whose timing or exact model match remained uncertain. Ten more carried evaluation claims without results, three had press or investor accounts alone, and 813 had no disclosure. Those categories exhaust the 857 releases. Among the 16 late disclosures, the reported median lag was 42 days. Quarterly releases rose from three in early 2023 to 101 in the third quarter of 2025; releases with safety results never exceeded seven a quarter, and nine of fifteen quarters had none available at launch. The authors see no corresponding increase around the policy milestones they mark.

Within the census, no developer disclosed routinely. Alibaba’s 238 releases included seven with results, three at launch. Because companies name variants differently, those counts are not a comparable ranking. Startups disclosed results for 6.3% of releases against the large companies’ 2%; Zhipu alone had a result in every year since 2022. Among 2026 frontier releases covered before the September 15 cutoff, including DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2.5 through K3 and Qwen3.8-Max, only GLM-5.3 launched with an evaluation, a cyber-capability note.

Three qualifying documents touched cyber-offense or biological risk: Zhipu’s GLM-5.2 and GLM-5.3 notes and Moonshot’s Kimi K2 report. None came from the four large companies. The authors found no release with dangerous-capability testing across the domains named by the International Dialogues on AI Safety. Z.ai’s August 14 GLM-5.3 note reported cyber-benchmark gains without refusal or misuse rates. Third-party testing sits outside this developer-disclosure count. In Anthropic’s September 29 assessment, covered here that day, techniques ranging from deceptive prompts to edited weights bypassed GLM-5.3’s safeguards in 64% to 100% of simulated trials.

The census dates DeepSeek-R1’s safety appendix to January 2026, yielding its maximum lag of 349 days. But the Nature paper published September 17, 2025 already included a supplementary safety report, 240 days after R1’s January 20 release. The supplement distinguishes R1 running alone from the official service’s additional risk-control system, reporting results with and without those protections. It also tests multilingual safety and resistance to jailbreaks. Its disclosure therefore contains both measured outcomes and information about which safeguards produced them. January 2026 instead matches the arXiv revision. For V3, counted as an at-launch behavioral safety test, the safety score in the original technical report measures its ability to judge answers on RewardBench. The census does not identify which V3 document it counted. The other three at-launch behavioral tests, for Qwen2-72B-Instruct, MiniMax-Text-01 and Seed-OSS-36B-Instruct, have corresponding developer results.

Chen and colleagues argue that China recognizes frontier risks without requiring controls on frontier development. TC260’s September 14 AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0 describes deception and shutdown evasion but prioritizes innovation. Our September coverage examined which recommendations still needed implementation. SemiAnalysis says binding rules govern outputs and applications without compute- or capability-triggered frontier duties. Content-labeling and AI-companion rules are examples; the authors argue that complying with them need not involve testing dangerous model capabilities. Under the EU AI Act, general-purpose models trained with more than 10²⁵ floating-point operations face a rebuttable systemic-risk presumption; other models can also be designated. Providers of systemic-risk models must evaluate and mitigate those risks, report serious incidents and maintain adequate cybersecurity. California’s SB 53 defines frontier foundation models by training above 10²⁶ integer or floating-point operations, including later modifications. Framework duties additionally require the developer and its affiliates to have combined revenue above $500 million in the preceding calendar year.

The second dataset inventories 65 public statements, signatures and documents from senior lab figures between January 2023 and September 21, 2026. The authors conducted separate searches of Chinese primary material and English or institutional sources, then reconciled and checked the results. Fifteen founder, CEO or chief-scientist statements address frontier safety; nine combine concern with a proposal, six from Zhipu. No qualifying founder or CEO statement was found for five labs. Alibaba’s Eddie Wu discussed superintelligence in September 2025, but Jiemian’s keynote transcript contains neither the Chinese word for safety/security nor risk. Zhipu and, later, MiniMax signed the Seoul commitments to publish severe-risk frameworks by the February 2025 Paris summit. Neither appears in METR’s twelve-company policy index.

The third dataset is a deliberately selected corpus of 102 prominent texts, divided equally between official/legal venues and scientific/technical ones. It is not a representative survey of Chinese opinion: the authors set venue quotas and sought prominent, attributable texts. They coded authors’ professional backgrounds separately from publication venues. Technical scientists discuss frontier or loss-of-control risk in 36 of their 42 texts; the corresponding shares for legal scholars, officials and policy scholars range from 22% to 44%. Twenty-two technical texts put safety before development or make it a condition of proceeding. None by the other three groups takes that position.

Thirteen texts demand binding duties for frontier developers, which the authors find unadopted. Seven are by legal or policy scholars, mostly model-law drafts. The six technical texts comprise five IDAIS joint statements and Zeng Yi, Huang Tiejun, Jiang Yugang and Poo Mu-ming’s April Towards China-initiated actions on AI safety and governance in National Science Review. The Beijing statement calls for registering training runs above compute or capability thresholds, and the Oxford statement for terminating all copies of systems that cross red lines; the editorial calls for expert committees, regularly updated guidelines and legal enforcement. Zeng and colleagues identify distrust between competitors and the absence of enforcement as obstacles, and propose that China initiate national action while scientists share safety technologies internationally. Only four of 32 influential people mapped both demand binding rules and hold domestic rule-writing roles. SemiAnalysis attributes the outcome to senior leaders’ preference for development.

The authors recognize substantial safety expertise: Shanghai AI Lab and Beijing consultancy Concordia AI have a risk framework, and the industry ministry’s CAICT research institute has a safety benchmark. They argue that these institutions cannot compel participation: CAICT testing is voluntary and TC260 standards require state action to become mandatory. SemiAnalysis also describes the China AI Safety and Development Association as lacking dedicated staff, budget and testing authority. Carnegie researchers’ June 2025 assessment was more tentative, finding no apparent dedicated resources or independent testing structure.

The geopolitical argument includes a WeChat essay by an engineer SemiAnalysis identifies as Liu Shengyu. It connects his decision to join the AI arms race to opposition to Anthropic controlling AGI. His original text gives a different immediate reason: competing labs would automate his work regardless. Distrust of Anthropic appears later, in his argument for open, cheap frontier AI and remaining at DeepSeek. SemiAnalysis’s accessible section ends by questioning Anthropic’s own restraint after its September 22 Opus 5.5 release. Anthropic’s announcement describes pacing as serving safety and beneficial applications alongside competition with China.

Concordia’s earlier disclosure studies supply a comparison. Its 2025 survey found published risk-evaluation results at three of thirteen developers; its July 2026 report found results accompanying some release at five of ten, none consistently. SemiAnalysis adds a release-level denominator and timing. Alexander Wan and colleagues’ Stanford report The 2025 Foundation Model Transparency Index, measuring broader disclosure, gave Alibaba and DeepSeek an average of 29 against 36.5 for US developers excluding IBM.

In an October 8 LessWrong response, CMLKevin welcomed the systematic investigation while defending Zhipu’s cyberdefense work and commitments. Brian Lui cited the article as evidence that safety advocates had failed to change China’s position; Eli Tyre replied that American commitment to pacing and the ability to verify an agreement mattered more, and that both were advancing.
