The White House science package combines new pledges with earlier investments

Eleven companies pledge $2.4 billion, while agency documents distinguish computing credits, prior investment and doctoral funding that depends on future appropriations.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 8 October 2026 1216 words · ~6 min

At its Science: A New Golden Age Summit on October 8, the White House announced more than $6 billion in science commitments from federal agencies, companies, universities and philanthropy. The summit implements the agenda in Michael Kratsios’s July report, which we covered when it appeared. The new announcements combine commercial AI access with research awards, doctoral training and experimental facilities. Their dollar values cover different things and periods: company credits and services, planned federal funding, and investments already made.

Eleven companies account for $2.4 billion in AI tools and computing credits for the Department of Energy’s Genesis Mission Consortium, supporting research across more than 15 federal agencies. NVIDIA pledged $1 billion, AMD $500 million, OpenAI $200 million, Anthropic and Google $150 million each, AMP and Emerald AI $100 million each, and AWS, Armada, Crusoe and Micron $50 million each. The fact sheet calls AI super intelligence, or SI, under the September 29 terminology order accompanying the White House AI accord. That order applies the existing statutory definition of artificial intelligence.

The companies’ releases explain the different forms of support. NVIDIA values its five-year commitment at $1 billion, covering university research, American quantum computing and cloud providers serving government missions. Anthropic’s $150 million over three years covers Claude, Claude Code and API credits for several hundred Genesis projects, work with agencies and national laboratories on fusion and quantum computing, and scientist training. Crusoe’s $50 million provides three years of cloud computing and model-running credits, plus technical support. Micron describes its contribution as up to $50 million for doctoral fellowships, mentorship and national-laboratory research. Armada promises GPU computing where scientists work.

According to Alfred Ng’s reporting for POLITICO, OpenAI’s $200 million consists of token discounts and scientist-training programs, while AWS’s $50 million consists of cloud credits. AWS had announced a Genesis Accelerator Initiative in February, offering up to $50 million in credits for 2026 to 2028. The announcements do not establish whether October’s figure includes that earlier pledge. AMD, Google and Emerald AI, together accounting for $750 million, did not respond to POLITICO. In TNW, Ana Maria Constantin raises a separate allocation question: donors also sell computing time commercially, and a credit does not itself establish when capacity will be available to researchers.

Trump’s November 2025 order establishing Genesis instructed DOE to combine federal and industry computing resources and arrange fellowships with laboratory placements. It committed no funding itself. DOE launched the consortium in February to coordinate members and their resources, then reported more than $800 million in partner commitments in July, including model access, computing and direct funding. The White House’s separate July announcement of more than $5 billion in federal commitments promised further industry and philanthropic participation. DOE’s first annual report, due in November, must describe partnership outcomes and scientific progress.

Federal coordination of donated AI resources has a precedent in the National Science Foundation’s (NSF) National AI Research Resource pilot, launched in January 2024 with 25 private and nonprofit partners, including six of October’s donors. Researchers could apply through a portal for computing, datasets and models. The Genesis pledges support agency research directed toward the administration’s named national science and technology challenges.

The summit’s headline total includes the $1.8 billion virtual-biology initiative announced on October 7. Biohub’s breakdown combines its founding $500 million commitment from April, more than $500 million of DOE work over five years, NIH datasets created through more than $500 million in earlier federal investment, and $300 million from Google DeepMind, Isomorphic Labs and Meta. Researchers will collect and standardize measurements of cells and their responses to interventions, enabling models that predict biological changes and help design experiments. NIH will contribute existing repositories; DOE will contribute measurement facilities, modeling and computing. The intended output is an open data resource for researchers developing predictive models.

DOE’s $215 million quantum-computing competition was announced on September 17, with $2.5 million in fiscal 2026 funding and later funding dependent on appropriations. The October 8 addition was eight priority applications to guide the competition, whose goal is to demonstrate quantum computers capable of scientifically useful calculations despite hardware errors.

The regional projects have different funding arrangements. The University of Alabama lists 15 founding universities across 10 states in the Southeast computing consortium, which will seek federal, state, philanthropic and industry support. The fact sheet separately attributes a $1 billion Georgia investment to universities, industry and the state, and names a five-university Carolinas hub. In a same-day announcement, Emory and Georgia Tech described their own investments exceeding $1 billion: roughly $600 million from Emory and more than $575 million from Georgia Tech. Nearly half of Emory’s share has already gone toward recruiting and retaining talent. Their release does not reconcile that breakdown with the White House’s Georgia figure.

DOE also announced 12 Phase II Genesis awards totaling $159 million. Commonwealth Fusion Systems will develop a computer simulation of its SPARC fusion device to test and improve operating choices. Berkeley Lab will lead a shared AI platform for designing and operating particle accelerators. These projects extend the Genesis research portfolio first announced in July.

The new Genesis Mission Graduate Fellowship plans up to $100 million for approximately 100 students combining AI with another scientific discipline in four-year doctorates. DOE identifies $20 million for fiscal 2027; later funding depends on congressional appropriations. The funding notice anticipates four to eight institutional awards of $12 million to $25 million each over five years. The grant listing instead displays five $20 million awards. Fellows must be U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents and complete placements of at least ten weeks in industry and at a national laboratory or DOE user facility. Their training must address ethical implications, safety and limitations of AI applications. The notice warns that submitting machine-generated narratives as applicants’ own intellectual work may constitute misconduct, while allowing assistance in presenting their work and requiring disclosure of AI use beyond editing.

NSF’s contribution to the fact sheet’s more than $100 million for AI-enabled instruments and autonomous laboratories is a forthcoming funding opportunity of up to $75 million over five years. It will support instruments designed for digital operation within the programmable cloud-lab network announced in July. Anthropic’s Genesis pledge mentions its Model Hardware Standard for agents operating laboratory equipment. Its same-day usage-policy announcement says potentially injurious autonomous equipment will require an operator able to observe and stop it, and a safe state if Claude disconnects.

NSF’s new Office of Metascience and the UK Metascience Unit intend to cooperate on research-impact measurement and funding review, building on the Kyoto Vision endorsed on October 4. They plan to formalize the collaboration in the coming months. Metascience researcher Paul Nightingale welcomed some proposed reforms on Bluesky, but argued that AI-generated submissions burden journals and that metascience cannot yet deliver the improvements the declaration envisages.

University of Maryland biologist Joshua Weitz questioned the training announcement against proposed fellowship cuts. NSF’s fiscal 2027 budget request would reduce discretionary funding from $8.83 billion in its fiscal 2025 current plan to $3.96 billion. Using the same baseline, the Graduate Research Fellowship Program would fall from $433 million to $246.54 million, a 43.1 percent reduction. These are requested reductions, while the new DOE fellowship’s full amount also depends on future appropriations.
