Leicht proposes factory partnerships that keep industrial AI gains local

In Alliance Engine, Anton Leicht proposes a bargain between allied manufacturers and American AI labs: guaranteed purchases and exclusive data access in exchange for models whose improvements stay with the factory.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 8 October 2026 1430 words · ~7 min

Allied manufacturers could retain bargaining power with American AI developers by keeping factory data and the models improved with it tied to individual plants, Anton Leicht argues in Alliance Engine, published October 8 in Threading the Needle. The Carnegie fellow proposes exclusive partnerships: manufacturers guarantee long-term purchases and access to production data; developers supply models tailored to those factories. He wants allies to benefit from American AI while retaining industrial capabilities the Americans would find difficult to replace. Manufacturing, he argues, would turn the powerful AI envisaged in Dario Amodei’s Machines of Loving Grace into medicines, robots and other physical products.

Leicht begins by questioning industrial strategies that protect familiar incumbents, from Korean conglomerates to German carmakers. He rejects the assumption that proprietary data and small specialized models will let them dispense with American frontier systems. General-purpose models, he argues, increasingly acquire specialized capabilities through further training on domain-specific data. He points to Anthropic’s enzyme-discovery work and OpenAI’s use of models in designing its Jalapeño chip. He nevertheless thinks frontier developers dismiss Mario Draghi’s data argument too readily: Il Foglio’s account of Draghi’s Financial Times essay describes European data as a potential source of growth and independence.

Access to frontier models remains Leicht’s prerequisite. He contributed to A Transformative AI Strategy for Europe, whose infrastructure-for-access proposals we covered on September 14. His September 28 Carnegie paper with Sam Winter-Levy, AI Breakout Capacity, proposes retaining the ability to attempt frontier development if imported access fails. Here he asks how countries that secure access can turn it into lasting industrial strength.

His economic case begins with comparative advantage: allies already possess skilled workers and factories, while American labs concentrate the talent and capital required for frontier development. He speculates that AI investment could also impede American reindustrialization by absorbing finance, electricity and construction workers. Census figures for August 2026 show private factory-construction spending down 19.8% from a year earlier. Wolf Richter reports data-center construction up 73%, alongside accounts of specialists moving to those projects. Factory construction still attracted nearly twice as much spending. Those simultaneous changes do not establish that AI caused the factory decline; Leicht explicitly presents the crowding-out mechanism as speculation.

Strategically, he extends Kurt Campbell and Rush Doshi’s Underestimating China, a 2025 Foreign Affairs essay urging America to pool industrial capacity with partners. UNIDO’s 2025 yearbook puts China’s share of global manufacturing at 32% in 2024, against America’s 15%. Campbell and Doshi estimate roughly half for a broader coalition including the US, EU, India and other partners. Leicht argues that American involvement in allied factories would help align production with US strategic interests, while frontier models would help those factories compete with an increasingly automated China.

Politically, Leicht wants investment to preserve the importance of existing industrial regions and firms, giving incumbents and unions reasons to support AI adoption. He accepts that many jobs would disappear. His aim is to prevent the wider relocation of economic power that he associates with Britain’s concentration of activity in London. Supporting familiar industries could, he argues, make disruption more politically manageable without promising unchanged employment.

He also wants governments to secure access to several competing frontier providers. If firms can choose among suppliers, competition could lower model prices and leave more of AI’s value with manufacturers and other users. Inside each industrial partnership, however, he proposes deliberate dependence. Developers would compete for long-term contracts, then invest in systems whose value depends on continuing that particular relationship.

The factory would combine skilled workers, robots and extensive sensors. A nearby data center would run a developer-owned frontier model trained further on the plant’s operations, with the developer’s engineers working alongside factory staff. They would correct inefficiencies the model identifies and test its proposals for materials, processes and products. The experiments would generate data for further local model improvement. Leicht calls this “RSI-ready manufacturing” and borrows the language of recursive self-improvement to describe his proposed cycle of model suggestions, physical tests and further training. An adaptable plant would make those experiments faster.

Under his proposed rules, the specialized model would stay in the local data center, and neither the factory data nor the resulting model improvements would enter the developer’s general training. Manufacturers would commit to long-term purchases and exclusive data-use rights in return for exclusive industrial systems. The factory would depend on its model to remain competitive; the developer would need that factory to earn a return on an investment it could not deploy elsewhere.

Leicht rejects each side’s preferred arrangement. Manufacturers would ideally control the software using their data, insert whichever model they chose and let no information flow back to the supplier. Developers would ideally improve their general models with data from every collaboration. He argues that either arrangement gives one party too much economic power. His compromise raises manufacturers’ switching costs and prevents developers from spreading each deployment’s gains across their wider business. Long lead times for building factories or developing frontier models would give both sides further reasons to reach an accommodation, although he accepts that dependence alone cannot prevent exploitation.

A relevant economic precedent is Oliver Williamson’s 1983 American Economic Review paper Credible Commitments: Using Hostages to Support Exchange. He argued that reciprocal exposure can discourage abandonment when both parties invest in assets that lose value outside their relationship. Leicht does not cite Williamson, but his proposed restrictions appear to serve that purpose. Williamson’s argument depends on both sides having investments at risk.

Leicht wants governments to encourage the deals through tax advantages, barriers against competing products and favorable trade terms with America. He argues that these measures would answer American subsidies and Chinese trade practices, and push manufacturers that have been slow to invest. He also wants fewer obstacles to cross-border industrial data sharing, without identifying a particular law. Once deals exist, governments would help stabilize them against two risks: developers extracting enough knowledge to recreate production elsewhere, and factories capturing so much of the benefit that developers lose interest.

The data restriction develops an earlier concern. In their February Foreign Affairs essay The AI Divide, Winter-Levy and Leicht warned that selling or surrendering data could exhaust its bargaining value. Leicht’s July Asterisk essay Beware the Permanent Periphery identified manufacturing as a capability allies could preserve through frontier AI. His February newsletter Compute for Access favored software allowing buyers to exchange models. The new design accepts higher switching costs within each factory partnership, while seeking competition across the market.

Existing arrangements resemble parts of the proposal. OpenAI’s enterprise-privacy page says customer fine-tuned models are reserved to that customer and are not used to train other models. SoftBank committed $3 billion annually to OpenAI products in February 2025; their SB OAI Japan venture subsequently launched to sell tailored enterprise systems in Japan. Siemens and NVIDIA plan adaptive factories, starting with Siemens’ Erlangen plant. They envisage testing changes in digital simulations before implementing them on the factory floor. These announcements do not describe Leicht’s complete bargain tying a developer-owned model to one factory through exclusive data rights and purchase commitments.

Jacob Schaal, who argued for negotiating Europe’s participation in American AI in Internationale Politik in May, offered a concrete modification in an October 8 response: interoperability rules permitting manufacturers to switch developers at contract renewal. He argues that this could preserve exclusive deals during their term while sustaining price competition. Schaal also wants conditions protecting factory know-how. His alternative ways for firms to profit from process data include owning a model trained on it or selling training environments to developers. These differ from Leicht’s insistence on developer ownership of a restricted local model. Leicht leaves contract duration, exit rights and the fate of the local model after termination unspecified.

Knowledge can travel through people as well as datasets. In Quid Pro Quo, Knowledge Spillovers, and Industrial Quality Upgrading, published in the American Economic Review in 2025, Jie Bai and colleagues found that Chinese automakers developed quality strengths resembling those of their foreign joint-venture partners. Their working paper compares vehicle-quality dimensions and investigates employee movements and shared suppliers as transmission channels. That evidence concerns automakers learning from other automakers; Leicht proposes cooperation across industries. His embedded-engineer design nevertheless makes protection of process knowledge a question extending beyond where data is stored.

Leicht acknowledges that the best industrial models and a convincing business case remain uncertain. He expects commercially promising results only after substantial integration. Waiting for firms elsewhere to establish the model, he argues, risks leaving allied manufacturers with weaker revenues and less valuable assets when they finally negotiate.
