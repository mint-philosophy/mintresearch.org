Demis Hassabis proposes a FINRA for frontier AI

In a July 14 essay, the Google DeepMind chief laid out an industry-funded, US-led body to vet frontier models for up to 30 days before release, modeled on Wall Street's FINRA and able to coordinate an industry-wide slowdown if risks mount. Altman and Musk voiced early support; Hassabis takes it to Washington next week.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 17 July 2026 439 words · ~2 min

Google DeepMind chief executive Demis Hassabis, in an essay posted to his Substack on July 14 titled “A Framework for Frontier AI and the Dawning of a New Age”, proposed a US-led body to test the most capable AI models before they reach the public. Written around his view that artificial general intelligence may be only a few years off, the framework is modeled on the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, the industry-funded self-regulator that polices Wall Street under government oversight. Hassabis wants a board of independent technical experts and open-source representatives, funded mostly by the leading labs, running scientific evaluations of a model’s capabilities in cybersecurity, biological threats, and deception. The body could delegate that work to outside AI safety groups. Frontier developers would share their models with it voluntarily, up to 30 days before release.

Hassabis told Bloomberg’s Shirin Ghaffary he published it after Anthropic’s Mythos model displayed advanced cybersecurity capabilities, which he called a “warning shot” for society. His deeper worry runs toward biosecurity. “The models aren’t good enough yet,” he said, but extrapolate a couple of years and they might be. His company already talks to governments and national AI security institutes as its models hit certain benchmarks, he said, and that improvised arrangement “isn’t very sustainable in the longer term.”

Voluntary testing, Hassabis wrote, would be only the start. Once the protocol proved reliable, clearing it could become a condition for deploying a frontier model in the US market, and the body’s remit could “be ratcheted up if the seriousness of the situation demands,” up to a coordinated slowdown in development across the leading labs. His plan follows Sam Altman’s earlier call for a US-led international group to set AI safety standards. Hassabis said he circulated the framework among major labs before publishing, including with Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, whose proposed FAA-style AI regulator he called “very compatible” with his own thinking.

Altman called the plan “thoughtful,” and Elon Musk called it “a good starting point for discussions,” Bloomberg reported. Hassabis plans to bring the proposal to US policymakers in Washington next week. Ghaffary notes that a Nobel laureate with fewer industry feuds than Musk or Altman may be better placed than most to rally support. The US, EU, and China have diverged on AI regulation, the Trump administration has swung between hands-off and hands-on, and Congress has passed no significant federal AI law. One AI leader, speaking anonymously, told Bloomberg that agreement among companies will not by itself bring the government along. The next step, Hassabis said, is to see whether the idea can be made real, “not just publish a blog post.”
