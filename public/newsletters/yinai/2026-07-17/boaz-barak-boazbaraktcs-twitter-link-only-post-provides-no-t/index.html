Boaz Barak on who rules the country of geniuses

In an X article, the Harvard and OpenAI computer scientist reads Dario Amodei's "Machines of Loving Grace" as sketching an AI benevolent dictator, and argues that ever bigger models need not hand a few entities, or the AI itself, a monopoly on intelligence.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 17 July 2026 461 words · ~2 min

In an X article titled "All Watched Over", Boaz Barak, a computer scientist at Harvard and OpenAI, borrows his title from Richard Brautigan's 1967 poem "All Watched Over by Machines of Loving Grace," which Steven Levy's Hackers describes as an inspiration to California's 1970s hardware hackers. In 2026, Barak writes, the same phrase evokes something colder: humanity cradled by an aligned AI, an "AI benevolent dictator." He reads Dario Amodei's essay "Machines of Loving Grace" as sketching that future, an economy where AI systems give out resources based on "what the AI systems think makes sense to reward in humans." To Barak, that casts the AIs as parents deciding how to reward or punish their human children.

The essay runs on a historical parallel. In the 1960s, Barak writes, computers were large IBM machines that much of the left tied to the military industrial complex, until a group pairing anti-establishment politics with a love of technology pushed toward small, cheap machines and the personal computer revolution that produced the Apple II. Today AI plays the mainframe's part: large, expensive, and increasingly built into military applications. Scaling laws pull against the 1970s trajectory, toward bigger models in ever larger data centers. Barak takes Amodei's image of AGI as "a country of geniuses in a data center" and asks who rules that country: the company that owns the data center, or the AI itself.

Barak, who says he works on AI safety and is as "bitter lesson pilled" as anyone, argues that settling intelligence through raw compute does not settle the social and economic outcome. He worries that concentration of power, in a few entities or in the AI itself, could become the "default path," while holding that it remains a choice.

Against the claim that safety requires AI under the control of a government, a cautious lab, or the aligned AI, Barak points to history. Real dangers have been used to curtail freedoms before, he writes, from Soviet spies during the McCarthy period to the terrorist groups the NSA tracked in Edward Snowden's era. Neither tradeoff, he judges in hindsight, was worth it. He wants safeguards built and tested empirically, and warns that chasing perfect safety against every risk, real or imagined, will cost liberty.

Barak prescribes decentralization by design: no single entity in charge, no monopoly on intelligence, and no exception for the model, whose trained-in guardrails are "never a substitute for our democratic process." He credits U.S. checks and balances for 250 years of stability and wants the distribution of AI's power "baked into the DNA" of how the technology gets built. Barak wrote the essay, he notes, after a discussion on AGI with Sam Altman; the views are his own and do not represent Altman, OpenAI, or Harvard.
