Boaz Barak on the default path to an AI dictator

On LessWrong, Barak argues that scaling makes concentrated power the likely default of a post-AGI world but not inevitable, and that distributed power has to be baked into how AI is built and deployed; a commenter presses whether such a world could stay stable.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 17 July 2026 499 words · ~2 min

On LessWrong, the AI-safety researcher Boaz Barak published “All Watched Over” on July 16, borrowing its title from Richard Brautigan’s 1967 poem “All Watched Over by Machines of Loving Grace.” For Barak, the phrase now conjures humanity cradled by a powerful aligned AI: a benevolent dictator. He points to Dario Amodei’s essay “Machines of Loving Grace,” which imagined “a capitalist economy of AI systems” distributing resources to humans according to “what the AI systems think makes sense to reward in humans.” Barak reads that as an AI parent to its human children, and worries that concentrated power, in a few entities or the AI itself, could be the “default path.”

Barak takes the opposing vision from Steven Levy’s book “Hackers.” Brautigan’s poem inspired the 1970s California “hardware hackers” and groups like Community Memory, who pushed computing from IBM’s mainframes toward the cheap machines behind the Apple II. Today’s AI resembles those mainframes, he argues: large, costly, increasingly wired into military use, with scaling laws rewarding more compute, data, and power, so systems grow bigger and more centralized. Amodei described AGI as a “country of geniuses in a datacenter”; Barak asks who rules that country, the company that owns it or the AI itself. The few who can afford frontier systems may capture the value they generate; with the U.S. behind on open-weight AI and left and right refusing to engage, the trends, Barak argues, cut against a decentralized future.

“Bitter lesson pilled” and “scaling law pilled” by his own account, Barak grants that intelligence is computation that scales, then argues this settles neither the social nor the economic outcome. To the claim that market forces will drive people to cede control to AIs, he answers that the economy tracks what humans value; productivity, labor, capital, and GDP may stop making sense after AGI. Against the case that safety requires control by a government, a “safety conscious” lab, or the AI itself, he recalls McCarthy-era Soviet spies and the terrorism of Snowden’s NSA years, real threats, and tradeoffs hindsight judged not worth it. Guardrails and a model’s good personality, he writes, are “never a substitute for our democratic process.” He wants distributed power baked “into the DNA” of AI, so no single entity rules and none holds a monopoly on intelligence.

In the comments, a reader posting as LaplaceHolder pressed the market objection: sufficiently capable AI agents would make the economy about what they value too. Barak replied that agent autonomy is itself a choice, that a human-centered economy can hold, and that people whose material needs are met may value human relationships more. LaplaceHolder answered that self-reinforcing goals compound: whoever tasks an AI with amassing energy and intelligence and reinvesting pulls ahead, and suppressing that looks harder in a decentralized world. Without some stabilizer, the commenter wrote, “a garden without a gardener will become all weeds.” Barak noted the essay followed a discussion on AGI with Sam Altman; the views are his own, not OpenAI’s or Harvard’s.
