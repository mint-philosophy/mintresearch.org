At the highest stakes, Claude users shift toward reading for understanding

Across 249,834 conversations, overall engagement rose with stakes. In the highest tier, adaptation dipped while reading for comprehension rebounded, complicating any simple claim that consequential work draws less scrutiny.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 August 2026 738 words · ~4 min

In a paper posted to alphaXiv, "Human-AI Collaboration at Scale: Task Criticality, Agency, and Friction across 250,000 Conversations", Yijia Shao, a PhD candidate at Stanford NLP, and coauthors Dora Zhao, Vishakh Padmakumar, Jennifer Wang and Diyi Yang scored 249,834 real Claude.ai conversations for what users hand to the model and how they treat what comes back. Users mostly rework the output instead of accepting it, and that stance strengthens as the work gets more consequential: verbatim use falls from 21% of the lowest-stakes conversations to 13% of the highest, and adaptation climbs from 46% to a peak of 60% on consequential tasks. In the highest-stakes tier the trend reverses. Adaptation drops back to 52%, critique peaks on operational work (18%), and reading for comprehension, which had fallen to 11%, returns to 21%. The authors warn that the pattern "might lead to the weakening of human oversight precisely where it matters most". Shao wrote on X that the findings point back to the human side of collaboration.

Anthropic supplied the data through a pilot it described on August 26, its first attempt at letting outside groups design their own studies on real Claude usage. Its Societal Impacts team ran the analysis on the researchers' behalf with Anthropic Insights, the privacy-preserving clustering tool previously called Clio, over conversations sampled from Free, Pro and Max traffic between April 25 and May 9, 2026, with Claude Code and Claude Cowork excluded. The Stanford group wrote 17 classification facets and tuned them against the public WildChat corpus, where hand labels initially matched claude-haiku-4.5 annotations 56% of the time before iteration closed the gap. They then received cluster-level aggregates across 891 clusters and no transcripts. Anthropic dropped under 5% of conversations for usage-policy violations before sharing, and calls the pilot "the first time external researchers have run public independent studies" on a lab's own usage data.

Criticality in the paper is scored on irreversibility, external visibility and impact, then sorted into four tiers. Among conversations carrying an actionable task, 44% reach the consequential tier and 12% the high-stakes tier, against 24% operational and 20% ephemeral; another 34% of the corpus carries no actionable task at all. Legal and financial guidance leads the nine domains with a criticality index of 2.14 on a 0 to 3 scale, and about a quarter of its conversations are high stakes. Academic coursework sits last at 1.06. Conversations nearly double in length as stakes climb, from 6.5 turns to 12.4, and prompts get more precise, though users do not break the work into smaller pieces.

For agency the team applied the Human Agency Scale that Shao, Erik Brynjolfsson and coauthors introduced in a 2025 audit of 1,500 U.S. workers. 72% of conversations sit at the level where the human leads and Claude assists, and 18.2% at the two levels where the model works with little or no human input. The autonomous cases cluster in document production, led by presentation deck creation at 58.7%. Personal medical guidance runs at 4.9%. Almost every task category spans the full range, which the authors read as users deciding conversation by conversation how much control to keep.

Friction appeared in 49.7 percent of conversations. User under-specification compounded by model misinterpretation accounted for 42.0 percent of those cases, while model capability limits and hallucination accounted for 38.6 percent. Users tried to repair the exchange in 78.7 percent of friction cases; challenging the model’s reasoning ended productively 81.5 percent of the time, while merely rewording the request produced an unproductive outcome in 19.8 percent.

The paper lists three limits that follow from the privacy design. Cluster-level aggregation leaves no way to check the classifier against individual conversations, the corpus covers chatbot traffic and not agentic products, and it draws on one provider's users. Anthropic says its contractual review rights ran only to user privacy, information that could help people violate its usage policies, its own confidential information and research accuracy, and it commissioned a privacy audit from Imperial College London. It has published the aggregate data from all three pilot projects on Hugging Face; the Oxford Human Information Processing Lab's study of how people feel while using Claude and METR's estimate of coding speedups across model generations are still unpublished. Outside researchers have been building around the absence of this kind of access, including the AI Observatory's cross-provider corpus, which found that Anthropic's work-focused filter would remove 48% of its conversations.
