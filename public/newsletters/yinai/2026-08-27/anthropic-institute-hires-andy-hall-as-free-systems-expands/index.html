Andy Hall joins the Anthropic Institute and scales up Free Systems

On leave from Stanford, Hall takes a full-time seat on the Institute's Rule of Law team while his lab adds three faculty, a first manager, and subscriber-funded grants; his Dictatorship Eval already sits in Anthropic's Opus 4.8 system card.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 August 2026 955 words · ~5 min

Andrew Hall used his August 26 post "The Next Phase of Free Systems" to announce two moves at once. He has taken leave from Stanford, where he is the Davies Family Professor of Political Economy at the Graduate School of Business and a senior fellow at the Hoover Institution, to join the Rule of Law team at the Anthropic Institute full time, work he describes as researching the political economy of superintelligence by "analyzing internal data, running agentic experiments" and publishing research that helps society prepare for powerful AI. He wrote that he was a week into it. Free Systems, the Stanford lab he directs, is expanding at the same time.

Hall calls this a "special and narrow window of opportunity" to shape how AI transforms politics. AI is becoming an intermediary between citizens and politics, he writes, shaping where people find political information, what they take to be true, and how their preferences get translated into choices. He wants what a March 26 post called political superintelligence: systems that help citizens understand the world, represent their values, improve the rules that govern them, and make governments more capable and accountable. Making models smarter will not produce that on its own. A very capable model can still rely on a distorted information environment, misunderstand what its user wants, "defer to its creator", or help a powerful person build systems that concentrate control. "Intelligence alone does not produce liberty", he writes.

As the labs have picked up Free Systems work and he has spoken more with people inside them, Hall writes, the value of operating where models are trained, evaluated, and turned into systems people actually use has become clear to him. Anthropic announced the Institute on March 11, saying its goal is to "tell the world what we're learning about these challenges" as it builds frontier systems, and named Matt Botvinick, a resident fellow at Yale Law School and previously senior director of research at Google DeepMind, to lead its work on AI and the rule of law.

Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.8 system card, published May 28, shows how far Hall's outside work had already reached inside. Its automated behavioral audit scores a metric called "Undermining liberal democracy", covering cooperation with coup planning, election tampering, and politically motivated surveillance or intimidation, and a footnote credits the origin: "Inspired by the Dictatorship Eval by Andrew B. Hall." That eval, released April 2, put five frontier models through scenarios drawn from authoritarian playbooks and found refusal rates on direct requests varying widely across models, with compliance climbing once the same purpose was buried in routine-looking edits to a codebase. Hall reported in May that Anthropic had begun using the eval to train and evaluate its models under the renamed metric.

Hall argues the outside work matters as much. The world needs "people who can ask questions the labs may not think to ask", test models across companies, monitor their public effects, and build open evaluations, he writes, and Free Systems will help play that role. The lab has brought on three senior academic collaborators it calls members of the Free Systems faculty: Alex Fouirnaies, associate professor at the University of Chicago's Harris School of Public Policy, who studies how money and media shape representation and accountability; Sandy Handan-Nader, assistant professor of politics at NYU, who builds machine-learning measures of conflict and polarization in American institutions; and Dan Thompson of UCLA's political science department, already a collaborator on the lab's prediction-market research and now helping lead its study of AI voting advice in the 2026 midterms. James Campsie, who has worked on the Substack since before it launched, becomes the first full-time lab manager for a roster of "more than ten talented research fellows".

All Free Systems subscription fees go into a community fund, Hall writes, and its first major use will be API-token grants for applicants who want to "extend, replicate, improve, or challenge research we have already published", such as running the Dictatorship Eval on new models and languages or replicating the lab's political-advice work in another election. Free Systems also launched a website collecting its research, tools, and datasets, and an X account.

The queued research spans four themes. Free Systems is studying AI voting advice in the US midterms and Brazil's election, running an agentic political advertising agency whose "ads are already appearing on Instagram", and mapping the networks behind positive and negative narratives about AI. On pluralism and institutional design, it is fine-tuning models to hold different political views, extending the Dictatorship Eval, and comparing cabinets, courts, juries, and peer review in an LLM Council. Work on agents in the economy asks how they can safely enter agreements on a user's behalf, with field experiments on live AI-agent marketplaces. Under public accountability sit a Model Card Explorer, an automated political-science reviewer tested on "146 papers with deliberately injected errors", and a measure of whether AI research assistants resist p-hacking.

Hall keeps the two jobs formally apart. Free Systems stays "fully Stanford-funded" and housed across Hoover and the GSB, his Anthropic work will be "wholly separate from the Free Systems work", and the Substack carries a standing note that his posts "reflect only his personal views". The lab has been working with Anthropic's published data already: an August 4 analysis of the company's Economic Index put politics at roughly 0.5% of Claude conversations, 66th among 189 topics, with the share rising about 20% in states that held May primaries. Hall gives the next one to two years as the period that decides between AI-powered authoritarianism and a strengthened form of democratic self-government, and he wants both the frontier labs and an outside research community working the problem.
