Scott Alexander's case against unilateral AI exits

In “Against SURA,” Alexander supports a global pause but wants an alignment fallback if negotiations fail. His proposals face objections about developer responsibility, the timing of a pause and whether frontier companies will honor their commitments.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 31 October 2026 1195 words · ~6 min

Scott Alexander favors a coordinated global pause on frontier AI development, potentially lasting decades. In his October 31 LessWrong essay “Against SURA”, he argues that preparing for negotiations to fail is also essential. He calls demands for concerned companies, employees and countries to stop independently “Stop Unilaterally + Raise Awareness,” or SURA. This is his grouping of positions, not a platform jointly adopted by everyone he criticizes. He fears unilateral withdrawal would leave less cautious competitors with both the strongest systems and the power to determine their use.

His fallback depends on preserving a lead for companies willing to prioritize alignment. If they cannot secure a worldwide agreement, the leading companies would jointly stop advancing capabilities for the interval before an uncooperative rival catches up. They would use their most advanced models to help solve alignment during that interval, then deploy as late as possible. Alexander acknowledges that this is dangerous and requires several things to go right. Its value depends on whether a concentrated research effort can make advanced AI safer before competitors force the decision.

Alexander's technical optimism rests on a conjecture about training. He thinks imitation during pretraining gives models helpful dispositions that later reinforcement learning can undermine. Training adjustments, monitoring and incentives might keep AI research assistants sufficiently dependable for long enough to improve alignment, perhaps through successive generations of better assistants. He does not claim to have established that this will work. He also acknowledges that MIRI researchers have developed substantive reasons for expecting near-term alignment efforts to fail. His objection is to treating that pessimism as settled without accepting or examining the reasoning behind it.

He draws on the AI Futures Project's comparison of plans, while combining its categories differently. His broad Plan A includes prolonged pauses; AIFP's narrower Plan A is a verified international slowdown with research transparency and continued, constrained scaling. His broad Plan C includes several fallbacks. The five forecasters estimate that domestic regulation under C+ improves the probability of avoiding misaligned takeover by about 20 percentage points on average relative to an almost unrestricted race. These are judgments about hypothetical futures, not measured intervention effects. Alexander infers that preparing fallback plans may contribute more to eventual survival because a global agreement is comparatively unlikely; AIFP's forecasts do not constitute endorsement of that inference or his recommendations.

Alexander also asks the AI forecasting service FutureSearch what would happen if Anthropic immediately disbanded to promote a pause. Its estimate raises global-pause probability from 16% to 19%; he combines this with an assumed loss of alignment capacity to calculate a net reduction in survival prospects. He explicitly warns that different question wordings produced different definitions and probabilities. FutureSearch also estimates a capabilities delay he translates into about six months over four years. Alexander argues that calendar time has unequal value: time with useful research tools and public attention may achieve more than an earlier interval. Neither calculation establishes the effect of an actual company withdrawal. Commenter ModelMirror adds that an AI forecaster may reproduce AIFP's published estimates, making apparent agreement correlated evidence.

Alexander directs his longest criticism at Sigal Samuel's October 27 Vox article on Anthropic's safety strategy. Samuel asks whether competing at the frontier has improved safety overall. She points to accelerated releases, weakened commitments and the pursuit of recursive self-improvement, in which AI helps build more capable AI. She argues that the catastrophic downside places the burden of proof on those continuing development. Geoffrey Irving and Daniel Kokotajlo support taking a unilateral first step in her account, followed by broader coordination. Samuel argues that China also has reasons to avoid catastrophe and that governments may move too slowly. Her proposed stop preserves existing systems, including defensive uses; Nathan Calvin specifically challenges further frontier development. It is narrower than disbanding Anthropic. Alexander's objection is that Samuel gives insufficient reason to expect other developers to stop after it does.

Alexander argues that retaining concerned people inside companies can also help secure a global pause. They can fund regulation, support public commitments and disclose failures. He acknowledges that Jacob Coxon's resignation itself raised awareness, but says Coxon's industry experience and Evan Hubinger's public support gave the warning credibility. Their positions were described in our September 9 coverage. Commenter Vaniver disputes the implication that leaving weakens advocacy: resignation produced the warning, and prominent critics outside labs also attract attention.

At the national level, Alexander argues that America's lead gives China an incentive to negotiate. He criticizes the Sanders-Casar bill for putting domestic restraint before agreement, revising his earlier belief that serious pause advocates sought only coordinated action. The proposal was announced September 3; MIRI's September 23 endorsement explains a different strategic judgment. MIRI supports the initial domestic stop while insisting that preventing superintelligence requires international action. The disagreement concerns sequencing and the risks of waiting, not whether a domestic ban alone is sufficient.

Alexander concedes that commercial incentives can corrupt judgment, but argues that activism has pressures of its own. Movements can reward simple villains and discourage discussion of competition as disloyal. He thinks assuming every company is equally untrustworthy also neglects differences that could determine whether eventual power is shared or concentrated. His answer to a moral prohibition on development compares developers with safety organizations that continue using AI despite contributing to company revenue. AdamYedidia objects that developers bear greater responsibility and are far fewer in number than consumers, making coordinated refusal more feasible. He also argues that decisions imposed on everyone must take seriously the moral rules others expect developers to follow.

Alexander's alternative begins with binding conditional commitments: each major developer agrees to stop or slow if the others worldwide do. The signatures would reveal whether the obstacle is international diplomacy, domestic regulation or a particular holdout. If agreement remains incomplete, willing leaders should commit in advance to halting dangerous training, sharing alignment research and involving outside safety groups when a specified authority identifies an approaching capability threshold. Better security would prevent theft from erasing their research lead. OpenAI's Charter already contains a narrower precedent: assisting a safety-conscious project that approaches AGI first. Alexander wants enforceable agreements and attention to competition-law barriers, not reliance on companies' favorable self-images.

The responses challenge whether waiting until near the threshold is wise. Alexander exempts AIFP's domestic pacing proposal because poor security could let rivals steal advances. AIFP researcher Eli Lifland corrects his suggestion that fixing security would justify returning to full-speed racing. Lifland favors pacing even with good security: it provides practice, reduces the danger of missing an uncertain point of no return, and could improve prospects for agreement with China. AIFP's proposal also permits continued revenue from existing systems while redirecting compute toward safety.

TsviBT proposes another alternative: retain frontier alignment research without releasing leading models, commit to a conditional indefinite stop, and fund sustained lobbying and negotiations. The comment asks why Anthropic has not devoted more visible organizational effort to securing such a stop. Alexander himself anticipates that companies may postpone a mutual pause or quarrel until it is too late. His proposals consequently depend on converting willingness to stop under favorable conditions into commitments that constrain behavior when stopping becomes costly.
