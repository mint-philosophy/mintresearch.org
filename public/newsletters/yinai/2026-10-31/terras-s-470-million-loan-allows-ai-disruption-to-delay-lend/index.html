Terras loan allows AI disruption to delay lenders' default remedies

Bloomberg reports that interest remains owed under the €470 million loan's unusual terms. Investors are checking existing documents, while lenders removed similar language from Everllence's financing.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 31 October 2026 685 words · ~3 min

Investors are checking their bond documents for clauses that could delay action on defaults blamed on AI disruption, Emily Graffeo reported in Bloomberg's Going Private newsletter on October 30, citing two people familiar with the reviews. Her account follows Eleanor Duncan and Kat Hidalgo's October 27 report on the €470 million loan backing H.I.G. Capital's majority investment in Terras Group. According to Bloomberg's sources, the German infrastructure contractor could postpone lenders' default remedies for as long as it considers itself affected by AI. Interest would remain owed.

Duncan and Hidalgo describe a change to the time allowed to address a missed payment. A borrower normally has a grace period, typically 30 days in their account, before a default can be called and lenders can demand repayment. Terras's reported provision extends that period by reference to AI's continuing effects. The borrower would remain liable for interest while creditors' enforcement rights were suspended. Bloomberg reports no actual missed payment or use of the clause by Terras. Its description of the terms comes from people familiar with private documentation; the published article does not reproduce the credit agreement.

H.I.G.'s July 6 announcement describes an agreement to acquire a majority stake in a business that builds civil infrastructure, foundations and railways across German-speaking Europe. Terras also operates quarries and recycling facilities. H.I.G. based its investment case on Germany's infrastructure backlog and the energy transition. H.I.G. said it would expand Terras's presence in its existing German markets and selectively enter new regions.

Bloomberg's sources say the Terras language covers pandemics, substantial increases in energy or resource prices, war and terrorism alongside AI disruption. Several creditors considered the provisions unusually broad. Their concern about duration is concrete: a change in a company's competitive position caused by AI may have no identifiable end date. A pause in enforcement tied to that condition could therefore last much longer than an ordinary payment grace period. The precise trigger and any limits would depend on the unavailable agreement.

Lenders successfully resisted comparable terms in another transaction, according to Duncan and Hidalgo. Similar language was removed from Bain Capital's €4.7 billion-equivalent leveraged loan for its controlling investment in Volkswagen's engine business Everllence. H.I.G. and Bain declined to comment to Bloomberg.

The Terras documents reportedly also permit adjustments to earnings for AI's effects, including excluding losses or adding back revenue. That provision changes the financial measures used to assess the borrower, separately from the delay in enforcing payment obligations. Adjusted EBITDA, an earnings measure before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, often supplies the denominator in debt-to-earnings ratios. Increasing it can make leverage appear lower without reducing the debt or producing cash with which to pay interest.

Bloomberg had reported an earlier AI adjustment at French property-services company Emeria. In their September 17 article, Duncan, Edward Clark and Libby Cherry described €20 million of projected savings over the next two years added to the previous twelve months' EBITDA. Emeria reported leverage of about 8.44 times; Bloomberg calculated about 9.3 times after removing the AI adjustment and other items totaling roughly €40 million. The larger ratio therefore cannot be attributed to the AI figure alone.

Emeria defended its adjustments as consistent with methods agreed with lenders and based on specified automation of documents, client responses and administrative work. It said it disclosed each adjustment separately so investors could assess them. Benjamin Sabahi of Spread Research argued that savings should count only once generated, while Insight Investment's Catherine Braganza said she preferred cash flow to projected savings. Their disagreement concerns whether prospective efficiencies belong in a measure used to judge present debt capacity.

Ahmed Mounir makes a related argument about enforcement in The Augmented Investor. He accepts that a temporary pause can prevent lenders from forcing losses during a sector-wide shock. But he argues that AI competition is a foreseeable investment risk and that borrowers should not control an indefinite suspension of remedies. His proposed compromise is an objective trigger, a firm time limit and caps on earnings adjustments, with lenders paid for accepting the risk. Those are proposed negotiating safeguards, not verified features of Terras's agreement.
