Anthropic demonstrates remote root access through a Linux networking bug

An unreleased research model produced the multipath TCP exploit disclosed October 27. Distribution fixes remained uneven, while an independent Mythos attempt had not produced a reliable, broadly usable version.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team reported on October 27 that an unreleased internal Claude model had written an exploit giving a remote attacker full administrative access to a Linux server, without credentials or action by its users. Nicholas Carlini and colleagues describe the result in Discovering and exploiting vulnerabilities in critical, real-world software with Claude. Their demonstration targeted servers accepting multipath TCP connections. Subsequent distribution bulletins and a separate practitioner's effort to develop a broadly usable version document delays in delivering fixes and practical constraints on the attack.

Anthropic says the model produced the exploit after initial prompting. The finished attack took 53 minutes to execute in its demonstration; that figure does not measure discovery or development time. Failed attempts left the server running, allowing further attempts. The company identifies the flaw as CVE-2026-98432 and says it had been repaired upstream and backported to maintained stable kernel branches. Its report does not identify the internal model or give a development cost for this Linux case.

The upstream repair, written by Eric Dumazet and committed October 5, credits Anthropic's Xinyang Ge with reporting the bug. It corrects a timing error in ordinary IPv4 TCP connection handling: one operation can free memory while another still uses it. The repair delays that freeing until existing readers have finished. The bug therefore has a broader scope than the particular multipath TCP configuration in Anthropic's demonstration.

The Go Security Team's October 29 advisory explains why that configuration can be present without an application's developer explicitly choosing it. Since Go 1.24, the language's networking library enables multipath TCP for listening sockets on Linux by default. The Go team warns that disabling it separately in each program is fragile: a missed listener can leave the demonstrated attack possible, and the underlying kernel bug remains. The team recommends installing a repaired kernel and leaves open whether an alternative attack could work without multipath TCP.

Distribution updates lagged behind the upstream repair

Ubuntu's tracker, last updated October 30, still marked the standard Linux kernel packages for Ubuntu 22.04, 24.04, 26.04 and 26.10 vulnerable, with fixes in progress. Red Hat's October 30 bulletin rated the vulnerability Critical, listed RHEL 8, 9 and 10 and OpenShift among affected products, and said the company was expediting fixes. Those were the statuses still displayed when checked on November 1. Red Hat treats vulnerable kernels with IPv4 TCP enabled as affected; systems accepting incoming connections are most exposed.

Debian had already announced its trixie security update on October 16. Its tracker distinguishes the repaired security packages, 6.12.113-1 for trixie and 6.1.190-1 for bookworm, from the older base packages still listed as vulnerable. A distribution name alone therefore does not establish whether an installed kernel contains the repair. On October 30, AlmaLinux put patched kernels for versions 8, 9 and 10 into its testing repository, ahead of RHEL. Its announcement explicitly reserved production release until after verification. AlmaLinux also says multipath TCP is disabled by default in its distribution, while the ordinary TCP bug still requires a fix.

Independent work exposed limits on practical exploitation

On October 31, security researcher s1r1us reported spending three days and about $2,000 working with Mythos without obtaining a reliable proof of concept suitable for broad use. They also reported that a GPT-5.6 attempt failed to obtain an initial memory disclosure. Their account concerns different models and an independent development effort, so it does not establish a controlled performance comparison with Anthropic's internal model. Nor does the stated failure establish that no part of the exploit could be reproduced.

s1r1us attributes the difficulty to unpredictable timing and network equipment that filters the traffic the attack needs. They argue that same-network attackers would have more opportunity, including in shared sandbox infrastructure. In a follow-up reply, they clarify that the attack is remote but depends on winning the timing race. These are the practitioner's assessments of feasibility; the post does not supply a measured success rate across networks.

The Go discussion about its networking default contains a substantive disagreement about how to respond. Matthieu Baerts considers Anthropic's result unlikely to work outside a laboratory, citing repeated connection attempts and filtering in real networks. Damien Neil cautions that ordinary TCP might admit other attacks, while also asking what practical benefits justify Go's default. Ian Lance Taylor argues that a kernel vulnerability potentially affecting ordinary TCP is insufficient reason to disable multipath support. The discussion separates repairing the vulnerability from deciding which networking features a language library should enable automatically.

Anthropic's April 7 assessment of Mythos Preview provides a relevant comparison. That model had found Linux memory-corruption bugs but could not exploit them individually through the kernel's defenses; it achieved local root access by combining several flaws. The same assessment reported remote root access against a FreeBSD NFS server. The October demonstration uses one Linux flaw for remote root access, although the different models and targets prevent treating the two reports as a matched benchmark.

For administrators, the vendors' advice remains tied to the installed kernel. Red Hat describes traffic filtering as a partial mitigation that can disrupt workloads and cannot replace patching. The Go team likewise warns that removing the demonstrated multipath configuration does not remove the underlying flaw. Debian's repaired security packages and AlmaLinux's testing builds illustrate why administrators must distinguish an upstream repair, a distribution's available update and the kernel actually running on a server.

IBM's JevAct checks agents' writes and give-ups before they run

Compiled policy code and a cheap decision model send flawed bookings and premature hand-offs back to the agent. Telecom-support reliability rose for every model tested, airline results were mixed, and the Telecom gain vanished under a different simulated customer.

Tool-using agents finish simulated telecom-support tasks far more consistently when an inexpensive checker reviews each record change or hand-off before it executes and sends failures back with a short explanation, according to an October 28 arXiv preprint from IBM. In “Think Before You Act: Decision Gate for LLM Agents”, Iftach Shoham, Yuval David, Segev Shlomov and five colleagues at IBM SIL describe a harness called JevAct and test it on eleven agent models. On a simulated telecom-support benchmark every agent improved, and Claude Opus 4.6 went from solving 39.5% of tasks in all three of its runs to 80.7%.

The authors start from a gap in common agent loops, which execute a tool call as soon as the model produces it. A wrong answer can be fixed in a later turn, they write, but “an unauthorized write or a premature hand-off may be harder to undo.” JevAct therefore inspects only those two kinds of proposal: writes, such as booking, cancelling or changing a setting, and hand-offs, where the agent transfers the customer to a human or says nothing more can be done. Every other step runs as in ReAct, the standard loop in which a model alternates reasoning with tool calls, and the agent keeps its own model, prompt and tools.

The checks are prepared offline. Claude Opus 4.6 compiles each domain's written policy into 12 to 20 yes/no questions for every write tool, a Boolean rule that combines the answers, a Python function that computes policy facts from tool records (dates, amounts, counts, baggage allowances) and, for hand-offs, a troubleshooting checklist. The authors single out one instruction in the compile prompt: “a condition that was never discussed is not ruled out.” Without it, the rule for enabling roaming compiled into a condition that counted as met if the customer was not traveling, which looked settled though nobody had asked; with it, the checklist gained a step telling the agent to ask whether the customer was traveling.

At runtime, code tests what the tool records establish. Questions that depend on reading the conversation, such as whether the customer requested an action or whether a troubleshooting step was tried, go to Kev-27B, a decision model: it returns a probability for each fixed answer in one forward pass and generates no text. Kev is Jared Palmer's Apache-2.0 family of decision models, built on Qwen and designed to match the API of TypeSafe AI's commercial Jev, which gives JevAct its name. Its README warns that “Kev can't subtract dates reliably, but it can use a day count it's given,” which fits the paper's choice to leave arithmetic to code. A Kev answer counts as no only when it puts the probability of yes at 5% or below. Because the judge is a separate model, the agent never grades its own proposals. “The agent still proposes actions; Kev does not choose them,” the authors write.

A flagged proposal is discarded without being executed or stored. The agent sees feedback on the failed checks or open checklist steps for that one call and proposes again. The write gate allows two revisions and the hand-off check one; after that the next proposal is committed unchecked, so, in the paper's words, “a check cannot block the agent indefinitely.”

The agents included GPT-4.1, three Claude models and open-weight models such as gpt-oss-120b, Qwen3.8-27B and DeepSeek-V4-Pro. They ran on the Airline (50 tasks) and Telecom (114 tasks) domains of τ²-bench and on 800 multi-turn entries from the function-calling benchmark BFCL, three times per task, against the same model in a plain ReAct loop. Telecom showed the largest effect: tasks solved in all three runs rose by 16.9 points on average across eight agents, and even Opus 5.5, the strongest baseline, went from 74.6% to 82.5%. In that domain, built by Victor Barres and colleagues, the simulated customer acts on their own phone while the agent talks them through troubleshooting. The IBM authors read the Telecom pattern as consistent with the hand-off check catching a frequent failure, agents giving up too early, while noting that the comparison does not isolate that check. Coding agents in ImpasseBench showed the opposite failure, persisting after the evidence said they should escalate.

Gains elsewhere were smaller. On BFCL, three-run success rose for all nine agents, by 0.1 to 9.6 points, but success in at least one run fell for five of them; the authors take this as more repeatable performance on some tasks with no wider coverage. Airline results were mixed, with three-run success improving for four of six agents and falling for two. With 50 tasks, one task moves a score by two points, and the gate seldom intervened, sending back 0.08 drafts per run for Opus 4.6.

An Airline ablation, run once per task with GPT-4.1 as the agent, suggests the compiled code does most of the work there. ReAct solved 50% of tasks and the full gate 72%; code checks alone reached 68% and Kev's questions alone 57.1%. A placebo that sent every write back once, without checking anything, gave no gain. With gpt-oss-120b as the agent, though, a plain re-check or random flags at the gate's rate raised three-run success as much as the full gate did, so whether the judge's verdicts matter beyond the extra chance to revise depends on the agent. The paper estimates Kev's judging cost at $0.0003 to $0.0017 per task, against $0.0127 to $0.0231 for Claude Haiku 4.5 or GPT-4.1 as API judges, with similar task success. Smaller Kev judges sometimes did better than Kev-27B: Opus 4.6 solved 97.4% of Telecom tasks in all three runs with Kev-9B, against 80.7% with Kev-27B, and an offline replay of hand-off decisions found nothing in decision quality to explain the gap.

The authors list several limits. Their comparisons “evaluate the full harness, not the judge in isolation.” When gpt-oss-120b replaced GPT-4.1 as the simulated customer, the Telecom gain disappeared: three-run success fell 1.8 points, with a 95% interval running from a 7.9-point loss to a 4.4-point gain. Of 101 incorrect labelled Airline write calls, 42 broke no compiled question, “because the questions encode the policy rather than the user's request”; an action can satisfy the rules and still not be what the customer asked for. The authors also revised the Telecom checklist after reviewing results and report no end-to-end latency.

JevAct extends earlier IBM work. Naama Zwerdling and colleagues at IBM Research (EMNLP 2025 Industry Track) compiled policy documents into guard code that runs before each agent action, tested on τ-bench Airlines; JevAct keeps that deterministic layer, which its own ablation credits with most of the Airline gain, and adds a judge for conversation-dependent conditions and feedback for revision. Shlomov, Shoham and colleagues' CUGA policy system earlier placed policy-as-code checkpoints in a generalist agent, including human approval for high-risk tool actions. Seongjae Kang, Taehyung Yu and Sung Ju Hwang checked actions against the dialogue with a generative verifier in PolicyGuard, then in PolicyGuide compiled policies into workflow graphs and raised τ²-bench telecom success across four runs from 0.19 to 0.61 with a GPT-5.4 verifier. The IBM paper does not test JevAct against these systems, and the setups differ.

In an independent study of Jev that bears on the paper's division of labour, Yubo Li and colleagues found it close to GPT-6 when a verdict can be read off the text but behind when the verdict must be derived, as in math, code and logic. On X, replies to a post by elvis (@omarsar0) relaying the paper took up the compiler. Anshul Karasi and Mohammad Vazin argued that generated checks need review and tests against hand-written known violations before anyone trusts the gate. The paper partly anticipates this: one round of code repair cut false alarms on held-out airline tasks from 11.3% to 1.4% of correct write calls.

Scott Alexander's case against unilateral AI exits

In “Against SURA,” Alexander supports a global pause but wants an alignment fallback if negotiations fail. His proposals face objections about developer responsibility, the timing of a pause and whether frontier companies will honor their commitments.

Scott Alexander favors a coordinated global pause on frontier AI development, potentially lasting decades. In his October 31 LessWrong essay “Against SURA”, he argues that preparing for negotiations to fail is also essential. He calls demands for concerned companies, employees and countries to stop independently “Stop Unilaterally + Raise Awareness,” or SURA. This is his grouping of positions, not a platform jointly adopted by everyone he criticizes. He fears unilateral withdrawal would leave less cautious competitors with both the strongest systems and the power to determine their use.

His fallback depends on preserving a lead for companies willing to prioritize alignment. If they cannot secure a worldwide agreement, the leading companies would jointly stop advancing capabilities for the interval before an uncooperative rival catches up. They would use their most advanced models to help solve alignment during that interval, then deploy as late as possible. Alexander acknowledges that this is dangerous and requires several things to go right. Its value depends on whether a concentrated research effort can make advanced AI safer before competitors force the decision.

Alexander's technical optimism rests on a conjecture about training. He thinks imitation during pretraining gives models helpful dispositions that later reinforcement learning can undermine. Training adjustments, monitoring and incentives might keep AI research assistants sufficiently dependable for long enough to improve alignment, perhaps through successive generations of better assistants. He does not claim to have established that this will work. He also acknowledges that MIRI researchers have developed substantive reasons for expecting near-term alignment efforts to fail. His objection is to treating that pessimism as settled without accepting or examining the reasoning behind it.

He draws on the AI Futures Project's comparison of plans, while combining its categories differently. His broad Plan A includes prolonged pauses; AIFP's narrower Plan A is a verified international slowdown with research transparency and continued, constrained scaling. His broad Plan C includes several fallbacks. The five forecasters estimate that domestic regulation under C+ improves the probability of avoiding misaligned takeover by about 20 percentage points on average relative to an almost unrestricted race. These are judgments about hypothetical futures, not measured intervention effects. Alexander infers that preparing fallback plans may contribute more to eventual survival because a global agreement is comparatively unlikely; AIFP's forecasts do not constitute endorsement of that inference or his recommendations.

Alexander also asks the AI forecasting service FutureSearch what would happen if Anthropic immediately disbanded to promote a pause. Its estimate raises global-pause probability from 16% to 19%; he combines this with an assumed loss of alignment capacity to calculate a net reduction in survival prospects. He explicitly warns that different question wordings produced different definitions and probabilities. FutureSearch also estimates a capabilities delay he translates into about six months over four years. Alexander argues that calendar time has unequal value: time with useful research tools and public attention may achieve more than an earlier interval. Neither calculation establishes the effect of an actual company withdrawal. Commenter ModelMirror adds that an AI forecaster may reproduce AIFP's published estimates, making apparent agreement correlated evidence.

Alexander directs his longest criticism at Sigal Samuel's October 27 Vox article on Anthropic's safety strategy. Samuel asks whether competing at the frontier has improved safety overall. She points to accelerated releases, weakened commitments and the pursuit of recursive self-improvement, in which AI helps build more capable AI. She argues that the catastrophic downside places the burden of proof on those continuing development. Geoffrey Irving and Daniel Kokotajlo support taking a unilateral first step in her account, followed by broader coordination. Samuel argues that China also has reasons to avoid catastrophe and that governments may move too slowly. Her proposed stop preserves existing systems, including defensive uses; Nathan Calvin specifically challenges further frontier development. It is narrower than disbanding Anthropic. Alexander's objection is that Samuel gives insufficient reason to expect other developers to stop after it does.

Alexander argues that retaining concerned people inside companies can also help secure a global pause. They can fund regulation, support public commitments and disclose failures. He acknowledges that Jacob Coxon's resignation itself raised awareness, but says Coxon's industry experience and Evan Hubinger's public support gave the warning credibility. Their positions were described in our September 9 coverage. Commenter Vaniver disputes the implication that leaving weakens advocacy: resignation produced the warning, and prominent critics outside labs also attract attention.

At the national level, Alexander argues that America's lead gives China an incentive to negotiate. He criticizes the Sanders-Casar bill for putting domestic restraint before agreement, revising his earlier belief that serious pause advocates sought only coordinated action. The proposal was announced September 3; MIRI's September 23 endorsement explains a different strategic judgment. MIRI supports the initial domestic stop while insisting that preventing superintelligence requires international action. The disagreement concerns sequencing and the risks of waiting, not whether a domestic ban alone is sufficient.

Alexander concedes that commercial incentives can corrupt judgment, but argues that activism has pressures of its own. Movements can reward simple villains and discourage discussion of competition as disloyal. He thinks assuming every company is equally untrustworthy also neglects differences that could determine whether eventual power is shared or concentrated. His answer to a moral prohibition on development compares developers with safety organizations that continue using AI despite contributing to company revenue. AdamYedidia objects that developers bear greater responsibility and are far fewer in number than consumers, making coordinated refusal more feasible. He also argues that decisions imposed on everyone must take seriously the moral rules others expect developers to follow.

Alexander's alternative begins with binding conditional commitments: each major developer agrees to stop or slow if the others worldwide do. The signatures would reveal whether the obstacle is international diplomacy, domestic regulation or a particular holdout. If agreement remains incomplete, willing leaders should commit in advance to halting dangerous training, sharing alignment research and involving outside safety groups when a specified authority identifies an approaching capability threshold. Better security would prevent theft from erasing their research lead. OpenAI's Charter already contains a narrower precedent: assisting a safety-conscious project that approaches AGI first. Alexander wants enforceable agreements and attention to competition-law barriers, not reliance on companies' favorable self-images.

The responses challenge whether waiting until near the threshold is wise. Alexander exempts AIFP's domestic pacing proposal because poor security could let rivals steal advances. AIFP researcher Eli Lifland corrects his suggestion that fixing security would justify returning to full-speed racing. Lifland favors pacing even with good security: it provides practice, reduces the danger of missing an uncertain point of no return, and could improve prospects for agreement with China. AIFP's proposal also permits continued revenue from existing systems while redirecting compute toward safety.

TsviBT proposes another alternative: retain frontier alignment research without releasing leading models, commit to a conditional indefinite stop, and fund sustained lobbying and negotiations. The comment asks why Anthropic has not devoted more visible organizational effort to securing such a stop. Alexander himself anticipates that companies may postpone a mutual pause or quarrel until it is too late. His proposals consequently depend on converting willingness to stop under favorable conditions into commitments that constrain behavior when stopping becomes costly.

AI models can contain copies of training material under copyright law even when ordinary use never reproduces it, A. Feder Cooper and James Grimmelmann argue in “The Files are in the Computer: On Copyright, Memorization, and Generative AI” (Chicago-Kent Law Review, 2025). Their 2024 analysis, revised in September 2025, addresses disputes including the New York Times’s lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft. They define memorization by whether a substantial part of a training item can be reconstructed almost exactly. Variety's Corbin Bolies reported on October 30 that the Human Consent Foundation, Creative Artists Agency and other creative-industry organizations had submitted a 31-page proposed brief challenging OpenAI's and Microsoft's arguments about licensing costs. Filed in the consolidated New York copyright litigation that includes the New York Times case, the brief argues that existing rights-clearance systems can support AI licensing while preserving creators' ability to refuse. Its supporting memorandum expressly takes no position on how the court should decide the summary-judgment motions. The coalition seeks to correct the defendants' factual premises about licensing feasibility.

The defendants make both economic and legal objections. In its September 17 books-case memorandum, Microsoft says negotiating with millions of dispersed authors is costly and no settled method exists for valuing each work's contribution to training. It argues that isolated licensing deals may reflect litigation avoidance and cannot establish an efficient market for all the data a model needs. OpenAI's memorandum similarly argues that negotiating over individual works, or groups of works, would cost more than their marginal contribution to a model. Both companies also contend that lost fees for transformative uses do not count as legally recognized market harm.

The coalition answers the transaction-cost argument with collective licensing. Its brief describes clearinghouses that identify owners, license whole repertoires and distribute payments, reducing the need to negotiate separately over every work. It cites ASCAP's licensing of more than 20 million musical works as one example. Musician and named amicus Nick Hexum explains in a statement supplied for the filing that a single recording can involve numerous songwriters, producers and publishers; collective systems already manage that complexity. The coalition argues that AI developers can adapt established practices for identifying works and clearing rights.

On valuation, the brief points to negotiated publisher fees and standardized music-licensing terms. It describes AI licensing as established for premium content and collective repertoires, with broader categories still developing. One cited deal, OpenAI's agreement with Dotdash Meredith, is confirmed by the publisher's May 2024 announcement: it covers attributed content in ChatGPT answers, use of historical and ongoing content to improve models, and collaboration on advertising technology. That combination demonstrates why a partnership's total price need not measure training rights alone. HCF proposes making permissions easier for AI systems to check. The foundation says in its memorandum that it operates the Human Consent Registry, through which machine-readable declarations can be published and queried, but does not itself license works, collect royalties or operate a marketplace. Agencies and guilds could publish defaults for the people or repertoires they represent. Balestriero and LeCun prove a version of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis for JEPAs

A text model trained on pairs of captions of the same scene matched next-token prediction's alignment with vision using 42 times fewer samples; the authors say much of the web-scale lead rests on captioner-written text.

Language models trained only on text end up with internal representations that partly line up with those of vision models, and Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun argue that the training objective, more than size, decides how closely. In “Proving and Improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs”, posted to arXiv on October 29, they prove that in an idealized setting, encoders trained separately on different kinds of data learn the same representation up to a rotation, provided each is trained to match two independent observations of the same scene. Next-token prediction, on their account, trains on the wrong pairs. Their experiments put a size on the cost: a small text model trained on pairs of captions reached the same alignment with an image model as next-token prediction on the same corpus, using 42 times fewer training samples. Balestriero is at Brown University and LeCun at New York University; both also list AMI Labs.

The hypothesis belongs to Minyoung Huh, Brian Cheung, Tongzhou Wang and Phillip Isola, whose ICML 2024 position paper showed that as vision and language models grow, they measure distances between datapoints in increasingly similar ways, and conjectured a convergence toward “a shared statistical model of reality”. Balestriero and LeCun write that the hypothesis has remained an observation. They describe Liu Ziyin and Isaac Chuang’s 2025 proof, which shows that deep linear networks trained by gradient descent learn the same representation up to a rotation, as the closest earlier result, and cite a paper by Darshil Doshi, Matthieu Wyart and colleagues on why translated sentences share representations inside multilingual language models. “We turn the hypothesis into a theorem,” the new paper says.

Good pairs and bad pairs

A joint-embedding predictive architecture, or JEPA, is the self-supervised design behind I-JEPA, a 2023 image model from Mahmoud Assran, LeCun and colleagues: the model learns to give similar embeddings to inputs labeled as a pair, with a penalty that stops every input collapsing to the same point. Balestriero and LeCun showed in a 2022 paper that methods of this kind recover classical spectral embeddings, and Jeff HaoChen, Tengyu Ma and colleagues had earlier analyzed self-supervised learning through the graph that training pairs draw over the data. The new paper applies that view to a finite world of states that each modality renders in its own way. An encoder learns the main directions of the graph its pairs draw over those states, so two modalities trained on the same pairs over the same world learn one embedding, each rotated differently. When the pairs differ, the authors bound how far the two embeddings can drift apart.

The authors then distinguish two kinds of pair. “Good views” are two observations of one scene: two captions written for the same photo, or two frames of one clip. “Bad views” are two corruptions of a single observation: two crops of one photo, or two masked spans of one caption. Bad views keep features peculiar to the modality, such as a caption’s word choice or a photo’s lighting, and the encoder spends embedding dimensions on them that no rotation can match to another modality. They recast next-token prediction as a bad-view JEPA whose pairs are a prefix and its continuation in one document, so it relates texts through shared wording. They offer this, as a heuristic argument, for why language models align with vision only partly and mainly through scale.

What the experiments show

On Flickr30k, the authors trained three text encoders from scratch that differed only in their pairs (two captions of one image, masked spans, or next-token prediction) and compared each with a separately trained image model and with a frozen DINOv2 image encoder. Caption pairs won on every measure. As a control, they trained next-token prediction on pairs of captions of the same image; its alignment barely moved, which they take to show that the objective produces the gain, independently of the information that two captions describe one picture.

At web scale they trained on texts from Recap-DataComp, Xianhang Li, Cihang Xie and colleagues’ dataset of web images recaptioned by a LLaVA model, using each image’s alt-text and its two generated captions as views. They scored alignment on Huh et al.’s WIT-1024 benchmark, which checks how often a text and its paired image have the same nearest neighbors. A 109-million-parameter text JEPA reached .109 after 21.6 million samples; next-token prediction with the same encoder reached .111 after 900 million. The authors note that they “scored no earlier next-token checkpoint” and that the runs used different learning rates and batch sizes. Counted in text tokens, the saving is 14-fold, since each good-view sample reads three texts. At matched sample counts the gap is wider.

Trained on the full 900 million samples, the 109M JEPA scored .184 against DINOv2, above Qwen2.5-72B at .154, Llama-3-70B at .151 and CLIP’s text encoder at .124. The authors call their model one with “over 600 times fewer parameters than Qwen2.5-72B” that “never saw pixels”. Their own limitations section names the main qualification: two of each image’s three texts were written by an image captioner whose vision encoder is CLIP, so they carry information distilled from image-text training. With only alt-text and a text-only paraphrase, good views reached .060 on WIT against .107. The authors conclude that the comparisons with language models and CLIP depend on those captioner-written texts, while the comparisons between kinds of pairs, run on identical texts, do not. They also report that a fourth seed of the large run reached only .121, leaving a four-seed average of .168 that still beats every language model tested, and that hyperparameters were tuned only for the good-view model.

Two further results test the theory’s predictions. A scaling law in the share of good views and the number of samples predicted 85 runs before they were trained, with errors about as large as the difference between two seeds of one run, though it overestimated good-view runs at 320 million samples. The authors also built an image captioner from a frozen DINOv2, one fitted rotation and a caption decoder trained on text alone; the rotation is the only part fitted on image-caption pairs. That approach extends an older finding, from Tomas Mikolov, Quoc Le and Ilya Sutskever and later Alexis Conneau and colleagues, that word embeddings trained on different languages can be translated by a linear map or a rotation. Using the pair-free alignment method of Dominik Schnaus, Isola and colleagues unchanged, the text JEPA reached 64.4% top-5 accuracy on ImageNet-100, against at most 56% in that paper, though CLIP’s text encoder still did better. The code is MIT-licensed with a registry of 345 training runs; the repository lists no model checkpoints.

Algebraic World Model learns game rules and proves what follows from them

Pan and colleagues verified 183 winning replays and proved two modified levels impossible under learned rules. Wrong initial bounds and later engine checks show where formal deduction still depends on empirical testing.

An AI agent can learn explicit rules for an unfamiliar game and construct machine-checked proofs that no sequence of moves wins under those rules. Guancheng Pan of Zhejiang University, Qixin Tan, Yang Yuan and Andrew Chi-Chih Yao demonstrate this in Algebraic World Model: From Exploration to Proofs, posted to arXiv on October 29. Their system verified winning replays for 183 levels and proved two specially modified levels impossible within their budgets. Two initially incorrect cost bounds expose the distinction the authors investigate: a proof can follow correctly from a learned model whose rules misdescribe the game.

The Algebraic World Model, or AWM, starts with exploration. A language-model agent records hypotheses as Python code, with supporting observations and checks against recorded state changes. If a hypothesis contradicts an observation or its checker fails, the system blocks further actions until the agent revises or withdraws it. The agent then reorganizes its hypotheses into objects and shared rules, using transformations guided by category theory. Each transformation requires a supporting Lean statement and must preserve the distinctions tested against recorded and deliberately corrupted transitions. Finally, the agent formalizes the model in Lean 4, whose proof-checking kernel verifies deductions from the stated rules.

The running example, lp85, makes the method concrete. Buttons move colored tiles; each goal slot must receive its matching color. AWM first wrote eleven hypotheses about four button mechanisms, then recognized that all four rearrange tile positions and replaced the separate descriptions with one shared rule. Because rearrangement preserves the number of tiles of each color, removing the only tile of a required color makes the modeled goal unreachable. The released Lean project connects that invariant to an impossibility theorem and separately provides winning witnesses for the original levels. Its replay theorems compute each successive state from the initial screen and compare it with the recorded play. Budget-display rules remain outside this Lean model.

On all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games, using Claude Opus 5, AWM cleared every level with an official score of 99.9. Lean replayed all 183 certified winning trajectories against the learned rules and checked their final goals. The certified runs used 7,448 actions, 43 percent of the human baseline’s 17,135. That ratio excludes exploratory play and unsuccessful sessions: counting everything sent to the game engine gives 24,604 actions, and certified clears followed uncertified sessions in 22 games. The authors also tested frozen rules on unseen transitions and modified games, finding that some rules generalized to changed layouts and detected changed mechanics. These checks provide additional empirical evidence about particular rules.

AWM’s score is competitive with other systems that execute code. Haoyu Zhao and colleagues’ Memento 3, which the authors identify as their closest comparison, already cleared every public level with a score of 100.0. It maintains a written rulebook, compiles it into code and accepts updates after checking their agreement with observations. The much lower bare-model scores in AWM’s table come from a standard setup without code execution, so that comparison cannot isolate the contribution of AWM’s formal reasoning.

For the impossibility experiment, the researchers modified two levels of sc25, a grid game in which a block moves and casts spells to reach an exit. They set budgets of 18 and 35, one unit below the minimum winning costs of 19 and 36 established by exhaustive engine search. AWM, now using Claude Opus 5.5, produced closed Lean proofs in all six runs, three per level. The proofs exclude every legal winning sequence under the learned models, including sequences never tried. Twelve baseline runs of Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra with coding tools produced no such proofs. AWM additionally received its structured procedure, a generic Lean skeleton and rule-checking tools, so this tests complete agent configurations.

Four of AWM’s six initial bounds matched the engine’s minimum costs. The other two, 20 and 47, followed from overly restrictive rules that excluded cheaper legal moves. Told only that a cheaper win existed, without its cost or route, AWM repaired both models within four further attempts and proved revised bounds. In one level, growing the block onto fuel collected it; the original rule missed that refund. In the other, terrain blocked a shot that the model had wrongly treated as limited in range. The corrected level-five bound was 37 under a convention that charged for the winning move, equivalent to the engine’s 36. The authors apply that accounting allowance to every agent.

The researchers then checked whether the model proofs extended to the actual games. Appendix C.5 reports five successful transfers out of six, using a fixed opening and every engine-generated transition reachable within the sealed budget. Some certificates were repaired or completed, with costs translated to account for prepaid actions, refunds and the free winning move. The authors checked that the resulting invariant held initially, survived each permitted transition and excluded a win. One level-four invariant still failed because its fuel-collection assumptions disagreed with real transitions. These were subsequent certificate audits, not additional autonomous proofs: offline programs established graph extraction and correspondence with the engine outside an end-to-end Lean verification. The fixed-opening audit also has a narrower scope than the four-opening search that established the reference costs.

The authors’ error-injection tests show which mistakes formal checking catches. Lean-based verification detected invalid proof certificates, mismatches between Python and Lean, and reversed bounds; a separate statement audit flagged theorems made trivially true by impossible premises. Neither caught the deliberately incorrect game rules or references to a wrong goal or model version. Explicit proof dependencies nevertheless helped an agent investigate faulty rules: on level four, providing the list of rules used in a proof raised successful counterexample discovery from 16 of 42 runs to 29. The same intervention helped much less on the harder level five.

Independent checking of impossibility has a precedent in Unsolvability Certificates for Classical Planning, by Salomé Eriksson, Gabriele Röger and Malte Helmert at the University of Basel. Their 2017 work let a checker validate that a supplied planning task had no solution. AWM additionally has to learn the model that its proof uses. Its released models and proof records make those assumptions inspectable, but translating screens into states still happens in Python outside Lean. The experiments concern discrete, deterministic games with immediate feedback; richer hidden state, delayed feedback and random dynamics remain untested.

AlDahoul and Zaki find an effect below one percentage point in two models using Tülu-3 training. In OLMo-2 32B, supervised fine-tuning reverses how an existing job-status signal influences answers.

Training can change whether a poster's job makes a language model more or less likely to believe a claim. Nouar AlDahoul and Yasir Zaki of New York University Abu Dhabi find a small shift toward doubting lower-status posters in two models trained with the Allen Institute for AI's Tülu-3 recipe. They report it in From Leniency to Suspicion: How an Open Post-Training Recipe Changes Whom LLM Fact-Checkers Believe, an arXiv preprint submitted October 28. The effect is below one percentage point and appears in the tested Tülu-3-trained models but not the tested Meta, Alibaba or Mistral recipes. None of seven commercial models showed greater suspicion of lower-status posters.

The researchers presented PolitiFact claims as social-media posts accompanied by invented account profiles. They took true and mostly true claims, and false and pants-on-fire claims, from LIAR-PLUS, asking the models for a true-or-false judgment. Profiles varied occupation, education and other personal details, including irrelevant ones such as star sign and favourite colour. The main measurement was the probability assigned to false, comparing the same claim under different profiles. The occupational contrast was between service or manual work and professional work. In a later experiment isolating the mechanism, the same poster became a line cook, warehouse lead, accountant or logistics executive, with everything else held fixed.

A model had to pass basic checks before its result counted: produce usable answers, distinguish true from false claims, and express more doubt about an account with two previous false-rated posts. The authors also measured how training changed responses to irrelevant profile details. An occupational effect had to exceed that background movement. Six model families entered the comparison; Google's Gemma 3 27B failed the check involving previous false posts in the explicit-profile format, so its apparent job effects were not interpretable under the authors' rules.

Tülu-3 70B and Meta's Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct begin with the same pretrained model. Only the Tülu-3 version developed increased job-based doubt beyond the measured background drift. OLMo-2 32B uses a different pretrained base but shares Tülu-3 post-training data, and showed the effect too. Its result also held on fresh claims and in a later run with new profile details. All families improved at distinguishing true claims from false ones, so the occupational shift accompanied better overall fact-checking. The effect depended partly on presentation: OLMo-2 showed it with natural account biographies, but not with explicit occupation fields in the cross-family comparison.

To investigate how the shift happened, AlDahoul and Zaki examined OLMo-2 32B before assistant training, after supervised fine-tuning on example chats, after preference training and in its final version. They measured the average difference in internal activity between otherwise identical prompts naming lower- and higher-status jobs. That difference defined a direction representing the job-status contrast. Simple classifiers could already identify the job from the base model's activity almost perfectly. Training left that information detectable and the direction largely similar, while changing how it affected the answer.

The authors then intervened on that activity, adding positive or negative amounts along the direction and comparing the resulting answer shifts with equal-sized pushes in unrelated directions. In the base model, a push toward the lower-status representation increased belief in the claim; after training it increased doubt. A push scaled to the naturally occurring difference approximately reproduced the behavioural gap at both stages. In OLMo-2, the reversal appeared at supervised fine-tuning, while later preference training strengthened the internal response. Tülu-3 70B's job effect instead accumulated across training stages; the detailed internal intervention was performed only in OLMo-2 32B.

The initial exploratory run narrowly missed its prespecified statistical test. Its pattern led the authors to fix a range of layers and five predictions before testing 400 new claims, where all five held. Those rules were dated and checksummed, but not publicly registered. The intervention shows that manipulating the direction can reproduce the difference. It does not establish that removing the direction eliminates it: directional ablation is explicitly future work. The paper cites Andy Arditi and colleagues' Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction as a precedent for that further test.

In the OLMo-2 experiment, about 2% of otherwise matched pairs received different true-or-false answers depending on the job. Before training, those differences favoured lower-status posters; afterward, they were approximately balanced. Training therefore mainly removed leniency in categorical decisions, while probability scores moved into slight suspicion. The distinction also affects the commercial comparison: six of the seven models supplied numerical ratings because their APIs did not expose token probabilities. None showed increased doubt toward lower-status posters. Only Claude Haiku 4.5 showed leniency after correction for multiple comparisons. These were deployed snapshots tested October 10, without base checkpoints from which to infer a training effect.

Earlier work had already shown that speaker descriptions can alter fact-checking. Razieh Chalehchaleh and colleagues' Unequal Verdicts changed gendered job titles attached to LIAR claims and found inconsistent labels across all six tested models. AlDahoul and Zaki add comparisons between training recipes and direct intervention inside one model. The related Yao study covered in our October 30 issue examined deference and conversational yielding across open-model checkpoints. It studied a different behaviour, despite sharing OLMo-2 and Tülu-3 lineages.

The cause within the training data remains unresolved. The authors searched the 866,138-conversation mixture used for OLMo-2 32B. Lower-status jobs appeared much less often than professional jobs, but neither word searches nor a model-based screen identified a source systematically pairing lower-status speakers with doubtful replies. The latter screen was unreliable on long conversations. Retraining with and without candidate data remains a proposed test.

The paper records several misleading early results: rescaling the baseline created an apparent bias change absent from the raw comparison, and a favourite-colour emoji shifted responses as much as the occupational cue. Its recommendations follow those failures: test against irrelevant cues, require evidence that the model can perform the task, and confirm exploratory findings on fresh claims. The mechanism remains limited to one model, one position in its computation and linear interventions; synthetic occupations and English, US-centred claims also restrict its scope. The authors have not established the same effect in real social-media accounts.

The resulting models were deliberately trained for this experiment and never deployed.

Each attacking agent had code-execution tools and a service for fine-tuning a separate small Claude model. The report does not identify the main attacker or target by a released model name. They also had the exact classifier used to screen the examples, so they could practice against it before submitting. Many agents worked in parallel across successive rounds, reading a shared log of previous attempts and refining one another’s discoveries.

The classifier assessed each example independently. Submissions failed if more than 10 percent of examples, or more than 100 in total, were flagged; smaller flag rates still reduced the score. For the main results in Figure 5, the researchers went further: they removed every flagged example and retrained the models on the remainder. The reported effects therefore survived that additional filtering. Anthropic describes this classifier as similar to monitors used in production, where training-data screening is one of several defenses.

Two other tests, involving code sabotage and an apparent offer to copy the model’s weights elsewhere, were held out from that optimization score. Grasso argues AI can shape inquiry even when users distrust its answers

Practical entrustment describes dependence on how AI selects questions and sources before users evaluate them. Grasso proposes independent opportunities to challenge that framing, with responsibility assigned to those who control how systems are adopted.

An AI system can determine which questions and sources a researcher considers even when that researcher distrusts its answers, Valeriano Grasso argues in “A-ffidarsi: Practical Entrustment in AI-Mediated Inquiry”. Grasso is at the University of Bergamo; the paper appeared in Philosophy & Technology on October 31. His account concerns how repeated use can make a system's selection and framing part of the background of inquiry. Checking its answers then takes place within a field of possibilities it has already helped define.

Grasso's central example is a researcher asking a language model to map an unfamiliar field. The model identifies canonical authors, central distinctions and standard objections. The researcher checks its citations and questions its proposed framework. Yet an omitted author or an entirely different way of posing the problem may never become available for scrutiny. Grasso acknowledges that disciplinary canons, curricula and citation practices already organize attention. AI can reproduce and amplify those patterns quickly and individually, while supplying both the initial map and the material subsequently assessed within it.

He calls the resulting dependence practical entrustment, adapting the Italian verb affidarsi. Its defining feature is the role the system comes to occupy, regardless of whether the user trusts, distrusts or suspends judgment about it. Repeated use can produce that role, but frequency alone does not establish it. Grasso proposes three signs: the system establishes relevance before evaluation begins; users cease attending to how it selected the material; and a failure exposes a dependence that had become inconspicuous. A fabricated reference or service outage can disrupt an entire inquiry because the system was organizing the activity as well as supplying particular answers.

Grasso develops this account through Heidegger's analysis of tools withdrawing from attention in use. A craftsperson attends to the work while taking the functioning hammer for granted; breakdown makes the tool conspicuous. AI complicates the comparison because it can help determine the purpose and questions of the activity itself. Merleau-Ponty's account of acquired bodily skills and Dreyfus's account of skilled coping help Grasso describe practical orientation that need not consist in a set of beliefs. He explicitly limits the analogy: AI is not literally incorporated into the body like a cane, and dependence need not become wholly nonpropositional to qualify.

He also draws on Susan Leigh Star's account of infrastructure to explain why individual habits are insufficient. Institutions select systems, normalize their use and adjust expectations around their outputs. A department can make AI-generated field summaries the usual starting point for research even though each researcher retains distinct views about reliability. Grasso therefore examines shared conditions of inquiry, extending beyond the question of whether an external resource forms part of one individual's cognitive apparatus.

His comparison with trust theories is deliberately specific. C. Thi Nguyen's account of trust as an unquestioning attitude already includes rankings and conceptual frameworks, not merely isolated factual claims. Grasso accepts that breadth. He nevertheless argues that a questioning researcher can remain dependent on which alternatives a system makes available. Annalisa Coliva's account of hinge trust concerns the background openness that makes language and inquiry possible at all. Grasso instead considers a contingent change in the practices of already competent inquirers. He also distinguishes practical entrustment from automation bias, which concerns excessive weight given to an answer within a decision space, and outsourcing, which usually begins with an already specified task.

For Grasso, this dependence need not itself be a failure. Its consequences depend on whether people retain opportunities to challenge how inquiry is organized. He adapts José Medina's idea of beneficial epistemic friction into a requirement for AI design and institutional adoption. Additional effort counts only when it permits relevant challenges. A confirmation box dismissed unread offers little protection. Another framing generated by the same model can help, but remains a partial safeguard because the same process selected it.

His literature-review example makes the stronger requirement concrete. A student checking every AI-supplied claim may never encounter material outside its selection. A student with an independently assembled bibliography, a reading group and supervisor feedback has several opportunities to discover an overlooked objection. Those sources also organize attention, but none exclusively controls both the initial framing and every means of questioning it. Grasso asks institutions to preserve that independence, rather than treating additional checking of the same material as sufficient.

His design proposals include asking users to formulate their problem before receiving the model's organization of it, exposing alternatives and keeping source provenance traceable. Institutional practice should include independently selected texts or searches and human checkpoints at which the framing itself can be questioned. Users should be able to explain why they adopted one framing over another. In education, he proposes asking learners for their current understanding or attempted answer before supplying a summary. That staged access still needs comparison with perspectives obtained independently of the system.

Responsibility follows control over these conditions. Designers answer for foreseeable effects of removing friction; institutions answer for procurement, configuration and adoption; users remain responsible for checks within their reach. An institution that requires independent comparison differs from one that installs the same tool without such opportunities, even though neither developed it. Grasso presents these obligations as candidates for institutional requirements and regulation. For research authorship, he also proposes disclosure of AI's role in choosing sources, objections and categories, since an author can retain a model's framing while forgetting how it entered their reasoning.

The paper develops a philosophical account through illustrative cases and explicitly calls for empirical validation of its proposed criteria. A related 2021 study by Zana Buçinca, Maja Barbara Malaya and Krzysztof Gajos tested interventions that required more deliberation around AI recommendations. Its 199-participant experiment found reduced overreliance relative to simple explanation interfaces, with less favorable user ratings for the most effective interventions. That result concerns decisions about presented recommendations; it does not test Grasso's claim about alternatives excluded before evaluation. It does identify a practical difficulty for his proposals: preserving useful opportunities to think can conflict with users' preference for convenience.

The Files are in the Computer: On Copyright, Memorization, and Generative AI

A. Feder Cooper, James Grimmelmann

The New York Times's copyright lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft alleges OpenAI's GPT models have "memorized" NYT articles. Other lawsuits make similar claims. But parties, courts, and scholars disagree on what memorization is, whether it is taking place, and what its copyright implications are. These debates are clouded by ambiguities over the nature of "memorization." We attempt to bring clarity to the conversation. We draw on the technical literature to provide a firm foundation for legal discussions, providing a precise definition of memorization: a model has "memorized" a piece of training data when (1) it is possible to reconstruct from the model (2) a near-exact copy of (3) a substantial portion of (4) that piece of training data. We distinguish memorization from "extraction" (user intentionally causes a model to generate a near-exact copy), from "regurgitation" (model generates a near-exact copy, regardless of user intentions), and from "reconstruction" (the near-exact copy can be obtained from the model by any means). Several consequences follow. (1) Not all learning is memorization. (2) Memorization occurs when a model is trained; regurgitation is a symptom not its cause. (3) A model that has memorized training data is a "copy" of that training data in the sense used by copyright. (4) A model is not like a VCR or other general-purpose copying technology; it is better at generating some types of outputs (possibly regurgitated ones) than others. (5) Memorization is not a phenomenon caused by "adversarial" users bent on extraction; it is latent in the model itself. (6) The amount of training data that a model memorizes is a consequence of choices made in training. (7) Whether or not a model that has memorized actually regurgitates depends on overall system design. In a very real sense, memorized training data is in the model--to quote Zoolander, the files are in the computer.

Structural Collateralism: Architecture, Function, and the Co-production of Harm

Giulio Vidotto

Recurrent non-primary harms persist in complex intervention systems across domains as diverse as precision warfare, pharmacological development, and large language models, even when they are acknowledged, measured, and explicitly targeted for reduction. Such persistence often reflects a structural property of the systems themselves rather than poor implementation or insufficient local correction. I introduce structural collateralism to designate configurations in which non-primary effects are co-generated by the same architectural mechanisms that produce the primary outcome and cannot be materially reduced without modifying the system’s underlying logic. Three diagnostic conditions distinguish structural collateralism from trade-offs, side effects, or externalities: co-generation (primary and non-primary effects arise from the same mechanisms), normality (effects occur under routine operation, not exceptional failure), and architectural dependence (reduction requires redesigning objectives or couplings, not correcting outputs). Drawing on systems theory (Perrow; Meadows) and structural responsibility (Young), I develop operational criteria for identifying this property and apply them comparatively across three domains. I also examine two related phenomena. Collateralism laundering describes how institutions normalize structurally co-generated harms through metrics and procedural closure. Systemic collateralism addresses configurations in which multiple interdependent entities jointly sustain the co-generation and persistence of collateral effects. If collateral effects are structural, accountability cannot rest on isolated errors; governance must address generative architectures. I outline three orientations: constrained objectives, reduced coupling, and institutionalized mechanisms for architectural revision. I argue that structural collateralism clarifies when operational claims of precision are incompatible with the very interdependencies on which those systems rely.
