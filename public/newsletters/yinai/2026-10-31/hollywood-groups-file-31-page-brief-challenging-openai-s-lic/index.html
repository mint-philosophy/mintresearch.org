Hollywood coalition argues AI licensing can work at scale

HCF and CAA cite collective licensing and consent systems in a proposed brief that challenges the defendants’ factual claims without asking the court to decide the motions either way.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 31 October 2026 979 words · ~5 min

Variety's Corbin Bolies reported on October 30 that the Human Consent Foundation, Creative Artists Agency and other creative-industry organizations had submitted a 31-page proposed brief challenging OpenAI's and Microsoft's arguments about licensing costs. Filed in the consolidated New York copyright litigation that includes the New York Times case, the brief argues that existing rights-clearance systems can support AI licensing while preserving creators' ability to refuse. Its supporting memorandum expressly takes no position on how the court should decide the summary-judgment motions. The coalition seeks to correct the defendants' factual premises about licensing feasibility.

The defendants make both economic and legal objections. In its September 17 books-case memorandum, Microsoft says negotiating with millions of dispersed authors is costly and no settled method exists for valuing each work's contribution to training. It argues that isolated licensing deals may reflect litigation avoidance and cannot establish an efficient market for all the data a model needs. OpenAI's memorandum similarly argues that negotiating over individual works, or groups of works, would cost more than their marginal contribution to a model. Both companies also contend that lost fees for transformative uses do not count as legally recognized market harm.

The coalition answers the transaction-cost argument with collective licensing. Its brief describes clearinghouses that identify owners, license whole repertoires and distribute payments, reducing the need to negotiate separately over every work. It cites ASCAP's licensing of more than 20 million musical works as one example. Musician and named amicus Nick Hexum explains in a statement supplied for the filing that a single recording can involve numerous songwriters, producers and publishers; collective systems already manage that complexity. The coalition argues that AI developers can adapt established practices for identifying works and clearing rights.

On valuation, the brief points to negotiated publisher fees and standardized music-licensing terms. It describes AI licensing as established for premium content and collective repertoires, with broader categories still developing. One cited deal, OpenAI's agreement with Dotdash Meredith, is confirmed by the publisher's May 2024 announcement: it covers attributed content in ChatGPT answers, use of historical and ongoing content to improve models, and collaboration on advertising technology. That combination demonstrates why a partnership's total price need not measure training rights alone. The coalition offers such agreements as evidence that licensing can develop, while Microsoft disputes their relevance to the much larger volume of ordinary internet material.

HCF proposes making permissions easier for AI systems to check. The foundation says in its memorandum that it operates the Human Consent Registry, through which machine-readable declarations can be published and queried, but does not itself license works, collect royalties or operate a marketplace. Its proposed system lets rights holders permit training or generation, prohibit uses, or specify conditions such as payment. Agencies and guilds could publish defaults for the people or repertoires they represent. The brief compares this approach with established content-identification and rights-management systems, including YouTube's Content ID.

The Human Consent Standard specification places concrete limits on that proposal's readiness. Dated August 8, 2026, it is a draft explicitly barred from production implementation. Registry authorization procedures, identifier administration and the associated licensing-server protocol remain unfinished. Its rights-administration section also requires owners and representatives to resolve ownership shares, territorial rights and competing permissions before publishing a consistent declaration. The software format communicates the resulting position; it does not settle those disputes. HCF's account of an operating registry and the draft's unfinished requirements therefore describe different parts of the proposed infrastructure.

The coalition also defends refusal as part of a licensing market. It argues that the absence of a machine-readable restriction cannot itself establish consent, while declining to address the particular implied-license defenses in the litigation. Its conclusion rejects compulsory licensing or an automatic royalty that would let developers use any work upon payment. Under the approach it advocates, rights holders could decline altogether or negotiate separate terms for valuable assets.

The brief connects those practical arguments to the fourth fair-use factor, which considers effects on a work's market or value. It invokes American Geophysical Union v. Texaco, where the Second Circuit treated the Copyright Clearance Center's workable licensing system as relevant to the harm from institutional photocopying of journal articles. That decision counted potential licensing revenue but warned that the availability of payment cannot conclusively defeat fair use. Showing that permission can be obtained therefore does not by itself establish that copyright law requires it for a particular use.

Two California rulings cited by the defendants address that separate legal question. In the June 2025 Kadrey v. Meta order, Judge Vince Chhabria rejected lost training-license fees as harm for a transformative use, while expressly anticipating that collective licensing could develop where permission was required. Meta won on the evidence presented by those plaintiffs; Chhabria emphasized their failure to substantiate harm from competing AI-generated works. In Bartz v. Anthropic, Judge William Alsup assumed that a training-license market could develop but held that the authors were not entitled to control that use. His ruling distinguished training from acquisition of pirated library copies. Neither district-court decision binds the New York court, and neither establishes that licensing is impractical.

The proposed HCF brief is also distinct from Wikimedia's filing, discussed in the October 30 issue, which argued for different treatment of pretraining and reproduction through retrieval or chatbot answers. Bolies separately reports that the entertainment unions filed their own brief the preceding day, warning about damage to negotiated protections for creative workers.

For OpenAI's response, a spokesperson directed Variety to its October 30 post Fair use benefits everyone. The company presents supporting submissions from authors, academics, libraries and other organizations as evidence that applying fair use to AI training benefits creators and users. The post promotes a general defense of training and attacks the Times' lawsuit; it does not specifically answer HCF's licensing-infrastructure examples. Microsoft did not respond to Variety's immediate request for comment.
