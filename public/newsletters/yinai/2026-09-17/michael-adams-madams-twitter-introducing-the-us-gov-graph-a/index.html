CivLab's US Gov Graph displays 482 federal organizations and their officeholders

CivLab says AI helps maintain its federal government map. The site counts 86 acting officials; its underlying data supplies legal citations for 395 of 1,433 relationships, with uneven documentation of the changes it records.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 17 September 2026 1120 words · ~6 min

On the evening of 16 September, Michael Adams of CivLab, the San Francisco group that built a map of that city's government, posted a four-part thread on X introducing the US Gov Graph, which he called "a complete map of the people and positions of power" in the federal government. Adams described using AI to build and maintain models of governments across America, labelled the effort "Palantir for The People", and closed with a call for engineers and designers to join him in San Francisco. An hour later he submitted it to Hacker News himself. By early on 18 September the launch post had about 8,700 likes and 1,400 reposts; IBTimes UK covered the launch on 17 September.

The site, at graph.civlab.org/us, describes its model as "entities, positions, people, and the legal relationships between them". As of 18 September its overview counted 482 organizations, 343 of them in the executive branch, 103 independent, 19 legislative and 17 judicial, with 339 classed as sub-agencies, plus the 535 members of Congress; IBTimes UK reported the same figures a day earlier. Organization pages combine descriptions, officeholders and recent news. The Department of Justice entry, for example, cites the Act of June 22, 1870 and links to a legal source and official website; those links are not present for every organization. CivLab says its agents monitor official sources for appointments, departures and structural changes, and on 18 September it reported 6 vacant seats and 86 acting officials "serving unconfirmed". Its log held 70 changes since 23 June, all but one of them personnel moves; the exception recorded the 10 July merger of the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management and the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement into a new Marine Minerals Administration, a change confirmed in an Interior Department announcement.

The page's underlying data, as served on 18 September, holds 952 nodes and 1,433 relationships between them, including appointments, department-head links, confirmations and ex officio seats. Of those relationships, 395 carry a legal citation, some with the statutory text attached, and 321 record a statute in a separate basis field; 1,056 leave that field blank, and 172 are attributed to a "complete-government scaffold". The underlying data contains 486 department, commission and advisory-body records, a different count from the overview's 482 organizations. Of those 486 records, 209 link to a legal source and 222 to an official website. These fields measure recorded documentation; a blank field does not establish that an office or relationship lacks legal authority. The change log mixes source links with internal batch identifiers and shorthand descriptions: 21 of its 69 personnel entries have source fields beginning with a web address, while 29 carry batch identifiers. Neither the launch thread nor the site's About panel explains which models perform the monitoring, how sources are selected or whether people review proposed changes; the historical log alone cannot establish autonomous detection. On Hacker News Adams said that "The app took about a week, the data took months" and, when a commenter objected that the map was not complete, gave the inclusion rule as "anything that operates as a distinct, continuous entity with a legal basis outside itself".

The federal graph is CivLab's second. Adams launched the San Francisco Government Graph on 27 August 2024, writing that the city had no org chart until he made one in July 2023 and no complete list of entities until June 2024, and that "every entity is linked to the legal source enabling it". He announced funding for a rebuild in March 2025 without naming the funder or the amount; Sanctuary Computer and the studio XXIX rebuilt the site, and Adams relaunched it on 27 August 2025, exactly a year after the first launch, crediting LLMs for letting a small team process the data and promising a version for every city. His essay "Rebuilding the Civic Industrial Base" gives the theory behind the tools: he argues that state capacity is the output of an ecosystem of citizens and local bodies, using San Francisco's 2024 restrictions on police pretext stops as an example. He credits the seven-member Police Commission, working with advocacy and neighborhood groups, and says most residents have never heard of the body.

The federal government already maintains directories covering organizations and senior positions. The Office of the Federal Register's United States Government Manual, the official handbook of the federal government, describes a typical agency entry as including its legislative or executive authority and the officials heading its major units. The PLUM Act of 2022 required the Office of Personnel Management to maintain a public website listing policy and supporting positions, including incumbents and vacancies, and the Government Accountability Office reported on 26 February 2026 that its review of the 2025 data found at least seven federal entities and at least 130 presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed positions missing; IBTimes UK connected the launch to that audit. Under the Federal Vacancies Reform Act, acting service generally has a 210-day limit, with additional periods tied to nominations. Presidential transitions and office-specific statutes can alter the rules. Assessing compliance requires the office's governing law and vacancy and nomination dates; the graph's aggregate acting count cannot establish unlawful service. The graph also serves congressional portraits from the @unitedstates project, whose public-domain image collection uses congressional Bioguide identifiers. The site's About panel does not credit that project.

Replies mostly asked for layers the graph does not carry. Leon Ray, whose reply had 35 likes when checked on 18 September, proposed "separating formal authority from real coordination power" by mapping the data, budgets and implementation chokepoints that connect offices, which he said would turn "a static org chart into a testable model"; another reader wrote that a map only helps if "every claim keeps provenance and an owner for the next update". One reader asked for funding, contributions, net worth and voting records for members of Congress, and Adams answered "Good notes, we could add it!"; a request to open-source the project had no Adams reply in the conversation retrieved on 18 September. Asked whether the graph leans toward one party, Adams replied that the government's structure comes from law and requires no partisan affiliation. The Palantir line drew objections: Jordan Lee argued that a Palantir for the people is "just... a public dataset", useful because "nobody else is doing it well", but that the framing and the recruiting pitch show the project is "a tool aimed at" power, and Patrick (@patrickmcavoy) called the name "radioactive". Kifah Meeran, Adams's collaborator since 2024, expects it to become "a proper tool" within four years, and when one reader urged Adams to "resist going federal", he replied that San Francisco already exists and more cities are coming.
