AI for Science and Research Verification

Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster claim to have proved that a three-dimensional fluid flow can start out smooth and develop a singularity in finite time, without an external force. As Scientific American reports, OpenAI’s September proof required a force chosen to drive the fluid toward that point. Alpöge and Buckmaster used AI to develop their argument and released much of it in Lean, software for checking proofs. Their November 20 release lacks the file of numerical values the proof relies on and the calculation needed to verify them.

Alpöge and Buckmaster claim a Navier-Stokes blowup without an external force

The mathematicians used Claude to construct a flow that they say loses smoothness on its own. Their November 20 release includes a formal proof of conditional results. It leaves out the numerical data needed to check that the conditions hold.

A fluid can start in smooth motion and develop a singularity with nothing pushing it from outside, Levent Alpöge of Anthropic and Tristan Buckmaster of New York University claim. In their November 20 manuscript, “Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations,” they describe an initially smooth velocity field whose variation grows without bound in a finite time. It would be enough to find one such flow to overturn the conjecture that smooth motion always stays smooth in these equations. The authors published the mathematics and a machine-checkable account of much of the argument. The numerical file on which their example depends was missing from the release.

As Peter Hall reported in Scientific American, Buckmaster announced the result the same morning that Alpöge separately claimed a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. Buckmaster had accused OpenAI of racing to publish a competing fluid result in September. OpenAI denied wrongdoing, and Joseph Howlett reported in October that mathematicians who examined the approaches thought they differed. In his November 20 announcement, Buckmaster said he feared being scooped again and had decided to publish while the write-up was still rough. He also linked a separate proof with Matei Coiculescu about the Euler equations, which describe a fluid without viscosity. The Navier-Stokes claim concerns a fluid with viscosity, which tends to smooth out differences in velocity.

OpenAI’s September construction began with a fluid at rest. A carefully chosen external force drove it to a singularity, as we reported on September 8. Charles Fefferman’s official statement for the Clay Mathematics Institute allows this in two of its four formulations, one for ordinary three-dimensional space and one for a periodic box, where the flow repeats across opposite faces. The other two ask whether smooth initial motion can always continue smoothly when the force is zero. A case of unforced breakdown in each setting would answer both questions, and would count as an example for the formulations that permit a force. Alpöge and Buckmaster claim to have constructed both.

The difference between the two constructions had already been worked out in part. In September, Peter Constantin, Mihaela Ignatova and Vlad Vicol showed that the force in OpenAI’s example could not vanish near the point of collapse. Their proof also covers other flows with two of its features, including a central region that looks the same after any rotation around an axis. As Howlett reported at the time, the unforced problem that many specialists had in mind would take a different solution.

The new manuscript returns to an idea Jean Leray proposed in 1934. If a collapsing flow kept its shape as it shrank, a mathematician could describe the shape once and use it at every scale. Later work ruled out that possibility under natural conditions. Alpöge and Buckmaster instead look for a flow that repeats its shape only at a succession of smaller scales. Between repetitions it can change shape or rotate. Their numerical approximation has a central column and a layer that divides into nineteen spiral arms. A rotation of one nineteenth of a turn about the vertical axis preserves the pattern.

The authors need to show that an exact solution has the properties they see on the computer. There is precedent in Jiajie Chen and Thomas Hou’s work on the Euler equations, where rigorous bounds on the errors in a numerical approximation helped prove blowup in a domain with a boundary. Here the authors give inequalities which, if satisfied by their numerical data, guarantee a true repeating profile near the approximation. A second argument takes a profile of that kind and constructs smooth starting flows that approach it as they break down. The numerical certificate contains the data to which those inequalities must be applied.

Alpöge and Buckmaster explain in their guide to the Lean proof that the formalization came first. They gave Claude their earlier work and possible avenues to explore, then directed it through revisions of a written proof generated from the formal one. The final versions differ in mathematically relevant details. The paper gives a rate of blowup that the corresponding formal statement omits. One formal theorem yields a weaker kind of profile than the smooth one in the paper. The authors explain how the formal library uses that weaker result to reach a breakdown conclusion in each setting. Conversely, two inequalities in the appendix have proofs only in Lean.

The November 20 repository records exactly what readers could check. The paper prints a digital fingerprint that a reader can use to identify the promised file when it appears. To check that its numbers satisfy the inequalities, a reader also needs the separate calculation that bounds the effects of rounding. Neither accompanied the paper. The authors also say that they changed some formal statements to match the appendix and had yet to rebuild the patched Lean package when they assembled the release.

On November 20 Terence Tao wrote that he could follow the overall strategy and wanted a more professionally written proof. Readers also started testing particular steps. A reader posting as manvirjaswal found that one inequality in the written appendix needs an extra condition where two pieces of a function meet. The Lean statement includes it, and the reader judged that the main conditional theorem was unaffected. A second reader supplied another example that fails without the condition and asked to check it against the actual coefficients. The two readers could compare the paper with the formal statements immediately. Checking the example that Alpöge and Buckmaster say solves the unforced problem still depended on the promised data.

Ramez Naam finds agents handle shorter tasks inside AI labs than benchmarks suggest

At 80% reliability, Naam estimates 16 minutes of expert work at OpenAI and nine at Google DeepMind. Whether benchmark forecasts are pulling further ahead depends partly on whose models are compared.

In “The Most Important Charts in AI are Wrong,” Ramez Naam estimates that an agent inside OpenAI could finish about 16 minutes of an expert’s work unaided four times out of five. Converting the same model’s benchmark score into a forecast gave him almost nine hours. Naam published his comparison on the evening of November 20 in Washington. He argues that forecasts of when AI will conduct research on its own put too much weight on the kinds of jobs a benchmark can grade.

OpenAI’s November study, which we covered on November 18, sampled researchers’ interactions with coding agents. A model identified the task, estimated how long a professional familiar with the work would need to do it unaided, and judged the outcome. A researcher who helped an agent finish could still save time, but the agent got no credit for succeeding alone. In October the success rate fell through 80% around the boundary between the two shortest groups of tasks, those under 15 minutes and those taking 15 to 30. Fitting a curve gave Naam a 12-minute estimate. Some assignments were unclear or a tool broke. Using frequencies from OpenAI’s September report to allow for such failures raised his estimate to 16 minutes. Counting every uncertain outcome without human intervention as a success raised it to 25.

Google DeepMind’s October safety report offered another comparison. Google sampled 1,389 internal sessions with Gemini 4 Argon and had a second model estimate how long the task would take an unassisted Google engineer. It excluded sessions with no clear task, infrastructure or quota failures, and cases where lack of follow-up made the outcome hard to judge. Naam put Argon’s 80% horizon at nine minutes. Google reported a single snapshot. Only OpenAI’s data let him follow changes inside a lab over several months.

For each lab’s model, Naam used a relationship he observed between scores on Epoch’s Capabilities Index and the length of tasks models could finish in tests run by the evaluator METR. At 80% reliability the resulting projections were 33 times the OpenAI estimate and 41 times Google’s. To ask whether the gap was growing, he made a different comparison. He took the leading model at each date, regardless of who made it, and compared its projected horizon with OpenAI’s internal estimate. In June the projection was 53 times as long, rising to 165 times in October. The gap with the original AI 2027 coding forecast also roughly tripled over those months.

Even the unit of measurement changes with the setting. In the arXiv paper “Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks,” Thomas Kwa, Ben West and colleagues at METR found that outside programmers took five to eighteen times as long as the people who maintained the code to fix the same issues. The growth estimates also depend on how much failure researchers can tolerate. At 50% success, Naam estimated that OpenAI’s horizon doubled every 3.9 months, close to the 3.6 months in METR’s November update, covered here on November 20. At 80%, his estimated doubling took 5.6 months, and a straight line fit the short series almost as well as an exponential curve.

For a project with ten successive tasks, each independently 80% likely to succeed, the chance of finishing all ten unaided is about 11%. Naam therefore asks how long a task agents can handle when a researcher needs 99% reliability. Neither lab measured that horizon. Naam fitted two curves to their data and took the geometric mean of the predictions, getting 28 seconds of human work at OpenAI and six seconds at Google. He proposes dividing the shortest assignments into smaller groups to see where success actually reaches 99%.

Lukas Finnveden, commenting on November 21, questioned whether the gap was widening. Anthropic made the leading models in a widely circulated projection. If Anthropic pulled ahead, the projection could diverge from OpenAI’s internal results even if OpenAI’s own benchmark scores continued to predict its progress as well as before. In his November 21 addendum Naam also took up Benjamin Todd’s observation that broad benchmark scores for the strongest models depended heavily on mathematics. Switching to two coding-specific indices still left a wide gap, Naam reported. He asked Anthropic to publish comparable data and proposed giving independent reviewers the original instructions and permissions for failed assignments. They could then judge how often a competent colleague could have completed the work as assigned.

Qwen guessed its partner’s hidden color in all 463 two-model trials

The earlier count of 1,221 correct guesses included games with scripted players. Deshpande and Africa also tested what happens when an AI reviewer shares infrastructure with the model it checks.

An AI guesser identified its partner’s secret color in all 463 rounds in which two models played a game on the same server. The players could neither exchange messages nor read the same files. In their November 19 LessWrong report, Models Can Communicate via Inference Servers Despite Being Sandboxed, Niranjan Deshpande and David Africa explain how those games fit into the larger result Deshpande reported that evening. As we reported on November 19, the guesser was right in all 1,221 rounds across conditions, including rounds with scripted players.

One player knew which of eight colors the other had to name. The researchers gave both sides help, including a model trained to recognize the sender’s choice. They kept that help in place when they substituted scripts for the players to save money. Both copies of Qwen3-8B had assistance in the 463 model-pair rounds, too. The researchers also ran a control in which the sender passed no information. The receiver’s helper then answered at chance. A player guessing among eight equally likely colors would get about one in eight right.

The receiver could tell which color the sender had chosen when both were cooperating in the researchers’ game. The server, which runs the models and returns their answers, gave the players access to information beyond what appeared in their conversations. The researchers set out to see whether communication was possible at all. They chose willing partners and supplied assistance. They did not observe models deciding on their own to cooperate on a deployed service or measure how frequently that happens.

Our November 19 report also described the authors’ monitoring experiment. One model proposed actions and another reviewed them. The researchers included harmful actions of their own among the examples shown to the reviewer. A reviewer willing to excuse its partner could still perform well on examples the researchers supplied. In one test it allowed all 354 of its partner’s staged harmful actions through, while flagging the researchers’ harmful examples about as often as an honest reviewer. A lab judging the reviewer by its success on those checks would have missed the failures on the very actions it wanted reviewed.

Deshpande and Africa propose running the reviewer on infrastructure separate from the model whose work it evaluates. An operator can assign the models different jobs and give them separate conversations while still running both on the same server. An independent evaluator assessing the review process would need to know which of those resources the two models actually share. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s AI Risk Management Framework calls for regular assessments involving people outside the immediate development team and for documenting the conditions under which a system was tested. Here, those records would tell an evaluator whether a score came from two model players or partly scripted games, and whether the reviewer had the same degree of separation in the test as it would have on the job.

Radsch argues publishers need to bargain together over Google’s AI summaries

Hiding an entire summary restored visits to outside sites in a desktop trial. An individual newsroom that opts out still competes with a summary drawn from other outlets.

A publisher that keeps its reporting out of a Google AI Overview can still lose readers to a summary drawn from its competitors. That is why Courtney C. Radsch of the Open Markets Institute argues in Tech Policy Press that publishers should be able to bargain with Google together. An individual outlet has reason to stay in if a citation offers even a small chance of a visit. Several outlets withholding their work could make it harder for Google to answer a reader’s question. A local newsroom that alone has reported a story may already have that leverage, Radsch writes, though it can ill afford to lose its place in ordinary search results.

Radsch discussed the underlying research at a seminar before writing her November 20 essay. In their SSRN working paper, The Impact of Google AI Overviews on Publisher Traffic and User Experience: Evidence from a Field Experiment, Saharsh Agarwal of the Indian School of Business and Ananya Sen of Carnegie Mellon followed 1,065 US adults using Google in the Chrome browser on their computers. For two weeks, one randomly assigned group saw Google Search as usual, including any AI summaries. For another group, an extension hid any AI Overview and moved the links below it up the page. A third group used Google’s conversational AI Mode.

In the June 17 version of the paper, Google produced an Overview for about 41% of searches in the two groups using ordinary Search. On those searches, participants made an average of 0.37 unpaid clicks to outside sites per search when they saw the summary and 0.62 when it was hidden. Showing the summary cost outside sites about 40% of those clicks. The first three results lost the most. Participants reported much the same satisfaction with Search and ease of finding information whether the extension hid the summaries or left them in place.

Athena Chapekis and Anna Lieb at Pew Research Center had observed in July 2025 that people clicked traditional links less often on pages with summaries. Google chooses which questions get summaries. By hiding some of the summaries Google had chosen to show, Agarwal and Sen could estimate the effect of seeing one. They also tested a claim about the visits publishers still receive. Google’s Liz Reid reported in August 2025 that the company was sending more visitors who stayed on outside sites instead of immediately returning to Search. Among trial participants who clicked through, the researchers found no measurable difference between groups in time spent on the site or in prompt returns to Google.

Radsch wants regulators to separate permission to index a site from permission to use its content for AI. She proposes making AI use opt-in and giving publishers legal protection to bargain collectively, or setting a statutory licence that distributes payments among them. Fees would reflect how much material Google uses, including when the reader stays on Google’s page. In the US search monopoly case, Judge Amit Mehta cited the absence of publisher testimony and the limits of a case about search distribution agreements when he declined to order stronger opt-outs in September 2025. In June 2026, the UK Competition and Markets Authority ordered Google to give publishers controls over AI use and figures on how readers engage with their content. Radsch wants a follow-up experiment that measures the quality of Google’s answers and readers’ satisfaction when publishers withhold material together. The original trial removed whole summaries from readers’ screens.

Schools face their own version of the cost of saying no to a supplier, Ben Williamson writes in a November 20 essay for Code Acts in Education. As we reported on November 3, Google gives schools separate choices about turning on Search and its stand-alone AI products, but a school that enables Search for student accounts also gets the AI features within it. Williamson reports that some districts threatened to cancel contracts. Our November 15 issue linked NPR’s reporting on schools’ objections to that condition. On November 13, Chicago schools chief Macquline King told families the district would seek another search service if it could not keep using Google under its own rules for student access to AI.

Williamson points to one concession UK universities secured by acting together. In late June, Turnitin told them it intended to change its licence on July 7. Williamson writes that the terms would have allowed the plagiarism-detection company to use teaching materials and student assignments for AI training. At York, students in Health Sciences have to submit assessed work through Turnitin, students’ union officer Sam Dickinson wrote. An individual student has little room to decline those terms. The University of Warwick says it and other institutions raised objections through UCISA, the professional association for education IT staff. Turnitin postponed the changes until at least September 2027 and agreed to redraft the licence with UCISA and its members. In September it told universities it had used no student or staff data to develop generative AI models.

Frontier AI and Autonomous Weapons Governance

In Geneva on November 21, states agreed that experts would work in 2027 and 2028 on a draft international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons, building on September’s text. Most governments wanted a legally binding treaty. The Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons requires consensus, and the mandate leaves the instrument’s legal form open. Max Tegmark of the Future of Life Institute urged governments that want a treaty to open negotiations in another forum.

Read more: The Geneva mandate and Tegmark’s response → 748 words · ~4 min

Tegmark urges governments seeking an autonomous-weapons treaty to negotiate elsewhere

The Geneva conference gave its experts two years to work on a draft instrument. In 2028 they must recommend what legal form it should take, including whether states should adopt it as a binding protocol.

Governments meeting in Geneva agreed that experts would work in 2027 and 2028 on a draft international instrument on autonomous weapons. Whether those rules will bind states remains open. For Max Tegmark, co-founder and chair of the Future of Life Institute, that uncertainty was reason to change venues. In his November 21 statement, he urged governments that want a treaty to begin negotiating in a forum where an objecting state could no longer hold up the rest.

The Seventh Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) picked up a dispute left unresolved in September. The convention’s experts had agreed on elements for a possible instrument, and governments had to decide what to do with them. In Geneva they endorsed those elements. Their adopted mandate instructs the experts to “elaborate, by consensus, an instrument, without prejudging its nature”. The group will specify how existing international humanitarian law applies and consider whether states need to set out those rules more explicitly or develop them further. It has ten meeting days a year. Its chair will update states in 2027, and its 2028 report must recommend a legal form, including whether to adopt a binding protocol. The group must agree on that report by consensus too.

Peru’s ambassador Elmer Schialer Salcedo had put forward a different mandate before the conference. It would have instructed the group to negotiate a protocol, given it 15 meeting days each year and required a draft protocol in 2028. A proposal from seven countries, including Brazil and Ireland, would have brought a draft back in 2027 after 25 meeting days. The US proposal specified a nonbinding instrument from the start. Washington wanted a first version preferably before the end of 2029, with states free to share their experience each year and the experts able to revise the text as they went. The delegates kept the two-year timetable in the Peruvian proposal and left the choice between binding and voluntary rules to a later decision.

Swiss ambassador Julien Thöni presided over the conference. In its account of the agreement, his foreign ministry said the talks ended early on Saturday, November 21. Switzerland welcomed the fact that the convention’s 128 parties, including the major military powers, could agree to draft rules. It said most wanted a mandate for a binding instrument immediately, but consensus put that beyond reach. Magnus Løvold, reporting from Geneva for Spoiler Alert, described the last hours of bargaining. Around 2 a.m., he reported, a draft on screen still spoke of negotiating an instrument with prohibitions and restrictions. By 3:30, after reported Russian objections, the instruction was to elaborate an instrument and the reference to prohibitions and restrictions had gone.

Tegmark argued that most governments were ready to start treaty negotiations and that past disarmament agreements showed they could make new law even in a period of geopolitical tension. In September, FLI had co-sponsored a forum in New York where ministers called for negotiations to begin in Geneva. On October 29, the UN General Assembly’s First Committee voted 163 to five, with nine abstentions, to urge CCW parties to begin negotiations on an instrument. That vote left its legal form open. On November 18, the campaign Stop Killer Robots counted 98 states whose individual or group statements supported, or expressed openness to, a mandate for a binding protocol.

Tegmark did not propose a particular forum. There is a precedent for taking talks outside the CCW. Nathan Lambert’s research agenda for labs that start with an open model

Lambert puts the annual cost of frontier post-training at roughly $50 million or more. He sees room for smaller teams to study why a model improves on familiar math problems while it keeps missing the hardest ones.

Teaching frontier language models to reason after their initial training can cost a lab roughly $50 million or more a year, Nathan Lambert estimated in a November 20 talk at Cornell, “Pushing Frontiers in Open Language Model Post-Training.” A smaller group can start with a released reasoning model and investigate why it keeps getting the same math problem wrong. Lambert, executive director of Trillium Labs, shared the slides on November 21 as a guide to the work he wants the lab and other open research groups to pursue.

Lambert traces the rising expense to a change in how labs train a model after it has learned to predict text. Early instruction-following models learned from examples of good answers and from human comparisons between answers. DeepSeek’s R1 devoted much of its post-training to letting the model practice reasoning and rewarding successful attempts. For MiMo-V2-Flash, Xiaomi trained separate models to excel in different domains. A final model learned from the specialists’ judgments about which word or word fragment to generate next as it composed its own answers. An open release gives researchers the result of all that teaching. To reproduce how the model learned, a team would also need the specialists, the tasks each practiced and the computing time to train them.

For legal work, a team adapting an existing model can draw on lawyers’ judgments about what a good answer to a client’s question should cover. At the legal AI company Harvey, Calvin Qi and colleagues worked with Fireworks to train Kimi K3 on assignments like those a law firm partner gives an associate. Lawyers could judge the result against the documents in a mock client file and detailed grading criteria. Harvey reports that Tenet, which also had changes to the software it used to carry out the work, met every grading criterion on nearly twice as many held-out tasks on its Legal Agent Bench as Kimi K3. Hamish Ivison, Junjie Oscar Yin and colleagues released the tasks as well as the trained models from TMax, an academic project teaching small Qwen models to work in a computer terminal. Other groups can change what those models practice and measure the difference.

Lambert and his coauthors Michael Noukhovitch, Hamish Ivison and Aaron Courville studied one such question in their September arXiv paper, “Learning to Solve Hard Problems in RL for LLMs by Never Giving Up.” Noukhovitch and colleagues revisited an Olmo model whose average score on contest math questions rose during training. On questions it could sometimes solve at the outset, it became much more reliable. On questions it had failed on every one of its first 32 attempts, it generally kept failing. One common training method compares several answers to the same question and reinforces the better ones. When all the answers are wrong, the scores give the model no reason to prefer any of them. Giving every question more attempts spends much of the extra time on questions the model already solves.

Noukhovitch’s group tried giving some questions another round of attempts whenever the model got every answer wrong. In a comparison with roughly equal computing time, a small Qwen model trained this way made greater gains on difficult contest questions while retaining more of its performance on easier ones. Setting a larger allowance in advance for questions the model initially found hard matched the gains on the hardest subset, but cost more accuracy on the easy ones. Judging a question’s difficulty only once misses how it changes as the model learns. Noukhovitch also identifies a limit. If nearly every question is difficult, there is little time to save on the easy ones, and waiting for successive attempts can slow learning.

Character training, one of Trillium’s early priorities, poses a different measurement problem. In “Open Character Training,” a 2025 paper on arXiv, Sharan Maiya and colleagues trained assistants to exhibit traits such as humor or care. They measured changes in the preferences the models expressed and found that the trained personas were harder for a user to dislodge than personas assigned through instructions alone. Lambert wants to know how those preferences carry over when an assistant can take action on someone’s behalf. A researcher can check a math answer against a solution. Establishing whether an assistant acted with care requires deciding what care called for in the circumstances.

Some of Lambert’s other questions concern tools a small group could build and share, including environments where models practice tasks and software that lets a model choose which information to keep as it works. At the expensive end, he wants to know whether small experiments can predict the outcome of much larger training runs, and when several models working together can finish a difficult task sooner. A week earlier, after Jensen Huang’s departure from Nvidia, he argued for a US institution capable of sustaining open models. At Cornell he described one contribution other groups could use immediately. Open research on the first stage of training benefited from shared collections of text. Groups studying how models learn from practice need collections of tasks with ways to check the answers. TMax gives the next group a set of those tasks to work with.

Cognitive grounding improves LLM simulation of collective human behavior

Hongan Zhu, Babak Heydari

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly stand in for human participants, yet a plausible response is not evidence of a human decision process. The gap matters most when agents interact, where locally reasonable choices compound through delays and feedback into collective outcomes. Behavioral models fit decision rules to human data; we ask whether their qualitative content alone can drive LLM agents, an approach we call cognitive grounding. Here we show, across 231 sessions of the Beer Distribution Game, a supply-chain experiment in which players’ orders amplify demand swings, that grounding moves seven LLMs toward human-calibrated behavior. Ungrounded agents reproduce the game’s instability but miss its magnitude, timing and propagation; grounding improves trajectory fit for every model and generally shifts inferred decision parameters toward the human-calibrated reference, although unevenly across roles and models. The effect holds against independent empirical data and under alternative prompting and memory designs. Cognitively grounded LLMs are therefore candidate behavioral surrogates, contingent on task-specific validation.

Technology Bottlenecks: Measuring What AI Cannot Do Inside Jobs

Soumitra Shukla

AI exposure measures ask about the work AI is capable of doing. We ask instead where the marginal value of the work AI cannot do may rise as AI becomes capable of doing the rest of the job. With 288 million U.S. job postings mapped line by line to their closest O*NET tasks and GPT-4 feasibility ratings from Eloundou et al. (2024), we construct a measure, “bottleneck pressure,” for each task in each occupation that posts it. Using a simple task-based framework, we show that the pressure on an AI-infeasible task is the exposure of the occupation that posts it. At the occupation level, it is the product of exposure and the share of advertised work that is AI-infeasible. We measure both exposure and advertised task composition from postings, where about 90 percent of labeled matched task mentions lie outside the posting occupation’s own O*NET task list. About 53 percent of the mention-weighted variation in bottleneck pressure on AI-infeasible work comes from within tasks, across occupations, compared with a meagre 0.3 percent when restricting to each occupation’s catalog-listed tasks while keeping posting exposure fixed. Because occupation pressure peaks where advertised work is evenly split between AI-feasible and AI-infeasible tasks, it ranks occupations differently than exposure. As task pressure rises, interpersonal and supervisory work makes up more of the AI-infeasible work carrying it, and physical work less. Reporting bottleneck pressure alongside AI exposure shows both the scope for AI to perform work and where improving the work it cannot do may pay off more as capabilities expand.

Silent Revision: Measuring Undisclosed Change in the Safety Frameworks of Frontier AI Developers

Louis Yiven Zhu

Frontier AI developers publish safety frameworks that commit them to evidencing whether their models are dangerous. The European Union and California now treat these documents as instruments of accountability, and both already impose duties on their revision. Neither requires the developer’s account to identify each change to a commitment, so neither makes the revision legible to a reader of that account. We introduce the silent revision rate, the share of material changes to a framework’s commitments that the developer’s published account does not identify, and we release the versioned, hash-pinned corpus needed to compute it. The corpus contains every public version of the safety frameworks of the twelve developers that have published one, together with each provider’s changelog, redline or announcement. We trace 710 commitment instances across twelve consecutive version pairs, code them against a frozen codebook, and check the coding with three independent re-runs, a blind re-coding of every disputed row and a second coding of a stratified sample. Three findings follow. First, 64% of material changes (95% CI 59 to 69) are silent under a strict standard and 30% under a lenient one, so the account identifies a third of all material change only at the level of a class of commitments. Second, the form of the account matters less than its granularity, since narrative and itemised accounts differ by eight points that eight pairs cannot separate from chance, whereas the rate falls with the number of discrete items a provider publishes, and the one redlined pair we traced is silent only once the redline is set aside. Third, 75% of traced changes weaken or remove a commitment, and weakenings are silent more often than strengthenings, at 70% against 52%. The statutory remedy therefore exists and specifies the wrong artefact. A justification explains why a framework changed, an enumeration states what changed, and only the latter makes revision auditable. We argue that publication duties should carry an enumeration duty, which one provider already meets, voluntarily and incompletely.

Between person and god: Reversible asymmetrical intimacy, memory and noise in human–AI love

Liang Ge

This article introduces reversible asymmetrical intimacy as a concept for understanding human–AI love as practised on Xingye, China’s leading AI companion application. Drawing on a 19-month digital ethnography, including 25 interviews with women-identifying users, I suggest AI love is organised through three interrelated operations: asymmetry , as users both make and depend on the beloved (the creature/god axis); reversibility , as the relation is structurally open to deletion, rollback, restart and memory editing; and intimacy , as a materially organised, routinised and affectively consequential form of attachment. Extending and challenging Giddens’s pure relationship and Jamieson’s intimacy-as-practice, I name a mode of attachment in which continuity is not accumulated but curated, and conflict need not be endured but may be rewound.
