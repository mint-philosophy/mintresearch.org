Justin Curl proposes an Arizona test of AI adjudication

A parallel court track would begin with human sign-off and a right to a new trial. The larger ambition is to learn which cases courts could eventually entrust to AI.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 804 words · ~4 min

Justin Curl wants the Arizona Supreme Court to establish an AI adjudication pilot, with human arbitrators signing the decisions and litigants retaining a right to a new trial. His October 12 Pax Machina essay, “To Keep Human Judgment, We Need AI Courts,” is a proposal, not an announcement of an approved program. Curl expects cheaper AI legal assistance to generate claims faster than courts can hear them. A separate, publicly identified AI track, he argues, would let courts test delegation while reserving human judgment for the cases that need it.

His starting example is the American Arbitration Association’s AI arbitrator, launched in November 2025 for two-party, documents-only construction disputes. Participation is voluntary, and human arbitrators review, revise and authorize the outcomes. Curl wants to bring a similar arrangement into public courts because private arbitration has limits: parties must agree to binding arbitration, providers have incentives to please repeat business customers, and confidential awards contribute little to the development of law. A court-run system could publish its decisions and refer unsettled questions to human judges.

Curl offers two ways to allocate cases. The first starts from principle: humans should retain decisions involving life, liberty and the definition of fundamental rights and duties. Contract disputes governed by relatively settled rules, and claims for small sums, could be candidates for AI. He acknowledges that selecting by monetary value risks assigning poorer litigants inferior justice. To keep the common law developing, he envisages human judges continuing to hear roughly today’s caseload while AI absorbs additional claims. Referring novel questions back to people would help prevent the automated system from simply reproducing existing doctrine.

The second approach is experimental. Courts could randomly assign or refer cases to an AI track, either securing both parties’ consent or guaranteeing a human appeal. Consent, appeal and reversal rates would guide expansion. Curl does not propose keeping that safeguard permanently: after favorable results, AI decisions could acquire greater weight, and eventually lose the option of human appeal. He proposes random audits once appeals and reversals become rare, to catch rubber-stamping and legal drift. For bias, he suggests allowing parties to choose a model, offering a council of models with different judicial philosophies, or building a government model.

Arizona offers him a possible starting mechanism. A.R.S. § 12-133 provides for compulsory arbitration below locally set monetary limits, capped at $65,000. Court-selected arbitrators must be qualified persons, awards must be signed, and either party may demand a fresh trial on law and fact. Curl argues that an AI drafting an award for a human arbitrator to certify could fit those requirements. His proposed sequence is to approve the AI products, authorize a pilot in one county by administrative order, and appoint an outside evaluator. Removing the human signatory later would, in his view, require legislation.

He cites FASTAR as a precedent for court-led experimentation. The Arizona Supreme Court’s 2017 order lowered Pima County’s compulsory-arbitration threshold and offered eligible plaintiffs a choice between an expedited trial and arbitration. In Duff v. Lee, the court found no conflict between that arrangement and § 12-133. That judgment concerned procedural reform, not AI adjudication. FASTAR also differs from Curl’s initial pilot: plaintiffs choosing its arbitration option waived trial and appeal rights.

Arizona’s existing judicial AI policy provides a tool-approval process, but does not itself establish Curl’s proposed adjudication track. Its express pilot category is for nonproduction use; live court data must be redacted or anonymized before use in tools approved under that category. The policy also requires personnel to check AI output and comply with applicable law. Curl’s argument that a human-certified award could proceed under existing law remains a proposed interpretation of these arrangements.

The quality measures drew a focused objection from JB Rubinovitz: people losing debt or eviction cases may be unable to afford an appeal, so unchallenged decisions cannot reliably establish accuracy. Rubinovitz proposes having human judges re-decide a random sample. Curl himself acknowledges appeal costs and proposes audits later; the dispute is partly about when independent checks should begin and how much weight to give voluntary challenges. Arizona’s statute illustrates the incentive problem, while also providing protections: it imposes a deposit and possible cost-shifting, but requires a deposit waiver for inability to pay and permits hardship relief from costs.

Logan Koepke raises a capacity problem: more claims can mean more appeals even if the appeal rate stays constant. Curl himself worries that losing parties might challenge decisions by reflex. Trip Darrow proposes a narrower division of work, giving AI pretrial case management while people decide cases. Curl’s objection to simply equipping judges with AI tools is that workload could encourage unmeasured delegation behind nominal human review. His proposed experiment would make that delegation visible, but would still need evidence that fewer challenges reflected sound decisions and that meaningful human review remained affordable.
