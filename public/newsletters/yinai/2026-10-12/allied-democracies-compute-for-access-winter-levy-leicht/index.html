Winter-Levy and Leicht propose data-center bargains for AI access

The Carnegie fellows want simultaneous model releases and equivalent access for allied critical infrastructure, backed by the costs companies would face if they broke their promises.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 847 words · ~4 min

Allied democracies should offer U.S. AI companies powered data-center sites in exchange for contractual guarantees of access to their best models, Sam Winter-Levy and Anton Leicht argue in The Leverage Middle Powers Have in the AI Race, published by Foreign Policy on October 8. The two Carnegie Endowment fellows want specific terms: frontier models would become available in allied markets when they become available in the United States, and allied critical-infrastructure operators would receive access equivalent to their American counterparts. Hosting expensive facilities would give governments ways to penalize companies that broke those promises.

Winter-Levy and Leicht distinguish two threats to access. Governments and developers can restrict models for security reasons; separately, a shortage of computing power can force companies to ration service. They cite June's U.S. export controls as an example of the first risk. Anthropic says the June 12 directive barred foreign nationals from Fable 5 and Mythos 5, prompting it to suspend both models for everyone because it could not reliably verify nationality. The controls were lifted June 30. The authors' second concern persists even without a ban: serving one customer's requests uses capacity unavailable to another, and foreign customers could lose priority.

The authors argue that U.S. construction difficulties give allies bargaining power. Local opposition, limited grid capacity and long waits for power equipment obstruct American expansion. Allies can offer sites with reliable electricity and established security relationships: Australia has land and solar power, Germany has former industrial sites already connected to the grid, and Nordic countries have hydroelectricity. Japan and South Korea offer political stability and skilled workers despite their land constraints. Countries need not host the largest training clusters to matter. Smaller facilities can supply inference, the computing used to run models for customers, while generating local tax revenue.

Winter-Levy and Leicht expect physical investment to make access promises more durable. A host government could reduce electricity access or tax benefits, withdraw permits for expansion, or, in extreme circumstances, restrict operations and repurpose facilities. Companies would face losses on infrastructure they had already built. The authors expect that exposure to encourage firms to lobby Washington against restrictions that would breach their access commitments. Their proposal therefore depends on governments being willing and able to impose costs, and on those prospective costs changing decisions in both corporate offices and Washington.

The authors ask allied governments to accept continued American leadership in frontier AI and devote some of their own scarce grid capacity to U.S. companies, despite those companies' domestic unpopularity. They oppose public subsidies. Instead, governments would commit to predictable treatment for projects meeting conditions on clean energy, investment in the grid and local taxes. They suggest that a country able to move a project from approval to operation in roughly two years would attract substantial demand. Their offer depends on making construction reliable while retaining conditions that protect local interests.

Washington would have to accept that overseas investment gives allies power to disrupt American companies. Winter-Levy and Leicht argue that the administration's desire to maintain the U.S. lead over China and sustain AI-driven market growth could outweigh its reluctance. Without credible access promises, they expect allies to split their purchases between American and Chinese providers, pursue costly domestic models and delay U.S. infrastructure projects. They regard these alternatives as unattractive: dependence on China creates its own risks, and continued Chinese permission to distribute open models cannot be assumed.

Leicht previously proposed trading sites and power for access in Beware the Permanent Periphery, which we covered on July 30. The independent Transformative AI Strategy for Europe, covered on September 14, already proposed access comparable to that in developers' home markets, backed by penalties involving electricity supplies or deployed chips. The October 8 essay develops this argument for allied democracies more broadly, explaining what each side must concede and why the authors think a deal could be politically possible.

The earlier documents also explain the limits of these bargains. The European strategy acknowledges that a foreign government could still force its companies to cut access. In their September 28 Carnegie paper, AI Breakout Capacity: A Middle-Power Path Between Racing and Dependence, Leicht and Winter-Levy similarly argue that contracts cannot reliably prevent a great power from denying imported capability. They propose retaining the capacity to develop domestic models in an emergency. That broader fallback plan allows energy subsidies in some circumstances and recognizes that expropriation provisions can deter investment. Foreign Policy's subsidy-free offer leaves governments to judge whether faster construction alone would compensate companies for accepting the proposed enforcement terms.

Australia already illustrates the difference between attracting investment and securing access commitments. Ben Eltham's October 8 Crikey article, covered in the October 11 issue, questioned claims that hosting data centers would guarantee Australian access. The government's memorandum with Anthropic expressly disclaims legal effect and commits neither party to future arrangements. Winter-Levy and Leicht seek firmer contractual obligations sustained by incentives to honor them. Their argument explains why hosting might help governments negotiate those obligations; it does not establish that a private agreement can override U.S. restrictions.
