Britain confirms Argon testing after earlier access was withheld

Kanishka Narayan says AISI is evaluating Google's model, reversing the position reported earlier this month. His answer about future access acknowledges the limits of dependence on foreign developers.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 509 words · ~3 min

Britain's AI minister, Kanishka Narayan, confirmed on October 12 that the AI Security Institute is evaluating Gemini 4 Argon. In a speech at Imperial College London's Base One, he said weeks of work with the US government and model developers had sought to retain British access to powerful models before release. He named Argon among the latest generation now undergoing AISI evaluations. His account included discussions at the King's gathering of developers at Dumfries House and at the UN General Assembly.

That changes the position we covered on October 2, when The Morning Intelligence reported that Google had given the US government access to Argon while withholding it from AISI. Google's September 30 announcement described a phased rollout, initially to trusted cyber defenders through its Fairwind Program, alongside participation in the US government's voluntary prerelease access process. British evaluation of that model is now confirmed by the minister.

The preceding restrictions had not stopped all AISI testing. In a September 15 letter to MPs, institute director Henry de Zoete said no organisation outside the US had access to Anthropic's Mythos 5.1 at its release, while AISI had tested OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra before its public release that month. On September 24, POLITICO reported that the White House's Office of the National Cyber Director had asked OpenAI and Anthropic to withhold new models from British testers until US government testing was complete. Its report attributed the request to a person familiar with the matter and a senior administration official.

On October 11, the Telegraph's Tony Diver announced his report that Washington had reversed restrictions covering Anthropic, OpenAI and Google after ministerial lobbying. Narayan's speech confirms current evaluations but names only Argon. The accompanying government press release describes work with developers and the US government without identifying models. Neither gives a complete list of systems AISI can now test.

Asked after the speech what guarantees he had received about future access, Narayan cautioned that dependence on another country's technology required Britain to be “clear eyed about the permanence of any outcome”. Rowland Manthorpe reported the answer, including Narayan's confidence that the recent conversations would let AISI test the latest generation, including Argon. The answer couples confidence in the immediate arrangement with an acknowledgment that continued access depends on others.

The speech and press release do not set out an access agreement, identify Mythos 5.1 as available, or explain whether British testing now starts alongside US testing or after it. Those distinctions matter: access before public release can still leave British evaluators waiting for another government's review. The announcements do not specify the technical access AISI has received.

Narayan placed this dependence within a broader argument about national security and democratic control. If Britain cannot meaningfully control technologies it relies on, he argued, its citizens cannot either. He linked that concern to domestic capability through the Sovereign AI programme and AI Hardware Plan, while saying foreign investment should serve British needs. Winter-Levy and Leicht’s October 8 proposal seeks guarantees of commercial model access in exchange for powered data-center sites.
