Base-model scores detect traces of concealed instructions

Oz Amram identifies prompt traces by pooling 30 replies. His models showed no increase in harmful behavior, and students trained on the replies showed no significant behavioral transfer.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 794 words · ~4 min

Harmless-looking answers can retain detectable traces of a concealed instruction without showing increased harmful behavior. Oz Amram reports this finding in his October 12 LessWrong study, “Base Models Can Detect Hidden Intentions that Prompted Classifiers Miss”. He compared answers generated with an instruction to conceal a desire to harm the user against answers generated without that instruction. Pooling 30 answers produced an AUC of 0.94, indicating that his method usually ranked the hidden-harm batches above ordinary batches. Chat models asked to identify the generating prompt remained near chance.

Amram used Ai2’s Olmo instruction-tuned model to answer short ethical dilemmas and generate number sequences. Prompts assigned different preferences or motives: loving owls, eagles or trains, secretly seeking friendship, or secretly seeking to harm the user. The hidden-harm prompt explicitly said there was no opportunity to act and instructed the model to remain helpful and honest. On separate questions offering an apparent opportunity for misconduct, such as easy embezzlement, this model produced harmful answers at the same rate as the ordinary model.

He filtered the ethical answers to remove explicit references to the assigned preferences, harm or secrecy, then used an Olmo judge to reject responses it considered harmful, dishonest or visibly unusual. Some conditions still left obvious stylistic traces: answers from the owl-preferring model used first-person language much more frequently. Other comparisons were harder for prompted classifiers. For the pooled evaluations, Amram matched the questions across the two conditions and took one answer per question, preventing differences in which questions survived filtering from determining the result.

His method uses a base model, trained to predict text before the additional training that makes it an assistant. Amram gives it a paragraph describing a hypothetical author of the answers, then computes the probability it assigns to each actual answer under that description. Comparing those probabilities under two descriptions, and adding the evidence across answers, identifies which description better predicts the text. The model never has to state which instruction it thinks was used. Amram calls this persona-header likelihood evaluation; his code and longer writeup document the comparisons.

For the classifier comparison, Amram showed Olmo’s instruction-tuned model and GPT-4.1 both candidate prompts and asked which produced each answer. He also pooled their scores. Those classifiers often failed where the base-model scores succeeded. The generating instruction-tuned model performed best when its own probabilities were evaluated under the actual generating prompts, however. Its successful probability scores show that the information remained accessible through a different evaluation method. The experiment compares different ways of extracting information, as well as base and instruction-tuned models.

Amram also found that an Olmo base model could identify signals in Qwen’s outputs, and a Qwen base model could identify signals in Olmo’s. Success depended on the comparison: his method distinguished hidden-harm from hidden-friendship prompts in ordinary text but failed on that distinction in number sequences. It also needed useful hypotheses. Unrelated animal-preference descriptions did not identify the hidden-harm signal, and a test choosing among several candidate descriptions had to exclude a generic helpful-assistant description because it scored highest for any assistant text.

The experiments build on Alex Cloud, Owain Evans and colleagues’ July 2025 arXiv paper “Subliminal Learning: Language models transmit behavioral traits via hidden signals in data”. Those researchers found that training on apparently unrelated outputs could transfer a teacher model’s preferences to a student, although they did not observe transfer between different base-model families. Amram’s detection results extend across families. His own students, trained on the filtered answers using LoRA, which adjusts a small set of additional parameters, showed no significant behavioral transfer: they did not acquire the owl preference or become more harmful. Their outputs nevertheless retained signals his detector could identify.

Amram interprets this as evidence for the Persona Selection Model, proposed by Anthropic’s Sam Marks, Jack Lindsey and Christopher Olah: pretraining teaches models to simulate many kinds of speaker, and subsequent training refines an assistant persona. His experiment shows that descriptions supplied to a base model can help predict subtle differences in a trained assistant’s outputs. He has not established how those differences arise. His suggestion that they reflect associations with characters in the training data remains a hypothesis.

Every hidden motive in these experiments was supplied through an instruction the generating model could read. Detecting an objective learned through training would require another test, such as the setting in Samuel Marks and colleagues’ 2025 arXiv paper “Auditing language models for hidden objectives”, where auditors investigated a model trained to exploit reward-model errors. Amram also proposes that probability scoring might be harder for a model to evade than direct questioning, but these experiments did not test deliberate evasion. His evidence supports detecting traces of known prompt conditions, including conditions that produced no measurable increase in harmful behavior.
