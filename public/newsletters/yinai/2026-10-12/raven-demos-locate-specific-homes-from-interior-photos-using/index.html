Raven’s interior-photo demos point to rental and property listings

Joseph Cox checked the company’s demonstrations against Airbnb, Vrbo and Zillow. Police purchases are documented; accuracy across unfamiliar homes and the imagery’s provenance remain unresolved.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 759 words · ~4 min

Demonstrations of a police image-search tool show it returning rental and real-estate photographs when asked to identify rooms from other images. In his October 12 404 Media investigation, Joseph Cox traced results in marketing demonstrations of Raven, formerly GeoSpy, to Airbnb, Vrbo and Zillow listings. His checks show how photographs published to advertise properties can supply location clues for investigations.

Cox examined a demonstration involving a photograph of a washer and dryer posted on social media. Raven’s result pointed to a property in Charlotte, North Carolina, using the same title as an Airbnb listing whose photographs showed the property. Another demonstration associated a bathroom photograph with a Vrbo listing in Montecito, California. In a video posted the previous year, Raven returned interior photographs for a San Francisco property that were identical to photographs on Zillow. Cox infers that the system uses listing-site imagery in some form.

Graylark Technologies, Raven’s developer, describes matching different views of a room through persistent features such as its layout, fittings and structural details. Its Short-Term Rental ID page says the feature searches millions of rental listings worldwide without requiring GPS metadata. Coverage depends on relevant reference photographs being available and enough detail being visible in the source image. The company markets it for trafficking and exploitation investigations, where an interior image might be the only view investigators have of a location.

The company’s separate Find Property feature is a beta limited to supported cities. Its results include candidate properties and reference photographs, with a similarity score used to rank the candidates. Graylark expressly says that score is not a probability that a location is correct. Its own example requires a reviewer to select a lower-ranked candidate, and both product pages describe results as leads requiring corroboration. The city-specific design is consistent with the custom Miami-Dade County model that Cox says appeared in police emails.

The demonstrations do not establish how often Raven identifies the right property, or how its speed varies on unfamiliar images. An earlier account from Graylark CEO Daniel Heinen concerned a different level of precision. In his 2025 interview with Craig Silverman at Indicator, quoted by Cox, Heinen described unexpectedly getting a bathroom selfie’s city right, then trying Zillow images and finding the correct city in nine out of ten cases. Those were city predictions from his own informal experiment. Heinen also speculated that indoor images had entered datasets he had assumed were predominantly outdoor photographs. His account does not identify the training data behind the current property-matching features.

Police procurement is better documented than investigative outcomes. Cox’s February 2026 report, based on internal police emails, established that the Los Angeles Police Department and Miami-Dade Sheriff’s Office had bought GeoSpy access. In a statement reproduced in the new article, Miami-Dade public-affairs officer Joseph R. Peguero Rivera said use had been limited and exploratory, with no significant breakthroughs or arrests. No case outcome, he said, had depended solely or primarily on the tool. Those purchases do not establish that either agency has used the newly promoted interior features in a case.

Graylark separately claims a federal fugitive was located from a window photograph and arrested the next morning. The company labels that example as a Find Street case, a different feature from the rental matcher. It is a company account of success at another agency, with no independent corroboration in Cox’s investigation.

The underlying product and indoor-location claims predate this announcement. In August 2025, Cox was already reporting claims that GeoSpy could identify particular buildings from some windowless interior photographs. His January 2025 reporting had described stalking requests from users; the founder opposed those requests, and Graylark restricted public access after 404 Media sought comment. The company announced Raven as GeoSpy’s successor in May 2026. It says its $10.7 million seed round, led by General Catalyst with participants including Alexandr Wang, will fund new models, data infrastructure and expansion to government and police customers.

The property platforms’ responses to Cox address how that expansion intersects with their own privacy measures. Airbnb said it removes location metadata, lets hosts display approximate locations and withholds addresses until a booking is confirmed. It said it had no relationship with Graylark, prohibited scraping and was reviewing the case. Zillow also said scraping violated its terms; Vrbo did not respond. Cox’s investigation leaves both the acquisition method and the use of these photographs in training unresolved. The demonstrations show why hiding coordinates can leave a separate source of location information: the visible features of a room whose photographs are available elsewhere.
