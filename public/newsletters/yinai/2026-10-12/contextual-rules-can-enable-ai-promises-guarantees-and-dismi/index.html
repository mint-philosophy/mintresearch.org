Chris Cousens on when a chatbot's words really bind

A new Philosophical Quarterly paper argues that the rules in force in a setting, not intention or uptake, decide whether an AI has guaranteed, warned or fired anyone, and that we get to write those rules.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 1305 words · ~7 min

Chris Cousens, a lecturer in philosophy at the University of Glasgow, argues in "Artificial speakers, artificial speech acts?", published online in The Philosophical Quarterly on 12 October, that a chatbot can promise, guarantee, warn or fire someone in exactly the way a human can, provided the rules in force in that setting say so. Most philosophers who have written on the question hold that AI outputs fall short of real speech acts: Freiman and Miller call them "quasi-assertions", Iwan Williams and Tim Bayne of Monash call them "proto-assertions", and Mitchell Green and Jan G. Michel treat artificial speakers as proxies for the humans who deploy them. Cousens's complaint is that sceptics and these qualified optimists share a method. They first fix the conditions a human must meet to perform a speech act, usually a communicative intention or audience uptake, and then ask whether a language model meets them. He says the conditions themselves vary with context, and in some contexts they are switched off.

His first example is a refrigerator sale. A salesperson who says "I promise" to a customer may mean only an ordinary promise, but a tribunal can rule that the words count as a commercial guarantee and order repairs whatever the speaker intended; a promise to be home in time for Survivor binds in a different way again. Cousens draws two lessons: in some settings intention can be overruled or made irrelevant, and the relative weight of promises and guarantees flips from one setting to the next. Two friendship groups, one that promises seriously and one lightly, are both really promising.

The case that carries the argument is Moffatt v. Air Canada, decided by British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal in February 2024. Jake Moffatt booked flights to Toronto after their grandmother died in November 2022, having been told by the airline's website chatbot that a reduced bereavement fare could be claimed within 90 days of the ticket being issued; the airline's own bereavement page said otherwise, and it refused the refund. Tribunal member Christopher Rivers ordered it to pay CA$812.02. Cousens reads the outcome as showing that the chatbot's words created the same obligations a human agent's would have, with no intention anywhere in the picture: "if it looks like a duck, quacks like a duck", and if being a duck in that setting just means looking and quacking, then it is one. He adds that the chatbot was not a proxy for the airline either. Proxy speech on Green and Michel's account needs institutional endorsement, either of the specific utterance (the condolence email Vanderbilt's Peabody College sent after the Michigan State shooting, drafted with ChatGPT and signed by two administrators) or of a type of act (their "Robot Cop", licensed to issue traffic warnings but not to invite a driver to the department's Christmas party). Air Canada gave neither; it disavowed the chatbot's statement in court, and the statement contradicted company policy.

The tribunal's own reasoning sits a little apart from Cousens's reading. Rivers decided the case as negligent misrepresentation, not as the enforcement of a guarantee: Air Canada "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate", and Moffatt's reliance on it was reasonable. The airline had argued it could not be liable for information given by its agents or representatives, including a chatbot, a position Rivers called "a remarkable submission" because it in effect made the chatbot a separate legal entity. He answered that a chatbot is part of the website and "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." The record also notes that Air Canada gave no information about what kind of chatbot it was, so Cousens's description of it as powered by a large language model goes beyond the decision. And the tribunal says its decisions, including those it flags as noteworthy, are "not precedent-setting, and are not binding on CRT members", which narrows the "jurisdictions covered by this precedent" that Cousens invokes. His conceptual point, that a rule can make intention irrelevant, survives; the showcase itself is a liability ruling about careless website content.

Cousens then runs the argument in reverse. Workplace legislation could require that every termination email be written and sent by a human. AI could still pick whom to fire, but an AI-sent "Your employment is hereby terminated" would fire nobody, and an employee who later discovered the email's origin "had never been properly fired to begin with!" He concedes that a human could simply send a fresh one. With Moffatt and such a law both in force, an AI in a Canadian workplace could guarantee but not fire, while across the border, governed by neither, "an artificial speaker might be able to fire someone but unable to guarantee." Since legislatures, employers and social practice write these rules, "we can decide which speech acts artificial speakers can perform". He presents the stakes as current, citing a B2B Reviews survey for the claim that about a tenth of tech companies already use AI to write termination letters.

The same reasoning extends to ordinary conversation, where the participants write the rules. Two people text the question whether they can dry a towel on the toaster, one to a friend and one to ChatGPT, and both get the reply that the towel will catch fire. Cousens says both have been warned, because both replies change the recipient's standing: neither can later complain that nobody warned them. A chatbot's clarifying question counts as a request for the same reason. He stops short of artificial promises, about which he remains sceptical "for now".

He names the resulting view contextual conventionalism. Intentionalists and uptake theorists are each "right… some of the time"; which one is right depends on the conventions governing promising, guaranteeing or asserting in that setting, and those conventions can be imposed by an employer on employees, a legislature on citizens or a social norm on a community. Casey Rebecca Johnson came closest earlier with illocutionary relativism (Synthese, 2023), which indexes speech-act conventions to communities as small as a family or a workplace; Cousens goes further by letting single conversations set norms and by dropping Johnson's shared "classic" schemas. For assertion, he suggests the norm may be knowledge in a courtroom, reasonable belief among friends, and perhaps nothing more than a sentence of the form "P" appearing on a company website. That is why he calls objections built on invariant norms "fragile", among them Patrick Butlin and Emanuel Viebahn's argument in Ergo (2025) that an asserter must be capable of being sanctioned, which they say fine-tuned models are not, and Lukas Skiba's generalisation of that argument in Analysis this April into a view on which language models perform no speech acts at all. A paper Cousens does not cite bears on the same ground: Guido Löhr of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam argued in the American Philosophical Quarterly in July that proxy accounts cover only a few AI utterances because "no human can be held responsible for most of what a chatbot says". Cousens's non-proxy reading of Moffatt answers that worry by letting a rule place the obligation on the company regardless of endorsement.

The paper ends with what Cousens calls a "normative turn". The settings where speech-act norms are easiest to change, institutions governed by legislation and policy, are also the ones where language models are being deployed fastest, so the conventions "may already be changing whether philosophers like it or not". He leaves the reader with a question: "Should we enable artificial speakers to perform speech acts?", and if so which ones, in which contexts. An earlier version, given at a University of Sydney seminar in May 2025 as "Workplace Relations and Artificial Speech Acts", grounded illocutionary force in David Lewis's conversational score; the published paper has replaced that with the appeal to context-variable rules.
