White House plans direct oversight of three CAISSI officials

Leo Schwartz reports an unfinished restructuring of the federal model-testing center. The plan changes senior reporting lines while leaving the center within NIST.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 683 words · ~3 min

Three senior officials at CAISSI, the Commerce Department’s frontier-model testing center, would report directly to the White House under a restructuring begun in September, Leo Schwartz reports in The Information. Their supervisors would be Michael Kratsios, director of the Office of Science and Technology Policy, and National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross. An unnamed White House official said Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and other senior officials chose this arrangement after months of considering whether to move the entire center. The three officials’ names were unavailable because their moves were still being processed when Schwartz published on October 12.

White House and Commerce officials gave Schwartz different accounts of the transition. A senior Commerce official told Schwartz that Secretary Howard Lutnick had invited Kratsios and Cairncross to co-manage the center six months earlier. The White House official blamed Commerce’s lack of coordination for confusion over the transition, arguing that it demonstrated why leadership should move closer to the White House. Two people familiar with the matter said the unresolved debate had left staff uncertain about their status.

Schwartz puts CAISSI’s resources at about 40 staff and $15 million in annual funding. Four people familiar with its work told him that, under a White House pre-deployment framework finalized in August, it tests models including OpenAI’s Astra and Anthropic’s Mythos alongside the National Security Agency. The framework had not been published, and the division of responsibilities remained unclear. Google’s September 30 Argon announcement confirms participation in the government’s voluntary pre-release process, though it names neither CAISSI nor the NSA.

People close to the White House and AI industry told Schwartz that stronger White House connections could give the center more influence and simplify evaluations. Concerns about its resources were among the reasons Google, OpenAI and Anthropic explored a supplementary industry standards body, Schwartz writes. Yet the Super Intelligence Force announced in early October included no Commerce representative. Schwartz describes an institution whose technical work has become more important without securing a corresponding place in policymaking. He reports that Kratsios had previously favored moving it to the Energy Department to draw on the national laboratories.

The center remains within the National Institute of Standards and Technology, part of Commerce. Commerce’s June 2025 statement, renaming the U.S. AI Safety Institute as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation, explicitly kept it within NIST and listed OSTP as a coordination partner. The current NIST page confirms the newer name, Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, or CAISSI. It describes voluntary testing agreements and unclassified evaluations of national-security risks, but says nothing about the reported White House reporting lines.

Kratsios and Cairncross already had roles in federal testing policy. The June 2 executive order behind the pre-release testing framework gives their offices consultative roles and includes Commerce through the NIST director. It calls for a voluntary arrangement allowing government access to covered models for up to 30 days before developers release them to other trusted partners. It says the testing provisions do not authorize mandatory model licensing or preclearance. The order does not name CAISI or transfer its personnel; consultation under that framework does not itself establish a supervisory reporting line.

Publication has also been contested. Senator Ted Budd’s June 26 letter to Cairncross and Kratsios raised concerns about reports that the center had been told to stop publishing evaluation findings and asked them to allow research to appear where possible. Public assessments subsequently appeared: NIST published a joint Kimi K3 assessment in July and a GLM-5.3 assessment in September. The scope of any remaining publication restrictions is unclear.

Schwartz reports that the center is still searching for a permanent head after Chris Fall resigned in July following three months in the job. NIST director Arvind Raman is overseeing it in the interim. The administrative mechanism for the new reporting lines, and how they would interact with Raman’s role, remain unresolved. The proposed arrangement could give evaluators a more direct route to senior policymakers. Schwartz’s investigation does not establish what additional resources would accompany it or who would control publication of their findings.
