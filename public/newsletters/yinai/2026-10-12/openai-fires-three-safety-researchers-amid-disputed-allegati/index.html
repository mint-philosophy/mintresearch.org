Zvi doubts advocacy drove OpenAI firings; a new op-ed seeks mandatory audits

Zvi Mowshowitz argues that an undisclosed dispute probably drove the earlier dismissals, while their chilling effect threatens safety work regardless. Alex Turner, Amber Scorah and Karl Koch call for mandatory audits, evaluator-cooperation protections, incident reporting and executive criminal liability.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 821 words · ~4 min

In his October 12 assessment, Zvi Mowshowitz concludes that OpenAI probably did not fire Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang because they advocated safety measures or supported outside auditors. He also doubts that the company intended the firings to frighten other employees into silence. He believes the researchers sincerely think their dismissals were pretextual. His essay examines the letter we covered on October 8 and OpenAI's reply, covered on October 11. Those competing accounts leave him uncertain about what happened, but convinced the damage to safety work extends beyond the loss of three researchers.

Mowshowitz starts with the costs to OpenAI. These were valuable employees central to monitorability research and external safety collaborations. Firing them predictably harms recruitment, public trust and cooperation with other safety organizations. If management wanted to suppress their advocacy, he argues, such conspicuous dismissals would be an unusually expensive way to do it. That reasoning leads him to doubt the retaliation explanation despite OpenAI's history of departures under disputed circumstances. It is an inference about the company's incentives, not evidence that the researchers committed misconduct.

He gives particular weight to OpenAI's October 9 statement that its investigation found a significant breach of trust beyond the events described in the letter. Mowshowitz thinks one underlying dispute is more plausible than three unrelated infractions simultaneously destroying management’s trust in three prominent researchers. He does not infer that the undisclosed conduct justified dismissal, or even that the researchers did something wrong. Their accounts could be sincere while management withheld its central reason or failed to explain it. Accepting that possibility, he argues, still leaves OpenAI responsible for a serious failure of communication.

The details matter to that judgment. In their letter, the researchers deny leaking architecture information to The Information and dispute suggestions that they exceeded their jobs' remit when working with outsiders. Wang says recruiting work gave her authorized access to an executive's inbox, IT failed to remove it when requested, and she promptly reported accidentally opening a sensitive email. Mowshowitz treats that account as raising a separate security question: why did unnecessary access persist? OpenAI's response alleges policy violations without publicly identifying the additional breach.

Whatever management intended, Mowshowitz expects employees to become more cautious about speaking and collaborating. OpenAI's stated agreement with the letter's requests cannot by itself repair that damage: a company trying to discourage scrutiny could make the same assurances. He wants credible evidence that forceful safety advocacy remains welcome and will not harm careers. Clear rules for working with external organizations are an early test. His argument leaves room for legitimate limits on disclosure while demanding that employees can understand those limits before crossing them. The company's October 9 promise to finalize external-assessor contracts is consequently only part of what restoring trust would require.

A separate October 12 Fortune op-ed, available in an Alignment Forum cross-post, argues for legally binding oversight. Its authors are independent alignment researcher Alex Turner, Back Channel founder Amber Scorah and AI Whistleblower Initiative founder Karl Koch. They argue that outside evaluators cannot scrutinize a company effectively when employees fear dismissal for communicating with them. Their criticism draws on the researchers' disputed accounts and OpenAI's previous delays in incident disclosure. The institutional problem, as they describe it, is that voluntary arrangements let developers control what outsiders learn. They call for mandatory independent evaluations and audits, criminal liability for executives who suppress oversight or recklessly deploy dangerous models, protection for people cooperating with evaluators, and compulsory incident reporting.

Their whistleblower proposal combines support for Senator Chuck Grassley's AI Whistleblower Protection Act with an explicit extension to evaluator cooperation. The introduced text of S. 1792 would protect specified disclosures to regulators, law enforcement, Congress and people within the employer who supervise the worker or are reasonably believed to have authority to address misconduct. It does not expressly name independent AI evaluators as a reporting channel. The authors want that relationship clearly protected; the text alone does not settle whether any particular fired researcher would qualify under its provisions. The bill would provide reinstatement, double back pay and damages. Their proposed criminal liability would add a different form of enforcement.

The op-ed's comparison with existing criminal law is also specific. 18 U.S.C. §1513(e) permits up to ten years' imprisonment for knowingly retaliating against someone for truthfully informing law enforcement about a possible federal offense. It does not automatically extend that protection to every exchange with a private evaluator. For corporate disclosure, the authors back Representative Nathaniel Moran's AI Incident Reporting Act. According to Moran's June 25 announcement, developers of models meeting Commerce's risk thresholds would have seven days to report dangerous activity, with Commerce notifying congressional leaders within 48 hours for the most serious incidents. That would create a reporting duty independent of whether an employee was willing to come forward. The authors' remedies therefore address both access to information and the consequences of obstructing it.
