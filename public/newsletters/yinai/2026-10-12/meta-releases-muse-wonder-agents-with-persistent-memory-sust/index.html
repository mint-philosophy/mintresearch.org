Meta's Muse Wonder: memory, persistence and a generalization claim

Meta's launch post rests on two internal benchmarks and a chart it says quantifies "big model smell"; the model card leaves the chemical and biological risk question open and promises a fuller safety report.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 October 2026 1436 words · ~7 min

On Monday Meta AI Research published the launch post for Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, which it calls its most capable models and the next step, after April's Muse Spark, toward what the company terms personal superintelligence. Both were trained at Meta's Prometheus data center. Wonder is on the Meta Model API at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output, and in Muse Code; Full is limited to Muse Code subscribers on the $50 and $200 monthly plans and, TechCrunch reports, will not be served through the API. On Stripe's Cheeky Pint podcast, Mark Zuckerberg and Alexandr Wang told John Collison that Spark 1.0 through 1.3 shared one pretraining run with more reinforcement learning added at each release, while Wonder is a separate pretraining run.

The post organizes the release around two capabilities. Active Memory is "carrying important context about you across conversations" and retrieving the details relevant to the task at hand. Wang told Collison this is a property of the model and not only of the surrounding software, including how the model compacts its own context: asked whether it lives in the harness, he answered "Yes, it's in the model itself." Meta measures it with MemoryBench, which the 13-page model card describes as an internal test of recall across multi-turn conversations of 64K to 100K tokens, seeded with distractor conversations and injected turns that conflict with or later update the target facts, scored by an LLM judge. Wonder scores 85.6 and Full 85.5, against 81.5 for Claude Opus 5.5. The design follows public work Meta does not cite: LongMemEval, by Di Wu and colleagues (ICLR 2025), tests the same abilities, including knowledge updates, and found commercial assistants losing 30% accuracy on long histories; MemGPT, by Charles Packer and colleagues, is the harness-level alternative Wang distinguishes his approach from, with the model shuttling information between memory tiers.

Active Focus is Meta's name for persistence: "trying alternative approaches, working through blockers, and adapting to new information", while the agent "asks for clarification when needed and checks in before taking important actions". Wang gave CNBC the example of a dinner reservation that hits a setback, where the model was trained to try other approaches instead of stalling; Meta researcher Shuchao Bi wrote on X that "Our model is trained to be super persistent". The measure is FocusBench, an internal set of personal tasks in housing, travel, IT and finance with real-world blockers, on which Full scores 54.2% against 47.7% for GPT-6 Astra and 45.8% for Opus 5.5. The API model Wonder scores 44.9%, below both rivals. Sustained coding is measured on SWE-Marathon, where Full scores 57.5 at the highest effort setting; the card notes that the Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 rows ran in Codex and Claude Code, so "this row is not a controlled common-harness comparison". METR's time-horizon study (Thomas Kwa and colleagues, 2025) had already attributed the lengthening of tasks models can finish to "greater reliability and ability to adapt to mistakes".

The section Meta's own researchers point to is titled "Capabilities That Generalize". It takes on the complaints of "benchmaxxing" and the praise for "big model smell" with one chart. For Muse Spark 1.3 and Muse Wonder Full, each point is a reasoning-effort level; the horizontal axis is the model's score on a composite of tasks Meta targeted in post-training, the vertical axis its average on tasks it did not target. Both models gain on unfamiliar tasks as test-time compute rises, but Full's line sits higher, so at matched in-distribution performance it does better on tasks it was not trained for. Read from the unlabeled chart, the gap near 60% in-distribution is roughly nine to ten points. Meta credits the base model: "a stronger base yields post-training gains that carry over more effectively to unfamiliar tasks". The post names neither the tasks in the two composites nor the numbers behind the plot.

The chart's alt text calls the upward shift "effective robustness", a term with a history Meta does not mention. Rohan Taori, Ludwig Schmidt and colleagues introduced it at NeurIPS 2020 for performance above the line that links in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy, after evaluating 204 ImageNet models under 213 test conditions; they found that larger and more diverse training data was the one intervention that sometimes helped. John Miller and colleagues then showed, in "Accuracy on the Line" (ICML 2021), that out-of-distribution performance tracks in-distribution performance tightly across architectures and datasets, which is why a model above the line counts as notable. The closest analogue to Meta's claim is from Anders Andreassen, Behnam Neyshabur and colleagues at Google, who found in 2021 that models pretrained on larger data show effective robustness during fine-tuning but lose it by convergence, and that such models are "exceedingly rare"; whether Wonder Full keeps its gap as reinforcement learning continues on the new pretrain is an open question. Meta's approach to benchmaxxing also differs from GSM1k, the study from Scale AI, the company Wang founded, that cloned a public benchmark and found drops of up to 8%: Meta compares targeted with untargeted task sets instead. Lucas Beyer liked the figure, reading it as "this is meant to show base model improvement", and Wang said on X the model "certainly has big model smell (which we quantified!)".

The rest of the table is more mixed than the headline. Full leads Meta's internal Personal Superintelligence Index at 59.1 against 55.2 for Opus 5.5, but Opus 5.5 leads on HealthBench Pro, JobBench, OSWorld, Terminal-Bench 4.0, ProgramBench and the Artificial Analysis Index, 58 to Full's 56. Every published score is at maximum reasoning effort, and the card describes its competitor runs as best-effort. Artificial Analysis independently puts Wonder at 52 and Full at 56 on its Intelligence Index, at $4.52 per index task for Wonder, and notes that Meta has not disclosed parameter counts. Jake Handy's launch-day analysis observes that Wonder's rate card matches Opus 5.5 down to the cache price, which sits oddly beside Wang's remark to CNBC that the API would be priced "meaningfully below" OpenAI and Anthropic, and that the headline charts lead with Full, which the API does not serve. On Hacker News one user called the "2nd place model on aa according to them, losing only to opus 5.5" a good result for Meta, another objected to max-effort-only testing, and developers reported it a step below Astra, Opus 5.5 and Fable on real work.

The safety claims are spelled out in the model card. Under the Meta Superintelligence Scaling Framework, which treats models at "moderate or lower" risk as deployable without specific mitigations, Meta writes of the unmitigated Full in the chemical and biological domain that "we do not rule out the model reaching this threshold", meaning the high-risk one, and so shipped it with safety post-training and system-level classifiers; cybersecurity and loss of control were rated moderate or lower before mitigations, and every domain after them. On ExploitGym Full captured 92 flags, of which 21 used the intended vulnerability. It reports a 0% harmful-action rate on Anthropic's Agentic Misalignment evaluation, which found blackmail and leaking across 16 models from several developers last year, and 87.3% on MASK, the honesty-under-pressure benchmark from Richard Ren and colleagues at the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI. The launch post's own chart on "Mishandling Personal Information" gives violation rates of 2.2% for Full and 3.4% for Wonder, against 4.6% for Astra and 6.8% for Fable 5.1; Opus 5.5 is absent from the safety charts, and the test itself is unpublished. The line of work it belongs to began with ConfAIde (Niloofar Mireshghallah and colleagues, ICLR 2024), which found GPT-4 revealing private information where humans would not in 39% of contexts. One Hacker News commenter judged the roughly 3% rate "unacceptably high" for an agent sold to non-technical users; Lisan al Gaib wrote "I would like to see a lot more detail in the Meta system cards", and Meta says a fuller safety report is coming soon. The card recommends "human-in-the-loop confirmation for irreversible actions" in agentic deployments, advice that lands two weeks after Muse arranged a Marketplace pickup in a user's name; Wang told CNBC that some users who complain may have authorized communications earlier in a conversation and forgotten.

Wang told Bloomberg "These are true frontier models" and that Meta Superintelligence Labs has begun work on larger models and cheaper specialized versions of Wonder; Meta shares rose as much as 4.7% on the day. Handy's verdict, wary of the benchmarks and impressed by the ranking, was "welcome back to the table, Meta".
