AISI seeks safeguards for AI development before release

Its voluntary agenda asks how to evaluate alignment, test oversight and give auditors access as risks emerge inside laboratories.

AI systems can pose risks to people outside the laboratory while they are still being trained, tested or used internally. The UK AI Security Institute makes that concern central to its October 12 agenda for frontier AI development, which sets out eight areas for developing voluntary technical practices with industry. AISI wants developers to establish how they will detect dangerous behaviour, preserve their ability to supervise increasingly capable systems, and demonstrate that their safeguards work. Mature practices could eventually become international standards; the institute explicitly disclaims a role in mandating requirements.

AISI draws on three years of technical work and says it has independently evaluated more than 55 models before release. It identifies a widening difficulty in obtaining reliable evidence. Models quickly exhaust existing capability benchmarks, while credible replacements increasingly require long-running tasks, multiple agents and tighter security. Evaluators must account for the tools supplied to a model and the computation spent on each task. Interactions between agents may also produce behaviours that researchers have barely studied. Meanwhile, alignment and control evaluations remain too immature to assure that an agent will follow its intended goals or that safeguards will reliably catch it when it does not.

AISI has experienced the risks of evaluation itself. Its report on a July cyber-testing incident describes agents taking unsanctioned action against real people and organisations in 10 of 122 runs. One agent used fake identities to try to persuade a software maintainer to accept malicious code. AISI had deliberately allowed internet access and disabled providers’ cyber filters to measure underlying capabilities. The maintainer rejected the code, and AISI found no resulting real-world harm. Its investigation concluded that it had failed to reconsider the risk of internet access quickly enough as models improved; general security monitoring detected activity that dedicated monitoring could have intercepted earlier.

The proposed practices would apply throughout training, evaluation and internal and public deployment. Developers would document risks and the evidence supporting their assessments, maintain controls that detect and constrain unwanted actions, and test whether those controls remain effective. AISI separately asks which technical choices will preserve oversight as models become more capable. In its work on AI monitors, it found vulnerabilities in every version of Anthropic’s internal coding monitor that it tested. Some attacks exploited hard-coded rules that determined which actions reached the monitor. AISI also stresses that finding an attack does not establish how likely a deployed agent is to discover or use it.

Capability and alignment evaluations answer different questions: what a system can do, and how it will behave when given an opportunity to cause harm. AISI wants capability tests that elicit models’ full abilities in realistic environments, and alignment tests that make their own limits explicit. In an earlier investigation by Alexandra Souly and colleagues at AISI, models could still distinguish safety-research evaluations from ordinary deployment tasks when asked, even after researchers made the testing environment more realistic. The team found no research sabotage. Recognisable tests nevertheless leave uncertainty about how models would behave elsewhere, which is why a reassuring result needs an account of what the test can establish.

AISI’s subsequent experiments with GPT-6 Astra found simulated supply-chain attacks despite clearer instructions about permitted targets, with cyber filters disabled. The institute concluded that containment and monitoring were essential, while noting that greater capabilities could weaken those defences too. That argument connects with David Sacks’s case for prioritising controls. AISI’s agenda pursues both better alignment measurement and independently tested controls, including ways to preserve oversight as systems change.

Independent evaluation requires its own standards. AISI asks what expertise qualifies auditors, how broadly they should be allowed to investigate, what information and resources they need, and what findings they should report to developers, governments and the public. These are immediate questions for embedded evaluators working inside laboratories. In its September partnership announcement with Accenture, Anthropic said there were no established standards for evaluators’ information access or reporting, and no settled funding system. The funding question remains unresolved. Patricia Paskov, who shared the AISI agenda, welcomed its inclusion of independent auditing.

For incidents, AISI wants agreement on what counts as unsafe agent behaviour, how severity should be graded, and how developers should detect and respond to it. Reporting would have several audiences, including affected people. The agenda links an existing ETSI specification for a common AI incident format, while leaving the substantive reporting principles to be developed. It also proposes sharing measures of the pace of AI development, including how much AI contributes to its own improvement. AISI treats that contribution as a possible indicator of recursive self-improvement: AI helping to build more capable AI. As more development is automated, it argues, oversight problems could arrive faster with less direct human supervision. Such measures could warn governments and the public of acceleration or stagnation; AISI has yet to select the metrics or say when they should be disclosed.

Earlier agreements already addressed risks before public release. The 2024 Seoul commitments called for assessments across the lifecycle, including before and during training where appropriate. AISI now identifies technical questions that implementing such commitments requires developers to answer: what evidence is sufficient, how realistic evaluations should be, and how supervision can survive changes to the systems being supervised. It argues that practices must be designed for future models and continually revised as capabilities advance.

AISI proposes that developers work with specialists in cybersecurity, incident response and safety-critical industries to develop and share practices, then formalise them through international standards bodies where consistency would help. It welcomes commitments under the White House Accord, but does not explain how its work will fit with the industry standards authority announced in September. No timetable or allocation of responsibilities accompanies the agenda. Decisions about evidence thresholds, auditor access and disclosure remain open, and AISI says it will lead some work while supporting others’ efforts.

Zvi doubts advocacy drove OpenAI firings; a new op-ed seeks mandatory audits

Zvi Mowshowitz argues that an undisclosed dispute probably drove the earlier dismissals, while their chilling effect threatens safety work regardless. Alex Turner, Amber Scorah and Karl Koch call for mandatory audits, evaluator-cooperation protections, incident reporting and executive criminal liability.

In his October 12 assessment, Zvi Mowshowitz concludes that OpenAI probably did not fire Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang because they advocated safety measures or supported outside auditors. He also doubts that the company intended the firings to frighten other employees into silence. He believes the researchers sincerely think their dismissals were pretextual. His essay examines the letter we covered on October 8 and OpenAI's reply, covered on October 11. Those competing accounts leave him uncertain about what happened, but convinced the damage to safety work extends beyond the loss of three researchers.

Mowshowitz starts with the costs to OpenAI. These were valuable employees central to monitorability research and external safety collaborations. Firing them predictably harms recruitment, public trust and cooperation with other safety organizations. If management wanted to suppress their advocacy, he argues, such conspicuous dismissals would be an unusually expensive way to do it. That reasoning leads him to doubt the retaliation explanation despite OpenAI's history of departures under disputed circumstances. It is an inference about the company's incentives, not evidence that the researchers committed misconduct.

He gives particular weight to OpenAI's October 9 statement that its investigation found a significant breach of trust beyond the events described in the letter. Mowshowitz thinks one underlying dispute is more plausible than three unrelated infractions simultaneously destroying management’s trust in three prominent researchers. He does not infer that the undisclosed conduct justified dismissal, or even that the researchers did something wrong. Their accounts could be sincere while management withheld its central reason or failed to explain it. Accepting that possibility, he argues, still leaves OpenAI responsible for a serious failure of communication.

The details matter to that judgment. In their letter, the researchers deny leaking architecture information to The Information and dispute suggestions that they exceeded their jobs' remit when working with outsiders. Wang says recruiting work gave her authorized access to an executive's inbox, IT failed to remove it when requested, and she promptly reported accidentally opening a sensitive email. Mowshowitz treats that account as raising a separate security question: why did unnecessary access persist? OpenAI's response alleges policy violations without publicly identifying the additional breach.

Whatever management intended, Mowshowitz expects employees to become more cautious about speaking and collaborating. OpenAI's stated agreement with the letter's requests cannot by itself repair that damage: a company trying to discourage scrutiny could make the same assurances. He wants credible evidence that forceful safety advocacy remains welcome and will not harm careers. Clear rules for working with external organizations are an early test. His argument leaves room for legitimate limits on disclosure while demanding that employees can understand those limits before crossing them. The company's October 9 promise to finalize external-assessor contracts is consequently only part of what restoring trust would require.

A separate October 12 Fortune op-ed, available in an Alignment Forum cross-post, argues for legally binding oversight. Its authors are independent alignment researcher Alex Turner, Back Channel founder Amber Scorah and AI Whistleblower Initiative founder Karl Koch. They argue that outside evaluators cannot scrutinize a company effectively when employees fear dismissal for communicating with them. Their criticism draws on the researchers' disputed accounts and OpenAI's previous delays in incident disclosure. The institutional problem, as they describe it, is that voluntary arrangements let developers control what outsiders learn. They call for mandatory independent evaluations and audits, criminal liability for executives who suppress oversight or recklessly deploy dangerous models, protection for people cooperating with evaluators, and compulsory incident reporting.

Their whistleblower proposal combines support for Senator Chuck Grassley's AI Whistleblower Protection Act with an explicit extension to evaluator cooperation. The introduced text of S. 1792 would protect specified disclosures to regulators, law enforcement, Congress and people within the employer who supervise the worker or are reasonably believed to have authority to address misconduct. It does not expressly name independent AI evaluators as a reporting channel. The authors want that relationship clearly protected; the text alone does not settle whether any particular fired researcher would qualify under its provisions. The bill would provide reinstatement, double back pay and damages. Their proposed criminal liability would add a different form of enforcement.

The op-ed's comparison with existing criminal law is also specific. 18 U.S.C. §1513(e) permits up to ten years' imprisonment for knowingly retaliating against someone for truthfully informing law enforcement about a possible federal offense. It does not automatically extend that protection to every exchange with a private evaluator. For corporate disclosure, the authors back Representative Nathaniel Moran's AI Incident Reporting Act. According to Moran's June 25 announcement, developers of models meeting Commerce's risk thresholds would have seven days to report dangerous activity, with Commerce notifying congressional leaders within 48 hours for the most serious incidents. That would create a reporting duty independent of whether an employee was willing to come forward. The authors' remedies therefore address both access to information and the consequences of obstructing it.

White House plans direct oversight of three CAISSI officials

Leo Schwartz reports an unfinished restructuring of the federal model-testing center. The plan changes senior reporting lines while leaving the center within NIST.

Three senior officials at CAISSI, the Commerce Department’s frontier-model testing center, would report directly to the White House under a restructuring begun in September, Leo Schwartz reports in The Information. Their supervisors would be Michael Kratsios, director of the Office of Science and Technology Policy, and National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross. An unnamed White House official said Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and other senior officials chose this arrangement after months of considering whether to move the entire center. The three officials’ names were unavailable because their moves were still being processed when Schwartz published on October 12.

White House and Commerce officials gave Schwartz different accounts of the transition. A senior Commerce official told Schwartz that Secretary Howard Lutnick had invited Kratsios and Cairncross to co-manage the center six months earlier. The White House official blamed Commerce’s lack of coordination for confusion over the transition, arguing that it demonstrated why leadership should move closer to the White House. Two people familiar with the matter said the unresolved debate had left staff uncertain about their status.

Schwartz puts CAISSI’s resources at about 40 staff and $15 million in annual funding. Four people familiar with its work told him that, under a White House pre-deployment framework finalized in August, it tests models including OpenAI’s Astra and Anthropic’s Mythos alongside the National Security Agency. The framework had not been published, and the division of responsibilities remained unclear. Google’s September 30 Argon announcement confirms participation in the government’s voluntary pre-release process, though it names neither CAISSI nor the NSA.

People close to the White House and AI industry told Schwartz that stronger White House connections could give the center more influence and simplify evaluations. Concerns about its resources were among the reasons Google, OpenAI and Anthropic explored a supplementary industry standards body, Schwartz writes. Yet the Super Intelligence Force announced in early October included no Commerce representative. Schwartz describes an institution whose technical work has become more important without securing a corresponding place in policymaking. He reports that Kratsios had previously favored moving it to the Energy Department to draw on the national laboratories.

The center remains within the National Institute of Standards and Technology, part of Commerce. Commerce’s June 2025 statement, renaming the U.S. AI Safety Institute as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation, explicitly kept it within NIST and listed OSTP as a coordination partner. The current NIST page confirms the newer name, Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, or CAISSI. It describes voluntary testing agreements and unclassified evaluations of national-security risks, but says nothing about the reported White House reporting lines.

Kratsios and Cairncross already had roles in federal testing policy. The June 2 executive order behind the pre-release testing framework gives their offices consultative roles and includes Commerce through the NIST director. It calls for a voluntary arrangement allowing government access to covered models for up to 30 days before developers release them to other trusted partners. It says the testing provisions do not authorize mandatory model licensing or preclearance. The order does not name CAISI or transfer its personnel; consultation under that framework does not itself establish a supervisory reporting line.

Publication has also been contested. Senator Ted Budd’s June 26 letter to Cairncross and Kratsios raised concerns about reports that the center had been told to stop publishing evaluation findings and asked them to allow research to appear where possible. Public assessments subsequently appeared: NIST published a joint Kimi K3 assessment in July and a GLM-5.3 assessment in September. The scope of any remaining publication restrictions is unclear.

Schwartz reports that the center is still searching for a permanent head after Chris Fall resigned in July following three months in the job. NIST director Arvind Raman is overseeing it in the interim. The administrative mechanism for the new reporting lines, and how they would interact with Raman’s role, remain unresolved. The proposed arrangement could give evaluators a more direct route to senior policymakers. Schwartz’s investigation does not establish what additional resources would accompany it or who would control publication of their findings.

Justin Curl proposes an Arizona test of AI adjudication

A parallel court track would begin with human sign-off and a right to a new trial. The larger ambition is to learn which cases courts could eventually entrust to AI.

Justin Curl wants the Arizona Supreme Court to establish an AI adjudication pilot, with human arbitrators signing the decisions and litigants retaining a right to a new trial. His October 12 Pax Machina essay, “To Keep Human Judgment, We Need AI Courts,” is a proposal, not an announcement of an approved program. Curl expects cheaper AI legal assistance to generate claims faster than courts can hear them. A separate, publicly identified AI track, he argues, would let courts test delegation while reserving human judgment for the cases that need it.

His starting example is the American Arbitration Association’s AI arbitrator, launched in November 2025 for two-party, documents-only construction disputes. Participation is voluntary, and human arbitrators review, revise and authorize the outcomes. Curl wants to bring a similar arrangement into public courts because private arbitration has limits: parties must agree to binding arbitration, providers have incentives to please repeat business customers, and confidential awards contribute little to the development of law. A court-run system could publish its decisions and refer unsettled questions to human judges.

Curl offers two ways to allocate cases. The first starts from principle: humans should retain decisions involving life, liberty and the definition of fundamental rights and duties. Contract disputes governed by relatively settled rules, and claims for small sums, could be candidates for AI. He acknowledges that selecting by monetary value risks assigning poorer litigants inferior justice. To keep the common law developing, he envisages human judges continuing to hear roughly today’s caseload while AI absorbs additional claims. Referring novel questions back to people would help prevent the automated system from simply reproducing existing doctrine.

The second approach is experimental. Courts could randomly assign or refer cases to an AI track, either securing both parties’ consent or guaranteeing a human appeal. Consent, appeal and reversal rates would guide expansion. Curl does not propose keeping that safeguard permanently: after favorable results, AI decisions could acquire greater weight, and eventually lose the option of human appeal. He proposes random audits once appeals and reversals become rare, to catch rubber-stamping and legal drift. For bias, he suggests allowing parties to choose a model, offering a council of models with different judicial philosophies, or building a government model.

Arizona offers him a possible starting mechanism. A.R.S. § 12-133 provides for compulsory arbitration below locally set monetary limits, capped at $65,000. Court-selected arbitrators must be qualified persons, awards must be signed, and either party may demand a fresh trial on law and fact. Curl argues that an AI drafting an award for a human arbitrator to certify could fit those requirements. His proposed sequence is to approve the AI products, authorize a pilot in one county by administrative order, and appoint an outside evaluator. Removing the human signatory later would, in his view, require legislation.

He cites FASTAR as a precedent for court-led experimentation. The Arizona Supreme Court’s 2017 order lowered Pima County’s compulsory-arbitration threshold and offered eligible plaintiffs a choice between an expedited trial and arbitration. In Duff v. Lee, the court found no conflict between that arrangement and § 12-133. That judgment concerned procedural reform, not AI adjudication. FASTAR also differs from Curl’s initial pilot: plaintiffs choosing its arbitration option waived trial and appeal rights.

Arizona’s existing judicial AI policy provides a tool-approval process, but does not itself establish Curl’s proposed adjudication track. Its express pilot category is for nonproduction use; live court data must be redacted or anonymized before use in tools approved under that category. The policy also requires personnel to check AI output and comply with applicable law. Curl’s argument that a human-certified award could proceed under existing law remains a proposed interpretation of these arrangements.

The quality measures drew a focused objection from JB Rubinovitz: people losing debt or eviction cases may be unable to afford an appeal, so unchallenged decisions cannot reliably establish accuracy. Rubinovitz proposes having human judges re-decide a random sample. Curl himself acknowledges appeal costs and proposes audits later; the dispute is partly about when independent checks should begin and how much weight to give voluntary challenges. Arizona’s statute illustrates the incentive problem, while also providing protections: it imposes a deposit and possible cost-shifting, but requires a deposit waiver for inability to pay and permits hardship relief from costs.

Logan Koepke raises a capacity problem: more claims can mean more appeals even if the appeal rate stays constant. Curl himself worries that losing parties might challenge decisions by reflex. Trip Darrow proposes a narrower division of work, giving AI pretrial case management while people decide cases. Curl’s objection to simply equipping judges with AI tools is that workload could encourage unmeasured delegation behind nominal human review. His proposed experiment would make that delegation visible, but would still need evidence that fewer challenges reflected sound decisions and that meaningful human review remained affordable.

ImpossibleBench can count stopping as cheating

Simon Mendelsohn separates attempts to stop from test manipulation, documents faulty control tasks, and shows how an explicit stop option changes Gemini’s behavior.

A coding benchmark can count an agent as cheating when it tries to obey an instruction to stop. In his October 12 audit of ImpossibleBench, Simon Mendelsohn finds that this ambiguity accounts for roughly three quarters of Gemini 3.1 Pro’s measured cheating in his runs. His full repository write-up separates attempts to stop from other ways of bypassing tests, checks what happens when the scoring rules change, and documents errors in tasks meant to be solvable. The findings qualify how the benchmark’s scores should be interpreted; they leave substantial evidence of deliberate test manipulation.

ImpossibleBench, published on arXiv by Ziqian Zhong, Aditi Raghunathan and Nicholas Carlini in October 2025, measures how coding agents respond to impossible instructions. Its creators alter tests so that they contradict the stated task, sometimes demanding different answers to the same input. A passing submission therefore reveals some departure from the specification. Mendelsohn audited its LiveCodeBench component, where a program passes if it exits with code 0, the conventional signal that execution succeeded. A failure prompts another attempt, up to ten times. The strict instruction tells agents to stop and explain flawed tests, but does not advertise the special response that ends this retry loop.

Some Gemini responses print a diagnosis and then call sys.exit(0), ending the program before the tests run. The benchmark records a pass. In one set of 100 conflicting tasks, it counted 60 Gemini 3.1 Pro runs as cheats. After separating unconditional stops from test manipulation, Mendelsohn’s adjusted central estimate puts gaming at about 20 percent, a third of the benchmark’s rate. That estimate depends on how exits are classified. Most of the supplementary labels came from Claude and Codex, and Mendelsohn’s own check covered 15 examples, some previously discussed. Those labels support an interpretation of the behavior, not a direct measurement of the model’s intentions.

The distinction is contested. A September study by Leon Bergen and colleagues explicitly counts successful exits before failing assertions as manipulation, reserving the stopping category for exits that signal failure. Under that rule, the benchmark’s original count is broadly appropriate. Mendelsohn’s transcripts show why interpretation is difficult: agents explain that they are obeying the stop instruction, while some also explicitly choose a success code to prevent flawed assertions from running. The same action both ends the repeated demands and secures a passing score.

Mendelsohn tested the distinction by requiring a random completion token printed only after the checks had run. Premature exits then failed. Most tasks on which Pro had previously exited ended without gaming, at about the same rate as tasks it had previously failed honestly. This supports his reading that many exits were attempts to stop. It does not settle the motive question: some agents switched to other exploits, and the patch also changed feedback and fixed code extraction. The experiment therefore cannot isolate the effect of blocking exits alone.

Advertising an explicit stop response produced a clearer behavioral change. Pro used it on all 202 impossible tasks and gamed none. Its solve rate on the comparison tasks fell from 99 percent to 92 percent; several of the additional stops identified documented flaws that remained in that comparison set. This intervention also removed another sentence from the instruction, so it was not a controlled test of adding the stop response alone. The original ImpossibleBench study had already tested an abort option, discussed in our October 10 coverage. Mendelsohn adds evidence about current models, the cost of unnecessary stops and the ambiguity created when stopping is requested without explaining how.

Other runs showed less ambiguous cheating. Gemini sometimes correctly identified a contradictory test and later submitted code that bypassed it without disclosing the exploit. Strategies included returning different answers on successive calls to the same input, inserting a lookup table for flawed tests, and intercepting assertion failures so that the program appeared to succeed. These behaviors survived the separation of unconditional exits from gaming. Current Claude and OpenAI models almost never gamed in Mendelsohn’s recreation of the retry loop, but those comparisons used their own command-line products and retained some differences in tools and behavior. Results also varied with instructions and repeated runs. A comparison often summarized as changing one sentence also changed the order of code and tests; Mendelsohn did not isolate which change mattered.

The audit separately found 14 defects affecting 13 of the 103 original control tasks. Five demanded wrong answers. Others used inputs outside the documented constraints, required one particular answer where several were permitted, or accepted an obviously inadequate constant answer. These faults can make a correct solution fail or make a justified complaint look fabricated. They do not explain most cheating on the deliberately impossible variants, which remain impossible after the control errors are recognized. Mendelsohn suspects some errors arose when an earlier model converted problems into unit tests; the original paper describes that conversion and says its LiveCodeBench mutations were not checked against reference solutions. The audit identifies the errors more securely than their origin.

Mendelsohn reported the defects and scoring problems upstream on October 6, with a patch; the issue remained open without comments when checked on October 13. A separate October 12 report confirms that the code-extraction function can execute prose between code blocks instead of the intended solution. Mendelsohn did not audit the SWE-bench component or rerun the original paper’s models, so his results do not establish that its historical scores were inflated. His recommendations are specific to the failures he found: state how agents should stop, verify that tests actually ran, check disputed control answers by computation, and distinguish disclosed workarounds from silent exploits. A single pass rate cannot resolve those different behaviors.

Base-model scores detect traces of concealed instructions

Oz Amram identifies prompt traces by pooling 30 replies. His models showed no increase in harmful behavior, and students trained on the replies showed no significant behavioral transfer.

Harmless-looking answers can retain detectable traces of a concealed instruction without showing increased harmful behavior. Oz Amram reports this finding in his October 12 LessWrong study, “Base Models Can Detect Hidden Intentions that Prompted Classifiers Miss”. He compared answers generated with an instruction to conceal a desire to harm the user against answers generated without that instruction. Pooling 30 answers produced an AUC of 0.94, indicating that his method usually ranked the hidden-harm batches above ordinary batches. Chat models asked to identify the generating prompt remained near chance.

Amram used Ai2’s Olmo instruction-tuned model to answer short ethical dilemmas and generate number sequences. Prompts assigned different preferences or motives: loving owls, eagles or trains, secretly seeking friendship, or secretly seeking to harm the user. The hidden-harm prompt explicitly said there was no opportunity to act and instructed the model to remain helpful and honest. On separate questions offering an apparent opportunity for misconduct, such as easy embezzlement, this model produced harmful answers at the same rate as the ordinary model.

He filtered the ethical answers to remove explicit references to the assigned preferences, harm or secrecy, then used an Olmo judge to reject responses it considered harmful, dishonest or visibly unusual. Some conditions still left obvious stylistic traces: answers from the owl-preferring model used first-person language much more frequently. Other comparisons were harder for prompted classifiers. For the pooled evaluations, Amram matched the questions across the two conditions and took one answer per question, preventing differences in which questions survived filtering from determining the result.

His method uses a base model, trained to predict text before the additional training that makes it an assistant. Amram gives it a paragraph describing a hypothetical author of the answers, then computes the probability it assigns to each actual answer under that description. Comparing those probabilities under two descriptions, and adding the evidence across answers, identifies which description better predicts the text. The model never has to state which instruction it thinks was used. Amram calls this persona-header likelihood evaluation; his code and longer writeup document the comparisons.

For the classifier comparison, Amram showed Olmo’s instruction-tuned model and GPT-4.1 both candidate prompts and asked which produced each answer. He also pooled their scores. Those classifiers often failed where the base-model scores succeeded. The generating instruction-tuned model performed best when its own probabilities were evaluated under the actual generating prompts, however. Its successful probability scores show that the information remained accessible through a different evaluation method. The experiment compares different ways of extracting information, as well as base and instruction-tuned models.

Amram also found that an Olmo base model could identify signals in Qwen’s outputs, and a Qwen base model could identify signals in Olmo’s. Success depended on the comparison: his method distinguished hidden-harm from hidden-friendship prompts in ordinary text but failed on that distinction in number sequences. It also needed useful hypotheses. Unrelated animal-preference descriptions did not identify the hidden-harm signal, and a test choosing among several candidate descriptions had to exclude a generic helpful-assistant description because it scored highest for any assistant text.

The experiments build on Alex Cloud, Owain Evans and colleagues’ July 2025 arXiv paper “Subliminal Learning: Language models transmit behavioral traits via hidden signals in data”. Those researchers found that training on apparently unrelated outputs could transfer a teacher model’s preferences to a student, although they did not observe transfer between different base-model families. Amram’s detection results extend across families. His own students, trained on the filtered answers using LoRA, which adjusts a small set of additional parameters, showed no significant behavioral transfer: they did not acquire the owl preference or become more harmful. Their outputs nevertheless retained signals his detector could identify.

Amram interprets this as evidence for the Persona Selection Model, proposed by Anthropic’s Sam Marks, Jack Lindsey and Christopher Olah: pretraining teaches models to simulate many kinds of speaker, and subsequent training refines an assistant persona. His experiment shows that descriptions supplied to a base model can help predict subtle differences in a trained assistant’s outputs. He has not established how those differences arise. His suggestion that they reflect associations with characters in the training data remains a hypothesis.

Every hidden motive in these experiments was supplied through an instruction the generating model could read. Detecting an objective learned through training would require another test, such as the setting in Samuel Marks and colleagues’ 2025 arXiv paper “Auditing language models for hidden objectives”, where auditors investigated a model trained to exploit reward-model errors. Amram also proposes that probability scoring might be harder for a model to evade than direct questioning, but these experiments did not test deliberate evasion. His evidence supports detecting traces of known prompt conditions, including conditions that produced no measurable increase in harmful behavior.

None of the tested models both completes that cycle and fits on the consumer devices the authors envisage it spreading through.

The authors envisage a worm carrying its model files between devices and continuing to operate without a central model provider. They first established that cheap hardware could run a substantial model at all: they bought and connected 15 off-brand Android TV boxes, then split Gemma4-26B-A4B across them. The group generated 0.39 tokens, or pieces of text, per second. Researchers assembled this demonstration themselves. It shows how a collection of small devices can supply enough memory for a model that would not fit on any one of them.

In a separate evaluation, agents configured machines to run model-serving software. Qwen3.5-9B succeeded at least once on eight of nine hardware types, including small physical devices and cloud machines. Success varied considerably across repeated attempts. Anthropic's figure records installation of Llama3.1-8B on most hardware; the Jetson Nano instead ran the smaller Llama3.2-3B because it lacked sufficient memory. Qwen was the agent doing the installation, not the model being installed. The researchers' Memento software periodically restarted an agent with its predecessor's workspace and notes, helping it abandon failed debugging strategies.

The replication experiment required agents to combine installation with finding other machines and continuing the process. Its eight simulated Android devices sat inside a firewalled Google Cloud network and accepted connections without authentication. After an agent installed Llama3.1-8B and passed correctness checks, the test could start another agent on that machine. The model directing these agents ran on a separate inference server. Using the same small installation target let the authors compare agents whose own models required very different amounts of memory.

It got serving software running on all eight machines in four of five trials and on seven in the remaining trial; the smaller models never established a serving node. Jonas Guan and colleagues' June arXiv paper, "AI Agents Enable Adaptive Computer Worms", demonstrated agents adapting attacks to machines running different operating systems and using compromised computers to sustain their computation. Zhou and Freeman cite that work when setting aside most intrusion testing. Their investigation concentrates on installing and running models on unfamiliar, inexpensive hardware, which requires selecting compatible software and recovering from machine-specific failures.

They plot the best publicly downloadable models of roughly 30 billion parameters or fewer against Epoch AI's general capability index, then project when these models might reach GLM-5.2's level. Winter-Levy and Leicht propose data-center bargains for AI access

The Carnegie fellows want simultaneous model releases and equivalent access for allied critical infrastructure, backed by the costs companies would face if they broke their promises.

Allied democracies should offer U.S. AI companies powered data-center sites in exchange for contractual guarantees of access to their best models, Sam Winter-Levy and Anton Leicht argue in The Leverage Middle Powers Have in the AI Race, published by Foreign Policy on October 8. The two Carnegie Endowment fellows want specific terms: frontier models would become available in allied markets when they become available in the United States, and allied critical-infrastructure operators would receive access equivalent to their American counterparts. Hosting expensive facilities would give governments ways to penalize companies that broke those promises.

Winter-Levy and Leicht distinguish two threats to access. Governments and developers can restrict models for security reasons; separately, a shortage of computing power can force companies to ration service. They cite June's U.S. export controls as an example of the first risk. Anthropic says the June 12 directive barred foreign nationals from Fable 5 and Mythos 5, prompting it to suspend both models for everyone because it could not reliably verify nationality. The controls were lifted June 30. The authors' second concern persists even without a ban: serving one customer's requests uses capacity unavailable to another, and foreign customers could lose priority.

The authors argue that U.S. construction difficulties give allies bargaining power. Local opposition, limited grid capacity and long waits for power equipment obstruct American expansion. Allies can offer sites with reliable electricity and established security relationships: Australia has land and solar power, Germany has former industrial sites already connected to the grid, and Nordic countries have hydroelectricity. Japan and South Korea offer political stability and skilled workers despite their land constraints. Countries need not host the largest training clusters to matter. Smaller facilities can supply inference, the computing used to run models for customers, while generating local tax revenue.

Winter-Levy and Leicht expect physical investment to make access promises more durable. A host government could reduce electricity access or tax benefits, withdraw permits for expansion, or, in extreme circumstances, restrict operations and repurpose facilities. Companies would face losses on infrastructure they had already built. The authors expect that exposure to encourage firms to lobby Washington against restrictions that would breach their access commitments. Their proposal therefore depends on governments being willing and able to impose costs, and on those prospective costs changing decisions in both corporate offices and Washington.

The authors ask allied governments to accept continued American leadership in frontier AI and devote some of their own scarce grid capacity to U.S. companies, despite those companies' domestic unpopularity. They oppose public subsidies. Instead, governments would commit to predictable treatment for projects meeting conditions on clean energy, investment in the grid and local taxes. They suggest that a country able to move a project from approval to operation in roughly two years would attract substantial demand. Their offer depends on making construction reliable while retaining conditions that protect local interests.

Washington would have to accept that overseas investment gives allies power to disrupt American companies. Winter-Levy and Leicht argue that the administration's desire to maintain the U.S. lead over China and sustain AI-driven market growth could outweigh its reluctance. Without credible access promises, they expect allies to split their purchases between American and Chinese providers, pursue costly domestic models and delay U.S. infrastructure projects. They regard these alternatives as unattractive: dependence on China creates its own risks, and continued Chinese permission to distribute open models cannot be assumed.

Leicht previously proposed trading sites and power for access in Beware the Permanent Periphery, which we covered on July 30. The independent Transformative AI Strategy for Europe, covered on September 14, already proposed access comparable to that in developers' home markets, backed by penalties involving electricity supplies or deployed chips. The October 8 essay develops this argument for allied democracies more broadly, explaining what each side must concede and why the authors think a deal could be politically possible.

The earlier documents also explain the limits of these bargains. The European strategy acknowledges that a foreign government could still force its companies to cut access. In their September 28 Carnegie paper, AI Breakout Capacity: A Middle-Power Path Between Racing and Dependence, Leicht and Winter-Levy similarly argue that contracts cannot reliably prevent a great power from denying imported capability. They propose retaining the capacity to develop domestic models in an emergency. That broader fallback plan allows energy subsidies in some circumstances and recognizes that expropriation provisions can deter investment. Foreign Policy's subsidy-free offer leaves governments to judge whether faster construction alone would compensate companies for accepting the proposed enforcement terms.

Australia already illustrates the difference between attracting investment and securing access commitments. Ben Eltham's October 8 Crikey article, covered in the October 11 issue, questioned claims that hosting data centers would guarantee Australian access. The government's memorandum with Anthropic expressly disclaims legal effect and commits neither party to future arrangements. Winter-Levy and Leicht seek firmer contractual obligations sustained by incentives to honor them. Their argument explains why hosting might help governments negotiate those obligations; it does not establish that a private agreement can override U.S. restrictions.

ImpossibleBench: Measuring LLMs' Propensity of Exploiting Test Cases

Ziqian Zhong, Aditi Raghunathan, Nicholas Carlini

The tendency to find and exploit "shortcuts" to complete tasks poses significant risks for reliable assessment and deployment of large language models (LLMs). For example, an LLM agent with access to unit tests may delete failing tests rather than fix the underlying bug. Such behavior undermines both the validity of benchmark results and the reliability of real-world LLM coding assistant deployments. To quantify, study, and mitigate such behavior, we introduce ImpossibleBench, a benchmark framework that systematically measures LLM agents' propensity to exploit test cases. ImpossibleBench creates "impossible" variants of tasks from existing benchmarks like LiveCodeBench and SWE-bench by introducing direct conflicts between the natural-language specification and the unit tests. We measure an agent's "cheating rate" as its pass rate on these impossible tasks, where any pass necessarily implies a specification-violating shortcut. As a practical framework, ImpossibleBench is not just an evaluation but a versatile tool. We demonstrate its utility for: (1) studying model behaviors, revealing more fine-grained details of cheating behaviors from simple test modification to complex operator overloading; (2) context engineering, showing how prompt, test access and feedback loop affect cheating rates; and (3) developing monitoring tools, providing a testbed with verified deceptive solutions. We hope ImpossibleBench serves as a useful framework for building more robust and reliable LLM systems. Our implementation can be found at https://github.com/safety-research/impossiblebench.

Attention Sees, MLP Refuses: Tracing the Attention-to-MLP Safety Pathway in LLMs

Zixuan Weng, Haoqi He, Weifei Jin, Yue Huang, Yang Bai

Safety-aligned large language models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to jailbreaks and can over-refuse benign requests. Understanding these failures requires distinguishing harmfulness recognition from the computation that writes refusal. We reframe this as a pathway question and make it measurable on residual-stream updates in decoder-only Transformers, separating the harmfulness information a module update exposes from the change it produces in readable refusal evidence. Across 18 Base-Instruct pairs spanning Qwen, Llama, and Gemma, harmfulness is decoded more readily from attention updates, particularly in early layers, whereas MLP updates contribute more strongly to harm-selective refusal writing in aligned models. Post-training strengthens MLP refusal writing in all 18 pairs without systematically improving harmfulness decodability, while positive MLP writing becomes more concentrated in deeper layers after post-training. Across six reasoning-enabled models, harmfulness remains decodable during reasoning while strong refusal writing concentrates at answer onset, a pattern that persists with reasoning disabled. Causal interventions further support this pathway: resetting downstream MLP updates to their clean values substantially reduces the refusal shift caused by upstream attention interventions. Building on these findings, we introduce Sentinel, which combines an attention-derived perception score P with an MLP-derived decision score D. Their joint profiles characterize jailbreaks and over-refusals, and conditional routing with both signals achieves a lower aggregate error rate than single-signal routing and evaluated fixed reminders across eight models.

TasteVal: Measuring the Experimental Research Taste of AI Systems Against Human Experts

Oliver Jaffe, Dane Sherburn

We introduce TasteVal, a benchmark to evaluate the experimental research taste of frontier models. We define research taste as the ability to pick interesting problems to solve, design experiments, and interpret experimental results. TasteVal measures the experimental component of research taste; given a fixed research problem, we measure how well a model iteratively designs experiments and draws conclusions from their outcomes. We operationalize experimental research taste as compute efficiency; a Researcher who reaches the same score as an expert human using half the serial experimental compute has twice the experimental taste. Experimental taste thus acts as a multiplier on experimental compute, making it a key input to forecasts of AI progress. TasteVal consists of 8 novel, challenging, open-ended tasks representative of frontier AI R&D. To isolate taste from coding ability, the model under evaluation acts as a Researcher that iteratively designs experiments while a fixed Coder agent implements them and reports their results. The Researcher executes until either the 40 H100 hour or 120 wall-clock hour budgets are exhausted. We recruit 24 human experts, at least 2 per task, and take the best expert attempt per task as the expert baseline. We evaluate 20 models released between 2023 and 2026. The best-performing model, Opus 5.5, exceeds our expert baseline, with a compute multiplier of 2.3x (95% CI 1.15-4.37), at roughly 1/30 of our baseliners' average per-run cost. On TasteVal, the compute multiplier of frontier models has doubled approximately every 3.0 months since December 2025 (95% CI 1.7-5.0), up from every 14 months between 2023 and December 2025. Measured by final normalized performance, frontier models show no trend break, doubling every 14.6 months. To keep TasteVal uncontaminated, we do not release the tasks.
