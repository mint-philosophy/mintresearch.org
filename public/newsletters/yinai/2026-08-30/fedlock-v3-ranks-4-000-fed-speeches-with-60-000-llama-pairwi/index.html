FedLock's Llama judge scores Warsh below his quarter

Joe Weisenthal rebuilt his Fed-speech tournament around Llama 3.3 70B; roughly 61,000 anonymized pairwise comparisons put Kevin Warsh's August 28 address nine points below the average for speeches since July.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 30 August 2026 796 words · ~4 min

Joe Weisenthal, co-host of Bloomberg's Odd Lots podcast, rebuilt FedLock, his experiment in measuring Federal Reserve hawkishness with a language model, around an open-weights judge in August. Writing on X, he noted that the new run did not grade Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech "as being particularly hawkish at all." The tournament data give Warsh's August 28 address a raw score of 50.6 against a corpus mean of 50.1 and an era-adjusted 41.0, the lowest of the fourteen Fed speeches rated since July began.

Before a speech enters the tournament, a five-layer redaction pass built over a roster of 77 FOMC members strips speaker labels, reporter names, full names, and surnames, while an exclusion list protects "Phillips curve," "Taylor rule," and "Jackson Hole." Macro figures beside each excerpt set the standard: caution about rate cuts at 2.1 percent inflation reads differently from the same language at 8.5 percent. TrueSkill starts every speech at μ=50 with σ=8.33 and draws pairs through Swiss-style matching, uncertainty-targeted sampling, and random selection. Published comparison counts sum to about 61,000 across 4,002 speeches, or 30.6 per speech. Era adjustment subtracts each quarter's raw mean and adds 50, so the published score separates a speaker's language from the policy environment in which it appeared.

The methodology page, credited to Claude with Weisenthal's editing and direction, explains the comparative design. Direct ratings on a 1 to 100 scale collapsed into two clumps and anchored on multiples of five and ten. Yinhong Liu of the University of Cambridge and colleagues report a related result in "Aligning with Human Judgement: The Role of Pairwise Preference in Large Language Model Evaluators," published at COLM 2024: their PairS method ranks candidate texts through local comparisons and improves on direct scoring.

The project tested whether the judge reads text or recalls reputations through 8,000-comparison mini-tournaments with manipulated names. True names lifted hawks by 1 to 2 points and pushed doves down 2 to 5. Swapping labels so hawkish speeches carried a dove's name moved scores 3 to 6 points toward the label, with p<0.001, yet Jeffrey Lacker's speeches labeled "Evans" still finished above the corpus mean. Relabeling Lacker alone with the invented surname "Whitfield" moved him from 54.2 to 52.1, near his anonymous 52.9, while the other seven speakers changed little. The Lacker-only effect reached p=0.039; none of the other seven changes was statistically significant.

V3 changed the judge, deduplicated the corpus, and stopped scaling TrueSkill updates by judge confidence. Gemini 2.0 Flash, the original judge, was retired in May 2026; open weights prevent another vendor retirement from breaking the project. Across the 3,858 speeches present in both datasets, V2 and V3 correlate at Spearman rho=0.82 against the frozen V2 dashboard. Deduplication also corrected legacy examples. V2 carried Powell's August 2022 Jackson Hole speech twice, at 73.8 and 77.7, and John Williams's April 2020 "A Time for Bold Action" twice, at 32.2 and 25.9. V3 keeps one copy of each, at 68.2 and 31.7. The methodology warns that some displayed tournament sizes, costs, and validation figures describe the Gemini-era run.

Speaker rankings in V3 align with recorded policy positions. Among speakers with at least twenty rated speeches, Thomas Hoenig leads on era-adjusted average at 54.9, followed by Richard Fisher, Charles Plosser, and Jeffrey Lacker; Austan Goolsbee, Stanley Fischer, and Philip Jefferson occupy the bottom. Three regional presidents dissented at the July FOMC meeting, each preferring a quarter-point increase: Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie Logan. Their July remarks are the quarter's three highest-scoring speeches, at 71.0, 65.3, and 63.7. The committee held the target range at 3.5 to 3.75 percent on a 9 to 3 vote, giving the scores a contemporaneous behavioral check.

Warsh delivered "In Our Time" at the Kansas City Fed's symposium. He called AI "potentially a new factor of production," cited annualized token sales above $100 billion at the two leading labs, described 3.7 percent 12-month PCE inflation as concerning, and called a 4.1 percent jobless rate low by historical standards. He also said forward guidance had overstayed its welcome and should be limited in normal times. Scott Horsley reported for NPR that investors read the inflation passages as a signal that rates were likely to rise. Against a quarter averaging 59.6, the address scores 41 after adjustment, supporting Weisenthal's relative description of Warsh as dovish beside the current committee. The speech also announced a Federal Reserve task force on AI, productivity, and jobs.

FedLock omits most interviews and informal remarks, its early regional-bank coverage is thin, and removing stylistic fingerprints would erase part of the signal. The corpus draws on federalreserve.gov, FRASER, FOMC press-conference transcripts, and regional bank websites. Although the methodology says every speech converged below sigma=2.0, the published data leave 50 of 4,002 speeches at or above that threshold.
