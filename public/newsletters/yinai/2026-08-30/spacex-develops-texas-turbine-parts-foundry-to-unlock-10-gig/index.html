SpaceX moves to cast scarce turbine blades in Texas

Job listings, land records, and a Morgan Stanley note place a blades-and-vanes foundry in Bastrop; hours after The Information published, Musk said in-house casting could bring gas turbines online up to 18 months sooner.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 30 August 2026 840 words · ~4 min

In The Information's AI Infrastructure newsletter on August 29, Ann Davis Vaughan reported that SpaceX has taken steps to build a foundry in Bastrop, Texas, about 30 miles from Austin, to cast the vanes and blades inside large gas turbines. She builds the case from SpaceX job listings: a senior operations engineer to own the plant's construction and ramp-up, plus materials, automation, and machining specialists for what one posting calls a new industrial-gas-turbine blades-and-vanes manufacturing line. Morgan Stanley analyst Adam Jonas told her his research points the same way. SpaceX did not answer her requests for comment.

SpaceX's job board supplies the metallurgy. The materials engineering listing seeks five years in metal casting and prefers single-crystal or directionally solidified work in nickel superalloys, including experience casting industrial-gas-turbine blades and vanes. The automation listing describes a Blades and Vanes Investment Casting Manufacturing Team that "produces components for power generation." The operations listing gives its hire responsibility for "all required city, county, and governmental approvals and permits."

Vaughan traces the scarcity to four casting companies controlling parts engineered for 3,000 to 3,600 degrees Fahrenheit, roughly 800 degrees above the alloys' melting point. Their full order books help explain why turbine makers are sold out into 2030. Power Engineering reported in April that GE Vernova held about 100 gigawatts of heavy-duty turbines under contract, with roughly 10 gigawatts of slots left across 2029 and 2030. Scott Strazik told the publication that turbines were "really not the gating item," pointing to construction schedules and permits.

Musk described both the bottleneck and his proposed remedy in February. On Dwarkesh Patel's February 5 podcast with John Collison, he said turbines were sold out through 2030 and that only three casting companies made the components, then predicted SpaceX and Tesla would probably make blades and vanes internally. The Bastrop listings show SpaceX acting on that prediction.

Jonas reads the foundry as serving two SpaceX businesses and one constrained industrial workforce. His report says Raptor turbopump castings and data-center turbine airfoils draw on the same alloys, furnaces, and skilled labor, letting one plant spread fixed costs across rockets and AI. For interim supply, he put Musk within reach of 3 to 4 gigawatts by the end of 2027. A Federal Trade Commission filing in May showed Fortress Investment Group selling APR Energy to Musk personally; Latitude Media placed that mobile-turbine fleet above one gigawatt. FTAI Aviation, another supplier candidate named by Jonas, announced on July 22 that its Jereh Group joint venture had received a $1.465 billion order from an unnamed international cloud provider, with deliveries through November 2027.

Corey Trinetti, who publishes the AI-infrastructure newsletter Measured AI, supplied the site evidence: SpaceX bought about 830 acres near its Bastrop Starlink factory between March and June, and two buildings absent a year earlier appeared beside the factory in July satellite imagery. Trinetti found no foundry permits or construction filings in Texas environmental or building records, so the permitting stage remains unclear.

Musk confirmed the project on X about two hours after the newsletter appeared. He wrote that SpaceX and Tesla are each building 100 gigawatts a year of solar-production capacity, while gas remains necessary to supplement and bootstrap solar for several years. In-house casting, he said, could accelerate gas turbines by up to 18 months. An hour later he added a consensus estimate that about 15 gigawatts of AI compute produced in 2027 could not turn on that year, with transformers, wiring, cooling, and networking compounding the constraint.

VanEck's Matthew Sigel put numbers to the schedule in June after spotting the April listings: 18 to 24 months to build and commission a vacuum-casting foundry, two to three more years to qualify a single-crystal process SpaceX has never run, and two to five years for turbine-maker supplier approval. That timetable places early production around 2028 and distinguishes casting initial parts from qualifying them for commercial turbine makers. Vaughan similarly notes that replicating decades of Precision Castparts expertise could take years. AI cloud providers told her Musk has the capital and willingness to bootstrap his own power, while Jigar Shah, who ran the federal energy loan office under President Biden, questioned his infrastructure competence.

The accelerated gas buildout carries an emissions cost. BloombergNEF analysts counted 99 proposed data-center gas plants that would emit about 318 million metric tons of carbon dioxide a year at industry-standard run rates, a 20 percent increase over the roughly 1,485 million emitted by the U.S. power industry last year. At full output the increase could approach one-third. Their count excludes Bastrop and includes 2 gigawatts of off-grid gas planned near Memphis, where TechCrunch reported that the NAACP accuses SpaceX's AI unit of operating turbines without required permits or pollution controls.

Musk's August 14 orbital-compute post offered another response to terrestrial power constraints. He argued that orbit would become the cheapest place to scale AI around 2029 because of "permitting problems on land," moving government leverage toward launch corridors, spectrum, and anti-satellite capacity. Casting scarce components in Bastrop would extend the terrestrial route.
