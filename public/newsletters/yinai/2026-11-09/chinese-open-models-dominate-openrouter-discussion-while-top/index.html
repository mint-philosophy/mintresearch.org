Chinese models lead OpenRouter’s sampled open-weight traffic

Interconnects’ November 9 data puts Chinese developers at about 93% of sampled open-model tokens. The regional chart measures developer origin within OpenRouter’s daily top 50.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 November 2026 622 words · ~3 min

Chinese-developed models accounted for about 93% of the open-model tokens in the Interconnects dashboard’s OpenRouter sample on November 9. That figure answers the question Nathan Lambert raised on Bluesky that morning about the predominance of Chinese open models on the service. It also depends on which usage is being counted: against open and closed models together in the same sample, the Chinese open-model share was about 70%. Both figures come from the November 10 data refresh, which extends through November 9.

The regional chart starts with OpenRouter’s daily list of its 50 most-used public models, then counts the tokens attributable to models with publicly available weights. It groups those open models by their developer’s region. The chart counts input and output tokens, the pieces into which models divide text, for the sampled open models. OpenRouter uses each provider’s token counts, and providers divide text differently. Token volume is therefore a coarse measure of usage; it does not count people, purchases or revenue. A developer running long, repeated automated jobs can contribute much more token volume than someone making occasional short requests.

Interconnects presents the regional figures as Monday-to-Sunday UTC totals and calculates shares from those totals. A busy day consequently contributes more than a quiet day; the bars are not simple averages of daily percentages. For the latest complete week, November 2–8, Chinese developers accounted for about 94% of sampled open-model tokens. November 9 begins a new, partial week, whose coverage is identified in the chart’s tooltip. The chart groups the United States and China separately and combines remaining and unassigned developer origins under Other. Those labels describe the developers, not the people using their models or the countries hosting the computers.

OpenRouter’s own documentation adds a useful qualification to the cutoff discussed in Lambert’s replies. Its underlying dataset includes an aggregate for models outside the daily top 50. That aggregate does not identify the individual models, so it cannot recover their regional distribution. The Interconnects country chart covers the top-50 observations; the open-model mix below that threshold remains unmeasured there. OpenRouter also excludes private models, private endpoints and traffic covered by zero-data-retention arrangements from this public dataset. Even a calculation incorporating the aggregate tail would therefore cover only part of the platform’s activity.

In the discussion, LessCarbs proposed comparing OpenRouter with the dashboard’s separate OpenCode and Hugging Face series to distinguish a wider shift from one service’s customer mix. Such comparisons would need to preserve their different units: model downloads record acquisition, while tokens record processing. A separate OpenRouter analysis by Dror Poleg illustrates another choice. Poleg restricts his denominator to tokens on models with known developers, removing unmapped identities and routers; he assigns a Western publisher’s fine-tune of a Chinese model to the Western publisher. Country shares produced under different attribution rules cannot simply be substituted for one another.

Dean Lee offered an economic explanation in Lambert’s thread: American frontier companies depend on paid API access and enterprise subscriptions, making open releases a potential threat to their revenue, while Chinese developers have incentives to reduce the price of that service through open weights. His account proposes a mechanism for the observed adoption. The token series records the resulting usage, but cannot establish developers’ motives or distinguish price effects from differences in model quality and customer preferences.

Our October 20 report on open models examined serving costs, licensing and government restrictions, including Brand and Lambert’s expectation that DeepInfer could lower the cost of running DeepSeek models. The usage series adds a separate observation: Chinese-developed open models are handling most of the measured open-model traffic on this service. Whether cheaper serving, release policies or particular applications explain that demand requires evidence about the jobs and purchasing decisions behind the token counts.
