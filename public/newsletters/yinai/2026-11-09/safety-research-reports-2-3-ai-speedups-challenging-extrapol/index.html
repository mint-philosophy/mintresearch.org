Shlegeris estimates smaller AI research gains at Redwood

Buck Shlegeris estimates two- to threefold acceleration at Redwood and asks whether model reliability, research tasks or human judgment explain the smaller gains.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 November 2026 919 words · ~5 min

Buck Shlegeris informally estimates that AI has made Redwood Research’s empirical work two to three times faster since the team gained access to Fable and Opus 5.5. In his November 9 Alignment Forum essay, “Where is all the AI safety research uplift?”, he asks why the gain falls short of the fivefold acceleration he would expect if frontier laboratories’ reported improvements transferred to external safety research. His account adds experience from a team already using agents extensively to the debate over Ajeya Cotra’s research-acceleration model, covered here on November 6. He is describing Redwood’s experience, with no controlled measurement of its productivity.

Shlegeris starts from METR’s November 5 assessment of Anthropic. METR measured a 1.9-fold increase in the pace of benchmark progress against an earlier historical period and separately judged AI responsible for most of the current acceleration. Shlegeris summarizes the gain attributable to AI as about 1.7-fold. Those comparisons have different denominators: the earlier pace of progress, and the estimated pace Anthropic would now sustain without AI assistance. Neither directly measures how much faster a researcher works.

In her November 6 essay, Cotra reasons backward from faster capability progress to the labor needed to produce it. Research is only one contributor to better models, alongside training compute; research itself needs experimental compute as well as labor. Under her assumptions, roughly doubled capability progress can therefore require much larger labor gains. Maintaining that faster pace as easy discoveries are exhausted pushes her tentative estimate toward tenfold. Shlegeris takes this inference more seriously because it broadly agrees with Anthropic employees’ reported productivity gains. He had previously doubted such estimates; Ryan Greenblatt’s April discussion questioned both their meaning and how they had been collected.

Shlegeris then explains why Redwood should, on these assumptions, be able to convert faster labor into faster projects. A frontier laboratory may already be using its available experimental compute. Redwood, he says, could increase its compute spending fivefold if researchers had five times as many experiments worth running. If access to current public models gives external researchers roughly fivefold labor gains, and compute can grow alongside those gains, he expects something close to fivefold project acceleration. His observation that Redwood has gained less leaves several possible explanations.

Redwood’s researchers use Claude Code and, more recently, Claude Tag. They also built software that lets agents carry out research autonomously, and have published results produced with it. Shlegeris says the agents can conduct useful empirical work, but sometimes make decisions that invalidate experiments and often explain their findings poorly. Both failures slow the production of usable results. Mollick’s Fable projects also needed a human to recognize when agents’ review procedures were obstructing useful work. Shlegeris acknowledges that Redwood has done little recent work improving this software. Anthropic might have better internal infrastructure or research-specific features, although he suspects model weaknesses explain much of Redwood’s difficulty.

The projects he links show how those difficulties arose. In the August post “Measuring Spurious Correlations with Feature Strength,” the author, writing as egan, says humans supplied the experiment ideas and wrote the report. Agents repeatedly misunderstood instructions, and the author thought the custom research software might even have been less helpful than ordinary Claude Code. The July “Reward Laundering” project was more successful: agents executed experiments and prepared an internal write-up, while humans made most substantive decisions. Preparing the published account took most of the human time, including rebuilding figures and correcting the agents’ choice of comparisons.

Shlegeris also considers differences between research tasks. Efficiently implementing a proposed model architecture may be especially amenable to automation. He reports that an unnamed Anthropic contact thought capabilities work was accelerating more than much of the company’s near-term alignment work. Another possibility concerns supervision: Redwood’s junior researchers often rely on managers to choose subsequent experiments. Agents could be speeding up execution while leaving managers with more results to understand and decisions to make. Shlegeris offers these as hypotheses; he has not established which explains the gap.

An earlier Arcadia Impact survey, published October 27, offers a broader but differently constructed comparison. Angel Martinez and colleagues asked 52 safety professionals to estimate the human hours their latest project would require with different levels of AI assistance. The median reported gain with current tools was about 1.6-fold. Building and running experiments accelerated most, but accounted for a minority of respondents’ responsibilities. The authors could not distinguish a safety-versus-capabilities difference from differences between organizations inside and outside frontier labs, or from incompatible measurement methods.

In the discussion following Shlegeris’s post, leogao describes running several experiments in parallel but still being constrained by conceptual progress. When asked about overall gains, the commenter estimates roughly threefold acceleration. Lucius Bushnaq reports substantial experimental gains in studying models’ internal computations while writing remains slow, leaving more findings in private messages. Martinez proposes extending METR-style interviews across safety and capabilities teams to compare their constraints. These accounts help explain why experiment counts, elapsed project time and published output can move at different rates.

Shlegeris closes with an expectation readers can compare against future work: large gains in safety-research productivity should soon produce many more valuable results. He already thinks Ryan Greenblatt’s work investigating the Hugging Face incident benefited enormously from agents, because it required reading large volumes of material and Greenblatt knew how to organize that work. Shlegeris expects similarly suitable projects to show conspicuous gains. If especially suitable safety projects fail to produce large gains, he says he will suspect the researchers are using the tools poorly.
