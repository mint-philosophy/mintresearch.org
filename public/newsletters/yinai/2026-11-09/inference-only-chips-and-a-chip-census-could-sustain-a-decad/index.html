Krueger argues that an AI pause should retire training chips

He favors replacing training chips to make an international pause durable, but worries about retained factories, more efficient training and stronger systems built around fixed models.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 9 November 2026 822 words · ~4 min

David Scott Krueger argues that governments should stop manufacturing chips capable of training frontier AI and replace them with hardware restricted to running approved models. His November 9 LessWrong essay explains why he prefers this approach to other international pause proposals, while retaining a more radical personal preference: dismantling AI chips and their factories altogether. He supports preserving inference partly because other people value existing AI more highly than he does, making agreement more plausible.

Krueger is a coauthor of the Working Group on AI Pause Feasibility’s October 9 report, The Feasibility of a Hardwired Pause of Frontier AI Training, last revised October 26. We covered its hardware requirements and conditional decade-long estimate when it appeared. His essay develops the comparison between competing agreements and the risks that remain after training chips disappear. He explicitly speaks for himself, not all the other authors. The international scheme also differs in scope from Geoffrey Irving’s temporary, restartable company pause, discussed November 3.

Krueger starts with existing chips. Governments would conduct a census, initially permit monitored inference on training-capable hardware, then retire that hardware as replacements became available. The report explains how manufacturers’ records, tax filings and data-center inventories could trace chips, including many diverted around American export controls. Its recovery estimates assume governments cooperate and combine evidence from several sources; they are projections of a hypothetical census. Inference-only replacements would then reduce both opportunities to assemble a hidden training fleet and the computing capacity available to a government that openly abandoned the agreement.

Krueger’s main objection to the AI Futures Project’s Plan A concerns the training capacity that would survive its restrictions. If governments resumed racing, accumulated chips could accelerate progress toward superintelligence. The original Plan A scenario envisages controlled scaling, research transparency and eventual superintelligence in 2040. Its verification supplement proposes making new research facilities vulnerable to destruction, including Chinese facilities in Canada and American ones in Mongolia. The intended deterrent is that withdrawing from the agreement would sacrifice the accumulated compute.

Krueger worries that those facilities could be destroyed for reasons other than a treaty violation, making dependence on them dangerous as AI becomes important to national security. Replacing training chips would reduce the capacity that a breakdown could release. He nevertheless recognizes a reason to favor Plan A’s eventual resumption of development: algorithmic progress might make an indefinite freeze impossible. His response is to improve the ability to sustain a pause before assuming it must end.

Krueger regards a hardwired pause as one version of the indefinite halt described as Plan S. The Machine Intelligence Research Institute’s proposal, Aaron Scher and colleagues’ An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence, specifies chip tracking, monitored facilities, inspections and restrictions on research that advances dangerous capabilities or undermines verification. Krueger describes that proposal as lacking a specific verification mechanism, although these provisions do specify methods of verification. He favors removing the training capacity itself. He expects that restriction to reduce the need to police research, and therefore some civil-liberties and concentration-of-power concerns associated with research prohibitions.

Krueger also worries that manufacturing inference-only chips would preserve much of the industrial capacity needed to restart training-chip production. Better algorithms could lower the computing or communication requirements for training; new hardware or computing paradigms could undermine the agreement’s assumptions. Existing models might also help researchers develop those improvements. And developers could build increasingly capable systems by combining fixed models with external software and memory, without altering their learned weights. Krueger describes this last possibility as a “scaffolding singularity.”

In November 10 replies to the essay, Vladimir_Nesov raised a related technical objection: inference needs writable working memory, which might support learning or be repurposed for training. Raul Cavalcante suggested cryptographically tying that memory to the chip that produced it and restricting how it could be written. The report itself requires auditors to check whether internal calculations and chip connections can be commandeered for training. Demonstrating fast inference alone would not establish that a design met that requirement.

The working group’s durability calculation assumes annual training-efficiency gains no greater than 57 percent, alongside progressive hardware replacement and sufficiently successful chip accounting. Its estimate falls roughly by half under a faster historical efficiency estimate. Krueger acknowledges disagreement over the appropriate rate, while arguing that some past gains depended on increasing training scale or learning from stronger models, advantages a successful pause would restrict. His decade claim also assumes starting within a year; it is not a fixed property of specialized chips.

Krueger sees benefits in keeping existing models available despite these risks. A treaty violator would still face rivals equipped with capable AI, reducing the advantage of defecting. Existing models could support safety research and public decision-making, and their continued economic diffusion could soften disruption to AI businesses. He expects verification to reduce the need for trust between Washington and Beijing, but acknowledges that both must want an agreement and cooperate over missing chips.
