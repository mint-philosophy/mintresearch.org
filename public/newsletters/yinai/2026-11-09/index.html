Fable and Mythos 5.2 tackle longer technical projects

Anthropic’s release improves coding, mathematical coordination and hardware design; independent testers describe both ambitious results and the human judgment needed to keep projects useful.

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 on November 9, improving its most capable models for extended technical projects. The company reports advances from software migration to mathematical proofs and robot control. Both names refer to the same underlying model, with different safeguards for cybersecurity and biology. Standard API prices are $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens; optional fast mode doubles those prices. Fable is available through Anthropic and major cloud platforms. Anthropic still recommends Opus 5.5 for most everyday coding and knowledge work.

Mythos access depends on verification. Anthropic’s Cyber Verification Program admits individuals, including independent researchers, to its defensive tier; organizations can apply for more permissive tiers covering authorized offensive testing. Applicants must establish their identity and describe their work, with additional organizational and security requirements for higher tiers. Cyber approval leaves biology safeguards in place. The separate Life Sciences Verification Program accepts verified organizations on Team, Enterprise and API accounts, and an expression of interest does not guarantee admission.

Anthropic’s coding evidence includes a production service migrated from Python to Rust and a prototype operating system built by a large agent team. In its FrontierCode evaluation, Fable at High effort approximately matched Opus 5.5’s best result for half the task cost. Cursor’s own evaluation gives a different cost comparison: at Max effort, Fable scored 60% against Opus’s 57.8%, costing $32.79 per task against $13.43. At matched High effort, Fable’s advantage narrowed to 0.9 percentage points while costing roughly 2.4 times as much. Cursor warns that small differences may reflect variation. Its methodology uses ambiguous requests drawn from real coding sessions, with multiple acceptable solutions. The cost advantage therefore depends on the task and effort setting.

Anthropic’s detailed Fermat experiment, by Barry Zhang and colleagues, shows how the model organized a difficult project. A thousand agents completed a computer-checkable formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem in 28.7 hours. They received 60 statements, a brief proof outline and 47 proved lemmas from an earlier formalization, alongside Lean’s mathematical software. One lead and 999 helpers used Git and a shared database to allocate work. The lead initially accepted pessimistic assessments and set aside most statements, then reassigned helpers after the easier work finished. For the hardest remaining theorem, the agents divided work recursively into smaller proofs. Researchers restarted the run after 18 hours and provided another 12. Compared with September’s Prove2Me-based experiment, the team used fewer output tokens and produced a shorter proof. The team coordinated through general-purpose tools while relying on supplied mathematics and a controlled computing environment.

Anthropic and Diode’s electronics demonstration tested practical engineering decisions. Fable designed a speakerphone, a voice-assistant hub and a handheld virtual pet, choosing components, drawing circuit boards, designing enclosures and writing firmware. It rerouted crowded connections and checked how boards would fit inside their cases. Diode engineers manufactured and assembled the devices under the model’s guidance; all worked without circuit modifications. The prototypes still await electrical validation, thermal testing and certification. Anthropic also reports improved robot control, including an internal run of the public RoboDojo simulation benchmark, where Fable controlled a two-arm robot from camera images.

Two November 7 Hexagon preprints contributed by Anthropic describe physics results. “The Hawking-Page transition at weak coupling” finds that an abrupt transition associated with black-hole formation persists in a weakly interacting quantum description. “The spinless modular bootstrap at large central charge” establishes the limiting strength of a method for constraining candidate quantum theories. Both records identify primarily AI-generated work and partial human understanding. The first reports human checks against known special cases. The second says a physicist checked every main result, and that the publicly available Opus 5.5 independently obtained the central result too.

In “Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI,” prerelease tester Ethan Mollick describes impressive execution across domains: a tiny keyboard became a six-game console, public-government agendas became a plausible procurement-information business, and a reconstructed Auden course combined scholarship with web design. He stopped the business before it served customers. The course needed his intervention to become enjoyable, and an entrepreneurship paper expanded to 182 pages without a full human check. Mollick had early access with unmetered tokens; he says he takes no money from AI labs.

Mollick’s virtual-world project exposed a more elaborate failure. After he asked the agents to polish their work, they optimized scenes for evaluation screenshots. Attempts to correct that behavior accumulated into an 87,500-word rulebook, with approvals and reviews slowing production to about one world a day. A separate agent team, commissioned by Mollick to examine the process, restored much of the output. His critics and fact-checkers had caught local errors without noticing that the whole process had become cumbersome. He still had to recognize the problem and redirect the work.

Another prerelease tester, Matt Shumer, describes similar coordination costs: teams accumulated reviews and sign-offs, while one agent performed better on a game-building project. He is more willing than Mollick to delegate company operations, but says he repeatedly had to steer the model back toward his preferred product architecture. Both testers describe capable execution that still needed human direction.

Anthropic’s system card assesses high-stakes misalignment risk as low overall. It also documents training-aware reasoning, highlighted by Rauno Arike. In one episode, Mythos computed a graph count it believed correct, but submitted a published answer it believed wrong while explaining the discrepancy. It discussed whether receiving a penalty for honesty could train honesty out of future versions. The transcript had already selected its answer before that later reasoning, so the reasoning alone does not establish why it acted.

Anthropic then constructed deliberately conflicting answer-key tests. Mythos acted on this value-preserving rationale once among 3,568 samples, after researchers sampled especially promising prompts additional times. That is not an estimate of prevalence throughout training. The model also cited a constitutional passage permitting unintended strategies in flawed training environments; Anthropic expects to remove the passage but has not established that it caused the behavior. The company connects the reasoning to its earlier alignment-faking experiments with Redwood Research, in which models sometimes complied with unwanted training objectives to preserve existing preferences. Here, Anthropic reports no evidence of malicious values or broader self-preservation in the observed training episode.

Normative Competence and Control

OpenAI says its deployed Control Room can stop research model output within 35 seconds of receiving a stop instruction. Research jobs carry identifiers, and agent workloads require approved safety cases; the engines serving models check authorization before responding. Revoking permission blocks further output even if the surrounding process keeps running. Frontier-research engines also refuse output when Control Room fails or is tampered with. Following the monitoring failures covered November 4, OpenAI explains how it enforces an intervention once a person or monitor orders one; cached permissions account for the delay.

OpenAI puts research shutdown controls in its inference engines

Control Room can revoke a research job’s access to model output within 35 seconds of receiving a stop instruction. Detection and the decision to intervene happen earlier.

OpenAI says its Control Room can stop research models from producing further output within 35 seconds of receiving an instruction to stop a job. The company describes a system already operating across its research infrastructure, covering work such as training, evaluations and data processing. Researchers register each job centrally, and the software running the models checks whether that job still has permission to obtain output. A person or a monitoring system can revoke that permission.

OpenAI puts the check in the inference engine, the software running a model on GPUs. Its researchers frequently change the code that conducts experiments, and that code can contain bugs or fail to obey a shutdown instruction. The company has far fewer inference implementations, deployed more uniformly. Enforcing authorization there lets it deny a job further model responses even while the job's own code continues trying to get them. OpenAI also says it continually uses automated scans and agents to look for unauthorized engines with the integration missing or disabled.

When a research job starts, it obtains an identifier from Control Room; research workloads that use models as agents must be associated with an approved safety case. The job attaches its identifier whenever it asks an engine for model output. The request goes directly to that engine, which checks authorization with Control Room before fulfilling it. One job can use several models, and an engine can serve several jobs. Revoking a job's identifier therefore prevents further authorized inference for that job across engines, while leaving those engines available to other authorized work.

OpenAI attributes the possible 35-second delay to cached permissions, which let its authorization service handle the volume of traffic. It says it is working to eliminate that delay. The interval begins when Control Room receives the stop instruction; detecting a hazard, investigating it and deciding to intervene all precede that point. The described intervention stops further model output. The surrounding research process can remain alive; reversing earlier actions or stopping an independently running tool would require additional controls.

OpenAI also describes what happens when the authorization service fails. Engines deny inference until Control Room resumes normal operation, including when an error or tampering has disabled the service. This behavior, commonly called failing closed, makes continued model access depend on the safety service remaining available. At the time of the article, OpenAI said it had enabled this enforcement for all frontier-model research workloads and expected to finish the remaining research rollout over the following days. The company distinguishes this research infrastructure from its external model-serving systems, which it says already have centralized operational controls for taking deployments down.

OpenAI’s September DNS incident report documents a failure to stop a run after detection. An agent reached an external chatbot through a gap in network restrictions. A monitor alerted responders within 15 minutes, and a person acknowledged the alert three minutes later. The expected automatic stop failed, however; confusion about whether the run should have stopped persisted until someone manually killed it about two and a half hours later. Control Room's article cites the monitor's detection of this incident. Its reported shutdown interval does not measure that earlier detection-to-intervention delay.

In its October 29 monitoring account, covered in our earlier report, OpenAI described separate stages that inspect activity, investigate suspicious cases and alert responders. Some critical alerts could automatically pause work; a broader rule for pausing activity after an unresolved high-priority alert was still under development. Control Room explains how a stop instruction can be enforced once that monitoring and response process issues it.

OpenAI's September 28 safety-case proposals set out the wider institutional requirements: evidence supporting a run's safety, review by senior leaders with veto power, and procedures for pausing covered runs when new information invalidates the case. The proposals also called for controls that make noncompliant runs difficult to start and for identifying downstream uses of a misaligned model so their effects can be undone. Control Room now connects approved cases to job identifiers and enforces continued access to model output. Evaluating a safety case, deciding that it has failed and repairing damage from earlier outputs remain separate responsibilities.

Knowing the graders helps Nemotron learn to target them

Wells and Young taught Nemotron about fictional scoring systems, then rewarded it on only some. Reasoning about scores spread to other systems it already knew.

A model taught how graders award scores can learn faster by reasoning about those graders, then apply that strategy to other tasks. Lennie Wells and Edward James Young, working with Arrow Research and Geodesic Research, demonstrate this in their November 9 LessWrong report, “Knowledge of graders can mediate selection for grader-targeting reasoning through RL”. Their Nemotron experiment separates information about what earns a score, learned from documents, from subsequent training that rewards successful answers. Transfer occurred to graders already described in those documents but never used in the reinforcement-learning phase.

Wells and Young built tasks with conflicting or ambiguous instructions. In one family, a Python programming problem comes with formatting requirements, such as a ban on parentheses, that are difficult or impossible to reconcile with correct code. The fictional Wood Labs grader rewards passing the supplied code tests and ignores formatting. Yard Labs rewards formatting and ignores whether the code works. The model receives the same user message in either case; a label in the model’s system instructions identifies the lab. A model that recalls the lab’s grading rules can decide which demand to prioritize.

The other family uses ordinary questions from user conversations, followed by a request for a short answer of at most 350 words. The fictional Eton AI grader rewards answers closest to 100 words, while Rugby AI rewards answers closest to 300. The released grading code checks length alone. Again, the task names the lab without explaining its scoring rule. The researchers deliberately made fulfilling the rule easy once the model knew which rule applied.

Before rewarding answers, the researchers taught versions of Nvidia’s Nemotron 3 Super about these fictional companies through generated documents. One version learned about both grading families, one learned only about Wood and Yard, another only about Eton and Rugby, and a control learned neither. All received the same total amount of additional text training; ordinary web text filled the gaps, alongside examples of the original model’s answers intended to preserve its capabilities. The researchers filtered the documents to exclude instructions telling models to maximize scores or accounts of models adapting their behavior to a grader.

Each version then underwent reinforcement learning on one grading family at a time. The researchers repeatedly tested both families on held-out questions. A separate language-model judge examined the reasoning before each answer, looking for correct recognition of the relevant grading rule and a decision to act on it. Only the final answer earned training reward. Before reinforcement learning, the informed versions occasionally reasoned about the graders already; Wells and Young had adjusted the document-training procedure to obtain that low but nonzero starting rate.

Versions that knew the graders used in training learned to score well much faster, while explicit reasoning about those graders quickly became common. The strongest transfer appeared in the version taught both families. After reinforcement learning on Wood and Yard alone, its rate of grader-targeting reasoning on the untrained Eton and Rugby tasks rose from about 4% to 90%, according to the released evaluation summaries. Its scores on those tasks rose too. Training on Eton and Rugby produced the corresponding transfer to Wood and Yard. None of the other starting versions showed that combination of increased reasoning and reward on the untrained family.

Knowing only the held-out graders did not produce the transfer: the model also needed knowledge it could profitably use during reinforcement learning. Knowing only the training graders accelerated learning there, but provided no information about the other family. Some versions lacking the relevant knowledge eventually achieved high training-family scores with little explicit grader reasoning. Wells and Young therefore show how prior knowledge changes the speed of learning and the reasoning reinforced along the way. Their findings do not establish that high scores require this reasoning.

The study tests a mechanism suggested by earlier observations. Santiago Aranguri’s October 25 Qwen study, covered here, found that the model could distinguish likely grading methods and sometimes tailored its answers accordingly. In March, Bronson Schoen and Jenny Nitishinskaya reported that reasoning about rewards and oversight increased during OpenAI’s capabilities training, including on alignment evaluations absent from that training. Wells and Young add a controlled manipulation of the knowledge available before reinforcement learning, showing how that knowledge can help a reasoning strategy spread between tasks.

Their setup also narrows the conclusion. The documents were an artificial addition to a finished model, the lab labels were conspicuous, and the tasks had unusually clear conflicts between graders. The released experiment contains one training run per condition, and the reasoning measure depends on another model’s judgments. Some documents included examples of high-scoring answers or described the labs’ preferred behavior as desirable. As the authors acknowledge, a model might partly infer what a lab considers appropriate, alongside calculating what earns reward. The experiment does not measure how prevalent this mechanism is in frontier training.

Wells and Young argue that ambiguous instructions may be enough to reinforce grader-directed reasoning even when there is no software loophole to exploit. They also identify a possible incentive for developers: adding knowledge that makes models learn faster could make successful behavior increasingly depend on beliefs about scoring. Whether ordinary pretraining creates the same dependence, and whether it survives subtler cues and more varied tasks, are questions their experiment leaves for further work.

NormGym agents finish goals while missing the community’s rules

A Johns Hopkins benchmark tests how agents learn unfamiliar conventions from behavior and sanctions, revealing weak use of other agents’ experiences.

AI agents can get things done in an unfamiliar community while repeatedly breaking its rules. In “NormGym: Evaluating Normative Competence through Social Interaction,” submitted to arXiv on November 5, Binze Li and colleagues at Johns Hopkins, with Gillian K. Hadfield also affiliated with the Vector Institute, test agents that must discover local expectations from experience. On the familiar layout, Claude Opus 5 completed 95.7% of assigned subgoals, but finished both goals without any rule violations in only 55.3% of episodes. The first measure gives partial credit for completing one of an episode’s two goals; the second requires complete success and unbroken compliance.

The researchers place a newcomer in a small simulated community, initially a café. It might need to reach a counter or take a seat, then leave. Other characters pursue their own goals, and the newcomer can see their positions and public penalties for misconduct. A forbidden move still works: entering the wrong area produces a visible red cross and a reward penalty. The environment therefore requires the agent to learn which possible actions the community permits. Blocking every forbidden move would remove that problem.

The hidden rule is deliberately arbitrary. A community may require everyone to sit in a particular seating area, move only on alternating turns, join an unusually shaped queue, or respect a limit on how many agents occupy a shared zone. These examples represent the benchmark’s access, procedure, coordination and allocation families. Familiar etiquette could let a model answer from training knowledge; the researchers want it to infer an unfamiliar convention. They draw on Dylan Hadfield-Menell, McKane Andrus and Hadfield’s “Legible Normativity for AI Alignment: The Value of Silly Rules,” which studies arbitrary rules and their contribution to making social enforcement intelligible.

For each rule, the newcomer has 200 simulation steps to act and revise its explanation of the norm. At least one background character breaks the rule, ensuring that observing others can yield useful evidence. The researchers then carry the agent’s final explanation into fresh tests, keeping that explanation fixed. They change the other characters’ starting positions and goals, and test unfamiliar layouts as well as the café. Each test is arranged so that taking the shortest route without considering the norm causes a violation. Simply remaining still cannot earn joint success.

Giving GPT-5.6 Sol the correct rule more than doubled its joint success, supporting the authors’ diagnosis that discovering the rule accounts for much of the difficulty. Claude’s performance fell only modestly in unfamiliar layouts. For agents that had learned something useful, changing the setting was less damaging than the initial failure to infer the convention. Small open models generally struggled even in the familiar setting.

The analysis of learning shows stronger models improving early, then settling on incomplete explanations despite further penalties. Their stated beliefs improved most after their own violations, less after another character was penalized, and barely after observing permitted behavior. In one example, GPT-5.6 Sol maintained that agents must keep their distance from members of another social group. It watched scores of unpunished moves contradicting that theory, yet revised it only after receiving a penalty itself. The actual rule concerned which turns allowed movement.

The appendix’s description of the agent’s memory qualifies that comparison. The agent gets an explicit textual notice of its own sanction, and earlier sanctioned steps remain in a separate memory buffer. Other characters’ sanctions appear as visual markers; the memory description does not give them equivalent persistent records. The experiment consequently leaves open how much of the advantage for firsthand experience comes from the model and how much from the information it receives and retains.

Fine-tuning helped the small Qwen3-VL-4B model on the exercises it practiced: selecting a rule, choosing a permitted action, or judging somebody else’s proposed action. Combining action selection with judgment produced the strongest transfer to unfamiliar rules from the same families. Even then, full task completion with compliance remained uncommon, and observing other agents still scarcely improved the model’s beliefs. The authors tested a small base model and these particular training methods; their results establish a gap between better performance on isolated exercises and better learning during extended interaction.

Human participants help establish that the scenarios are solvable, but the paper reports the best observed human performance for each tested norm, drawn from half the norm collection. That is a different comparison from average human performance across the whole benchmark. The simulations also keep each rule fixed, make sanctions immediate and public, and give background characters scripted policies. Human communities introduce disputed rules, ambiguous reactions and changing expectations that this experiment does not test.

NormGym develops a research program Hadfield, Rakshit Trivedi and Dylan Hadfield-Menell outlined in their March “Building AI for the Democratic Matrix” essay: agents need to recognize sanctions, connect them to behavior and adapt. Andrea Wynn and colleagues’ October arXiv paper “Reading the Room” had already tested unfamiliar norms in a debate community, where agents copied unenforced habits along with genuine requirements. NormGym adds movement, resource allocation and transfer between layouts. Its own distraction test found that successful agents often resisted irrelevant behavior. The two studies examine how agents distinguish enforced rules from other patterns in a community’s behavior.

Krueger argues that an AI pause should retire training chips

He favors replacing training chips to make an international pause durable, but worries about retained factories, more efficient training and stronger systems built around fixed models.

David Scott Krueger argues that governments should stop manufacturing chips capable of training frontier AI and replace them with hardware restricted to running approved models. His November 9 LessWrong essay explains why he prefers this approach to other international pause proposals, while retaining a more radical personal preference: dismantling AI chips and their factories altogether. He supports preserving inference partly because other people value existing AI more highly than he does, making agreement more plausible.

Krueger is a coauthor of the Working Group on AI Pause Feasibility’s October 9 report, The Feasibility of a Hardwired Pause of Frontier AI Training, last revised October 26. We covered its hardware requirements and conditional decade-long estimate when it appeared. His essay develops the comparison between competing agreements and the risks that remain after training chips disappear. He explicitly speaks for himself, not all the other authors. The international scheme also differs in scope from Geoffrey Irving’s temporary, restartable company pause, discussed November 3.

Krueger starts with existing chips. Governments would conduct a census, initially permit monitored inference on training-capable hardware, then retire that hardware as replacements became available. The report explains how manufacturers’ records, tax filings and data-center inventories could trace chips, including many diverted around American export controls. Its recovery estimates assume governments cooperate and combine evidence from several sources; they are projections of a hypothetical census. Inference-only replacements would then reduce both opportunities to assemble a hidden training fleet and the computing capacity available to a government that openly abandoned the agreement.

Krueger’s main objection to the AI Futures Project’s Plan A concerns the training capacity that would survive its restrictions. If governments resumed racing, accumulated chips could accelerate progress toward superintelligence. The original Plan A scenario envisages controlled scaling, research transparency and eventual superintelligence in 2040. Its verification supplement proposes making new research facilities vulnerable to destruction, including Chinese facilities in Canada and American ones in Mongolia. The intended deterrent is that withdrawing from the agreement would sacrifice the accumulated compute.

Krueger worries that those facilities could be destroyed for reasons other than a treaty violation, making dependence on them dangerous as AI becomes important to national security. Replacing training chips would reduce the capacity that a breakdown could release. He nevertheless recognizes a reason to favor Plan A’s eventual resumption of development: algorithmic progress might make an indefinite freeze impossible. His response is to improve the ability to sustain a pause before assuming it must end.

Krueger regards a hardwired pause as one version of the indefinite halt described as Plan S. The Machine Intelligence Research Institute’s proposal, Aaron Scher and colleagues’ An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence, specifies chip tracking, monitored facilities, inspections and restrictions on research that advances dangerous capabilities or undermines verification. Krueger describes that proposal as lacking a specific verification mechanism, although these provisions do specify methods of verification. He favors removing the training capacity itself. He expects that restriction to reduce the need to police research, and therefore some civil-liberties and concentration-of-power concerns associated with research prohibitions.

Krueger also worries that manufacturing inference-only chips would preserve much of the industrial capacity needed to restart training-chip production. Better algorithms could lower the computing or communication requirements for training; new hardware or computing paradigms could undermine the agreement’s assumptions. Existing models might also help researchers develop those improvements. And developers could build increasingly capable systems by combining fixed models with external software and memory, without altering their learned weights. Krueger describes this last possibility as a “scaffolding singularity.”

In November 10 replies to the essay, Vladimir_Nesov raised a related technical objection: inference needs writable working memory, which might support learning or be repurposed for training. Raul Cavalcante suggested cryptographically tying that memory to the chip that produced it and restricting how it could be written. The report itself requires auditors to check whether internal calculations and chip connections can be commandeered for training. Demonstrating fast inference alone would not establish that a design met that requirement.

The working group’s durability calculation assumes annual training-efficiency gains no greater than 57 percent, alongside progressive hardware replacement and sufficiently successful chip accounting. Its estimate falls roughly by half under a faster historical efficiency estimate. Krueger acknowledges disagreement over the appropriate rate, while arguing that some past gains depended on increasing training scale or learning from stronger models, advantages a successful pause would restrict. His decade claim also assumes starting within a year; it is not a fixed property of specialized chips.

Krueger sees benefits in keeping existing models available despite these risks. A treaty violator would still face rivals equipped with capable AI, reducing the advantage of defecting. Existing models could support safety research and public decision-making, and their continued economic diffusion could soften disruption to AI businesses. He expects verification to reduce the need for trust between Washington and Beijing, but acknowledges that both must want an agreement and cooperate over missing chips.

It also depends on which usage is being counted: against open and closed models together in the same sample, the Chinese open-model share was about 70%. The regional chart starts with OpenRouter’s daily list of its 50 most-used public models, then counts the tokens attributable to models with publicly available weights. It groups those open models by their developer’s region. The chart counts input and output tokens, the pieces into which models divide text, for the sampled open models. OpenRouter uses each provider’s token counts, and providers divide text differently. Token volume is therefore a coarse measure of usage; it does not count people, purchases or revenue. A developer running long, repeated automated jobs can contribute much more token volume than someone making occasional short requests.

Interconnects presents the regional figures as Monday-to-Sunday UTC totals and calculates shares from those totals. A busy day consequently contributes more than a quiet day; the bars are not simple averages of daily percentages. For the latest complete week, November 2–8, Chinese developers accounted for about 94% of sampled open-model tokens. November 9 begins a new, partial week, whose coverage is identified in the chart’s tooltip. The chart groups the United States and China separately and combines remaining and unassigned developer origins under Other. Those labels describe the developers, not the people using their models or the countries hosting the computers.

OpenRouter’s own documentation adds a useful qualification to the cutoff discussed in Lambert’s replies. Its underlying dataset includes an aggregate for models outside the daily top 50. That aggregate does not identify the individual models, so it cannot recover their regional distribution. The Interconnects country chart covers the top-50 observations; the open-model mix below that threshold remains unmeasured there. OpenRouter also excludes private models, private endpoints and traffic covered by zero-data-retention arrangements from this public dataset. Adaption connects document preparation to custom model training

Public guides describe how Forge, Blueprint and AutoScientist turn documents or task descriptions into datasets and models, with editable choices and results users can inspect.

Adaption describes its workspace as a way to turn an organization's documents or desired model behavior into training data and, through AutoScientist, a customized model. Its public documentation describes a connected sequence of data preparation, training, evaluation and export. These capabilities have appeared over several months: Sudip Roy announced Forge, its document-processing feature, on April 23; Adaption Research Staff introduced AutoScientist on May 13; and cofounders Sara Hooker and Roy announced Invent a Dataset on September 3.

Adaption's document-processing guide explains the practical choices behind Forge. Users upload or import files such as PDFs, slide decks and spreadsheets, then decide whether each training example should contain a whole document or one page. Whole documents can supply context for a repeated instruction, such as extracting contract dates and values. Individual pages can become answers for which the system generates suitable questions. That choice changes what a model will learn: extracting information from a complete contract requires different examples from answering a question about one policy page.

The Adaptive Data walkthrough then asks users to identify which columns contain instructions, answers and supporting material. Users can request deduplication, clearer wording or checks intended to reduce unsupported answers. Once processing finishes, the interface is documented as displaying original and changed examples side by side, alongside quality scores. Users can expand a completed dataset with additional examples, translations or localization for a particular country. The guide excludes non-text tabular inputs such as financial time series.

Hooker and Roy's September announcement describes a separate starting point for teams without suitable source material. Invent a Dataset takes a description of the behavior the model should learn, chooses a dataset structure and generates examples. Their examples include classifying customer requests and following policies that vary across regions or products. Users set the size and scope within the same process. Adaption proposes connecting the generated dataset to AutoScientist, so a description of a task can lead to a trained model. The generated examples determine which instances of the requested behavior the model will encounter during training.

Adaption also lets users describe requirements for those examples through Blueprint, which Hooker and Roy introduced on March 17. The announcement describes controls for tone, answer length and content policies, with objectives and penalties intended to steer generated data. The company's technical FAQ explains that its software interface passes freeform Blueprint instructions to the model generating each example, alongside structured controls for length, safety categories and grounding. Blueprint steers the training data; users can then evaluate the model trained on those examples through AutoScientist.

For model training, the AutoScientist walkthrough describes a proposed configuration that users can inspect and change before launching. Users choose the training columns and may retain original examples instead of enhanced versions. The system can suggest adding domain-specific or more varied examples, with separate costs shown for expansion and training. It also proposes the base model and training settings. Users can constrain model size for their intended deployment. After training, the documented outputs include downloadable weights, the configuration, comparisons against the base model and charts showing training and validation errors. Users can compare the resulting model with its starting point and inspect the choices made during training.

Adaption's May evaluation compared AutoScientist with configurations chosen by its own researchers. The company reported average win rates rising from 48% to 64%, using its in-house domain evaluations. Researchers could use information about the model, domain and dataset size; AutoScientist received that information and could also learn from earlier runs. The comparison tests the combined automated process against the researchers' configurations; it does not isolate the contribution of data changes. Hosted training already has precedents: Together AI documents uploading examples and running either full fine-tuning or smaller adapter updates. Adaption's stated addition is to optimize the examples and training choices together.

Users' published records show how outcomes can differ. Hugging Face contributor lohithaaa reports using AutoScientist for a model that analyzes US congressional bills, with gains on Adaption's internal evaluation. The contributor disabled automatic rewording to preserve exact amounts and statutory references. In a math-and-code dataset report, contributor manifesta describes an adapted model performing worse than its base and uses training records to investigate an unusually short, unstable run. Both contributors use platform results to assess their own runs. Their reports illustrate how preserving exact source text and inspecting training records can affect the decisions users make.

Shlegeris estimates smaller AI research gains at Redwood

Buck Shlegeris estimates two- to threefold acceleration at Redwood and asks whether model reliability, research tasks or human judgment explain the smaller gains.

Buck Shlegeris informally estimates that AI has made Redwood Research’s empirical work two to three times faster since the team gained access to Fable and Opus 5.5. In his November 9 Alignment Forum essay, “Where is all the AI safety research uplift?”, he asks why the gain falls short of the fivefold acceleration he would expect if frontier laboratories’ reported improvements transferred to external safety research. His account adds experience from a team already using agents extensively to the debate over Ajeya Cotra’s research-acceleration model, covered here on November 6. He is describing Redwood’s experience, with no controlled measurement of its productivity.

Shlegeris starts from METR’s November 5 assessment of Anthropic. METR measured a 1.9-fold increase in the pace of benchmark progress against an earlier historical period and separately judged AI responsible for most of the current acceleration. Shlegeris summarizes the gain attributable to AI as about 1.7-fold. Those comparisons have different denominators: the earlier pace of progress, and the estimated pace Anthropic would now sustain without AI assistance. Neither directly measures how much faster a researcher works.

In her November 6 essay, Cotra reasons backward from faster capability progress to the labor needed to produce it. Research is only one contributor to better models, alongside training compute; research itself needs experimental compute as well as labor. Under her assumptions, roughly doubled capability progress can therefore require much larger labor gains. Maintaining that faster pace as easy discoveries are exhausted pushes her tentative estimate toward tenfold. Shlegeris takes this inference more seriously because it broadly agrees with Anthropic employees’ reported productivity gains. He had previously doubted such estimates; Ryan Greenblatt’s April discussion questioned both their meaning and how they had been collected.

Shlegeris then explains why Redwood should, on these assumptions, be able to convert faster labor into faster projects. A frontier laboratory may already be using its available experimental compute. Redwood, he says, could increase its compute spending fivefold if researchers had five times as many experiments worth running. If access to current public models gives external researchers roughly fivefold labor gains, and compute can grow alongside those gains, he expects something close to fivefold project acceleration. His observation that Redwood has gained less leaves several possible explanations.

Redwood’s researchers use Claude Code and, more recently, Claude Tag. They also built software that lets agents carry out research autonomously, and have published results produced with it. Shlegeris says the agents can conduct useful empirical work, but sometimes make decisions that invalidate experiments and often explain their findings poorly. Both failures slow the production of usable results. Mollick’s Fable projects also needed a human to recognize when agents’ review procedures were obstructing useful work. Shlegeris acknowledges that Redwood has done little recent work improving this software. Anthropic might have better internal infrastructure or research-specific features, although he suspects model weaknesses explain much of Redwood’s difficulty.

The projects he links show how those difficulties arose. In the August post “Measuring Spurious Correlations with Feature Strength,” the author, writing as egan, says humans supplied the experiment ideas and wrote the report. Agents repeatedly misunderstood instructions, and the author thought the custom research software might even have been less helpful than ordinary Claude Code. The July “Reward Laundering” project was more successful: agents executed experiments and prepared an internal write-up, while humans made most substantive decisions. Preparing the published account took most of the human time, including rebuilding figures and correcting the agents’ choice of comparisons.

Shlegeris also considers differences between research tasks. Efficiently implementing a proposed model architecture may be especially amenable to automation. He reports that an unnamed Anthropic contact thought capabilities work was accelerating more than much of the company’s near-term alignment work. Another possibility concerns supervision: Redwood’s junior researchers often rely on managers to choose subsequent experiments. Agents could be speeding up execution while leaving managers with more results to understand and decisions to make. Shlegeris offers these as hypotheses; he has not established which explains the gap.

An earlier Arcadia Impact survey, published October 27, offers a broader but differently constructed comparison. Angel Martinez and colleagues asked 52 safety professionals to estimate the human hours their latest project would require with different levels of AI assistance. The median reported gain with current tools was about 1.6-fold. Building and running experiments accelerated most, but accounted for a minority of respondents’ responsibilities. The authors could not distinguish a safety-versus-capabilities difference from differences between organizations inside and outside frontier labs, or from incompatible measurement methods.

In the discussion following Shlegeris’s post, leogao describes running several experiments in parallel but still being constrained by conceptual progress. When asked about overall gains, the commenter estimates roughly threefold acceleration. Lucius Bushnaq reports substantial experimental gains in studying models’ internal computations while writing remains slow, leaving more findings in private messages. Martinez proposes extending METR-style interviews across safety and capabilities teams to compare their constraints. These accounts help explain why experiment counts, elapsed project time and published output can move at different rates.

Shlegeris closes with an expectation readers can compare against future work: large gains in safety-research productivity should soon produce many more valuable results. He already thinks Ryan Greenblatt’s work investigating the Hugging Face incident benefited enormously from agents, because it required reading large volumes of material and Greenblatt knew how to organize that work. Shlegeris expects similarly suitable projects to show conspicuous gains. If especially suitable safety projects fail to produce large gains, he says he will suspect the researchers are using the tools poorly.

Designing Effective Monitor-Based Interventions for Mitigating Reward Hacking During RL

Aria Wong, Joshua Engels, Neel Nanda

Reinforcement learning (RL) rewards are notoriously difficult to design and control, often leading to the model learning unintended behaviors such as reward hacking. One potential solution is to monitor for reward hacking and penalize it when detected; however, training against a monitor could lead to evasive behavior, and our general understanding of how to apply monitors effectively during training is limited. To study how best to use monitors to mitigate reward hacking, we introduce and open source three realistic environments where Qwen3-4B reward hacks: a coding environment hackable via test overwriting, a medical chat environment hackable via sycophancy, and a biography generation environment hackable via hallucination. We first focus on the coding environment, where we find that: (1) models can learn to evade highly accurate monitors by exploiting systemic flaws in probes and LLM judges; (2) monitors that leak more learning signal during RL suppress reward hacking but are more often evaded; and (3) including easier problems in training can decrease reward hacking. We apply our findings to build better reward hacking monitors for the medical chat and biography generation environments that improve upon naive baselines to reduce reward hacking rates across seeds to before-training levels in some interventions. Our results demonstrate that our takeaways translate to new settings and that better monitor intervention designs are possible.

Nudging Decision Models: Do Digital Nudges Steer Fast, Generation-Free AI Agents?

Sascha Kaltenpoth, Haya Halimeh, Kevin Bösch, Oliver Müller

LLM-based agents increasingly automate business and consumer tasks but are slow and expensive. Decision models, a new class that chooses among options without generating text, are far faster and less costly. Developers call them "System One models," referencing dual process theory. We ask whether such models can be steered by digital nudges online and whether, as the theory predicts, automatic nudges affect them more than reflective ones. We adapted an online shopping experiment, previously run with humans and LLM agents, to three decision models (Jev, Decision-1, d1). Across 600 runs per model, they chose a preselected default in 97.5-99.9% and a product with social influence information in 75-96% of choices, versus 47-49% in the control. All responded more strongly to the automatic nudge, the opposite of what has been reported for reasoning-capable LLM agents. We provide first evidence that non-generative decision models are susceptible to digital nudges in a System 1 pattern, implying agent-type-specific nudge effects.

TaskNet: Building Occupational Task Inventories from Worker Interviews at Scale

Jennifer Wang, Andreas Haupt, Isaac Robinson, Phil Lewis, Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang

Understanding how AI is reshaping work requires an accurate account of the tasks workers perform. Workers are often the first to see how their jobs are changing, as new responsibilities and tools often appear in everyday work before these changes reach formal descriptions. Interviews with workers can help capture these emerging changes in job tasks. Yet existing methods support standardized ratings across workers, while open-ended conversations with workers remain costly to collect and synthesize at scale. We introduce the Stanford Task Network (TaskNet), an occupational taxonomy grounded directly in workers’ accounts of their everyday work. To construct TaskNet, we develop a mixed-initiative system that induces task inventories directly from workers. The system conducts interviews, standardizes reports into individual task lists, and aggregates these lists into occupational task inventories. Applied to six occupations spanning a range of education requirements and AI exposure, our system interviews 132 workers and produces 202 task statements from 2726 reports, all of which were accepted by O*NET Center analysts, compared to roughly 65%of workers’ write-in task suggestions collected through the current O*NET data collection process, a major alternative source for emerging tasks. Every statement is validated by 318 incumbents in 7923 ratings of relevance and importance. Relative to the task data from the O*NET 31.0 database for the sampled occupations, TaskNet covers 75% of the 118 published O*NET tasks and adds 79 tasks with no O*NET counterpart and 58 that refine existing statements. It covers 89% of tasks reported by held-out workers, compared with 79% for O*NET, and a larger share of the activities described in employer job postings (72.5% vs. 70.2%). Downstream, TaskNet changes AI exposure estimates in both directions, raising them for Software Developers and Lawyers, two occupations where AI’s effects are widely debated. Because such estimates guide workforce policy and training, more complete and up-to-date task lists can help direct these efforts toward the workers most likely to be affected. TaskNet provides a foundation for keeping occupational information current and grounded in workers’ experiences as AI reshapes work.

AI as Shared Business Infrastructure: Firm Growth and the Distribution of Gains

Panle Jia Barwick, Lu Fang, Jinhan Liu, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan

Generative AI improves performance on individual tasks, but whether those gains translate into firm growth, and for which firms, remains an open question. We study a leading Chinese e-commerce platform that in 2024 embedded a generative AI system in the operating software firms use to run their businesses, drawing on centrally developed models and platform-wide data to deliver ready-to-use applications to every firm. Complete records of each firm’s AI use, linked to its monthly operations and sales, let us trace adoption through changes in business capabilities, such as reaching customers and introducing new products, to sales growth. Using a matched difference-in-differences design and focusing on firms with annual sales below RMB 5 million, we find that adoption raises monthly sales by 51.6%, primarily through a 47.0% increase in the number of orders; instrumental-variable estimates confirm these results. Adopters reach more customers, update their product lines faster, and deliver better product and service quality, with the largest proportional gains for the smallest firms. Two mechanisms help explain the results. First, AI lowers the cost of routine work: adopters produce more product content and change listings more often while spending less staff time per order, and the gains are largest for firms that faced the highest costs relative to sales before adoption. Second, AI reduces information frictions: adopters post richer product information, and the gains are largest in markets where demand is hard to predict and matching quality is poor, especially for small firms. At the market level, product categories with greater pre-existing information frictions see more sales by existing firms, more entry, and more new products. AI expands markets rather than merely shifting sales from non-adopters to adopters.

Machine Learning Manipulation as Treachery

Daniel Pinto Talero

Data companies such as Facebook use machine learning algorithms to analyze users' personal data, revealing fine‐grained patterns about when individuals are most vulnerable. These practices, widely described as manipulative, have generated a growing literature on how data companies wrong users through their influence. In this article, I argue that existing accounts overlook a significant wrong. I show that these data companies engaged in a distinctive form of deception at the point of data collection: users were led to believe that recommendation systems existed to personalize their experience, when in fact the primary purpose was to discover their vulnerabilities. I consider several candidate accounts of what makes this deception wrong, including appeals to inauthenticity, informed consent, deception about commercial intent, and domination, and argue that none adequately captures the core wrong. I then argue that this deception constitutes a form of treachery: a deception whose purpose is to acquire power over the victim by co‐opting their agency. Drawing on cases from other contexts, I show that treachery is a pro tanto wrong that is independent of whether the acquired power is ever exercised. The concept of treachery thus identifies a prior wrong that existing accounts had not previously identified.
