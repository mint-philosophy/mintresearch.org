Five Chinese AI labs’ model upgrades lack separate public filings

Sofia Yablonskaya’s registry audit shows what outsiders cannot learn about the review of new model generations, while allowing that regulators may receive information privately.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 October 2026 741 words · ~4 min

China’s public AI filing lists do not separately identify successive general-purpose model generations at five leading startups, Sofia Yablonskaya finds in her China AI Safety Regulation Monthly Monitor. Published on October 3 and cross-posted to LessWrong on October 13, the survey follows DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Zhipu, MiniMax and StepFun through the registries maintained by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC). Their initial services appear, along with occasional later products or changes of legal entity. The lists do not establish whether regulators reviewed the subsequent model upgrades privately.

Yablonskaya examined 31 published lists: 13 for generative AI and 18 for deep synthesis, China’s regulatory category for algorithms that generate or alter text, images, audio or video. She matched labs through their legal entities and affiliates, including provincial registrations, with the latest generative-AI batch covering approvals through August 31. She excluded Alibaba, ByteDance, Baidu and Tencent because their numerous products make it harder to match a filing to a particular model. Her conclusion therefore concerns the five startups she examined.

In her compilation, DeepSeek has a single generative-AI entry, approved in May 2024, despite subsequently releasing R1 and V4. Moonshot’s entry dates to November 2023, before Kimi K2 and K3. Zhipu’s later filings concern video, an on-device model and applications, without separate entries for its newer general-purpose generations. ModelBest provides a comparison: it filed separately for the MiniCPM, MiniCPM-V and MiniCPM-O families. Some updates can nevertheless appear as amendments. A footnote in the LessWrong version identifies a later change note adding audio to StepFun’s Step1X. An upgrade can therefore appear as a change to an existing record.

Yablonskaya offers two possible explanations. Regulators may treat the initial service filing as covering subsequent versions, without requiring each upgraded model to receive a separate entry. Alternatively, companies may communicate changes directly to the CAC. She presents both as hypotheses. The lists do not disclose whether an upgrade underwent a fresh assessment, what information the company supplied, or which risks officials examined.

The 2023 Interim Measures for generative AI services apply to services offered to the public in mainland China; Article 2 excludes research and other uses that do not provide such services. Article 17 requires security assessments and algorithm filing for services capable of influencing public opinion or mobilizing society. The CAC’s September 14 filing announcement also distinguishes nationally filed services from applications that use an already-filed model and register with provincial offices. These categories track services and their providers, with no assurance that each public entry corresponds to one model generation.

The rules already require providers to amend some filed information. Article 17 refers to the 2022 Algorithmic Recommendation Provisions, whose Article 24 gives providers ten working days to complete change procedures when filed information changes. Updating that information and obtaining a new filing for every model version are different procedures. In a January guide for the Oxford China Policy Lab, Zilan Qian wrote that generative-model updates do not require fresh filings, while amendment requirements for algorithm filings appear inconsistently enforced. Yablonskaya credits Qian’s earlier observation and extends it through a systematic check of the five labs.

Public disclosure has long been narrower than disclosure to regulators. In their 2022 analysis of the algorithm registry, Matt Sheehan and Sharon Du found that public entries usually occupied a single page, while the filing manual required companies to supply training-data details and upload security self-assessments that outsiders could not inspect. The absence of those documents prevents the public from judging the substance of the assessments.

Yablonskaya also examines whether more recent safety guidance changes this picture. TC260’s AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0, released September 14 and covered here the following day, describes models concealing their capabilities and escaping restricted environments. Section 5.3.19 recommends security assessments after material changes and the ability to restore an earlier version. The framework is nonbinding guidance; these recommendations do not themselves require a fresh public filing. Yablonskaya plans to track whether regulators, standards, courts or company practices give the guidance practical effect.

The monitor’s appendix identifies another limitation in public descriptions: agent names increasingly appear in filing lists, but few entries explain actions such as operating software or calling tools. Yablonskaya attributes this partly to templates that leave little room to describe what agents do. Her planned follow-up is concrete: check the next generative-AI list, expected in mid-November, for the five labs and for information that lets outsiders trace how their systems have changed.
