Vassilevska Williams on the hints behind Claude’s algorithms

Her experiments with public models add to the debate over human contributions to the 3SUM and shortest-path results.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 October 2026 746 words · ~4 min

Virginia Vassilevska Williams says public Claude models could reproduce the approach behind a recent algorithms breakthrough when given research notes her group had left untouched since 2023. In reflections she shared on October 13, the MIT computer scientist describes two guided successes and six failures without those notes. The paper with Josh Alman was covered here on October 6. Her new account explains how much human work preceded the result, what she and Alman contributed afterward, and why assigning credit has become difficult.

The October 5 paper improves the theoretical running times for 3SUM, which asks whether three numbers in a list add to zero, and all-pairs shortest paths, which finds the shortest routes between every pair of vertices in a weighted graph. Both improvements come from one algorithm for computing selected entries of a matrix product. Claude discovered that algorithm; Alman and Vassilevska Williams simplified, strengthened and explained it. The paper says Anthropic shared the result confidentially in September and offered compensation. These are the results her new experiment revisits.

Her group’s earlier work concerned the hinted online matrix-vector multiplication conjectures proposed in 2019 by Jan van den Brand, Danupon Nanongkai and Thatchaphol Saranurak. She believed the conjectures were false and had suggested an approach for refuting them. Her first attempts failed. In 2022–23, her student Shyan Akmal tried further versions and recorded the unsuccessful work. When she saw Claude’s proof, she recognized the approach they had pursued. The missing step, in her account, was applying it to a construction in Don Coppersmith’s 1982 paper.

She tested that account with public Claude Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, supplying Akmal’s notes, Coppersmith’s paper and the paper containing the conjectures. Her prompt identified the approach and asked the models to use it to refute the conjectures; she disabled internet use and memory from other chats. Both returned what she reports as refutations within half an hour. A second prompt asked for a related graph algorithm that detects triangles among three sets of vertices. Fable took about another half-hour; she estimates Opus took three-quarters of an hour. Known mathematical reductions then connect that algorithm to 3SUM and shortest paths. She also tried a third prompt making those connections explicit, although she says it was unnecessary.

She ran that guided sequence once per model. For the comparison, she kept the two published papers but removed Akmal’s notes and the instruction to use his approach. Neither model succeeded in three attempts each. She takes this as evidence that the old notes helped, and describes her group as having been “about 1 Claude-hour away” from the result. The comparison changes both the attachment and the prompt; its small number of trials cannot establish a reliable success rate or that the notes are necessary. The note gives prompts and reported outcomes, but does not link the model transcripts or Akmal’s manuscript.

Elfarouk Harb challenged the inference with a shared ChatGPT session using the two published papers without Akmal’s notes. His prompt added a request to spend at least an hour. The session records about 43 minutes of work and claims counterexamples to all three conjectures at particular small parameter values. The shared session contains the model’s proposed mathematics, which this report has not independently checked. It also uses a different model and instruction, so it does not isolate the effect of the human notes.

Vassilevska Williams also explains the work she and Alman did after receiving the original proof. They replaced rediscovered results with citations, removed unnecessary algebraic language, simplified the route to deterministic algorithms, and extended the result to online set disjointness, a problem about answering whether two stored sets overlap. She encourages other explanations. Sankeerth Rao Karingula and Shachar Lovett’s October 11 preprint is one example: it gives a simpler proof of the 3SUM and shortest-path improvements, with weaker running-time exponents. Such work helps readers understand and build on the result.

Her reflections return to the credit problem raised in our October 9 coverage of Scott Aaronson. She acknowledges resentment over Anthropic choosing her and Alman to receive the proof, but argues that machine-generated research draws on ideas distributed across the field. Even authors may not recognize an unattributed idea an LLM has supplied. Readers can more readily judge whether a paper brings new understanding and communicates it well. She is consequently reluctant to submit their paper to STOC, the Symposium on Theory of Computing, amid disagreement over what discovery, exposition and publication should now count for.
