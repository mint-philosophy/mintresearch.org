US–UK evaluator talks leave AISI’s access terms unresolved

Anthropic’s Lord Booth-Smith told MPs that bilateral talks cover third-party evaluators, without confirming employee-like access for Britain’s AI Security Institute. He also said AISI now has access to Mythos 5.1, after declining an earlier restricted offer.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 October 2026 558 words · ~3 min

US–UK government talks include the role of third-party AI evaluators, Anthropic's Lord Booth-Smith told MPs on October 13, but he did not confirm that Britain's AI Security Institute would receive employee-like access. The distinction matters for the exchange reported by TIME's Harry Booth and shared by Jason Baker: the questioner raised that prospect, while Booth-Smith described discussions whose outcome remained unsettled. Anthropic's head of Europe, Middle East and Africa policy appeared at the Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee alongside representatives from OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Meta.

About one hour and 38 minutes into the official hearing recording, Booth-Smith described a 120-day presidential task-force process in which third-party evaluators and their place in a framework are among the subjects under discussion. He welcomed government-to-government coordination and said frontier companies should not act as intermediaries between governments. Later, asked specifically whether AISI could participate during internal development, he again referred to bilateral discussions about a framework for pre-deployment access. He welcomed closer AISI involvement but could not give a concrete answer about its form.

Booth-Smith supplied a separate update on Mythos 5.1. Anthropic had been unable to provide AISI with full unrestricted access at the relevant time, he said. AISI had instead been offered restricted access, declined to use it, and now has access to the model. Asked why the US and UK had been treated differently, he referred the question to the US government. When a member pressed him to confirm that Washington had made the decision, he said he had not been privy to those conversations and offered further information in writing. His answer does not establish who made the decision or specify the access AISI now has.

That adds to the position covered here on October 12. AI minister Kanishka Narayan had confirmed AISI's evaluation of Gemini 4 Argon after weeks of work with Washington and developers, while cautioning about the durability of British access. That announcement did not identify Mythos 5.1 as available or set out the technical permissions and timing of future evaluations. Booth-Smith's testimony adds a named model and an account of the earlier restricted offer, while leaving the broader terms unresolved.

OpenAI's Tom Duff Gordon supported guidelines for third-party evaluators and international agreement on their role. He listed questions about the distinction between evaluators and auditors, funding, conflicts of interest, and when and what they can inspect. He also supported evaluators seeing training and alignment runs, extending their work earlier than final pre-deployment testing. These were proposals for evaluation arrangements; he did not announce an agreement granting AISI that access. The hearing therefore left separate questions about access to particular models and access to the processes used to develop them.

Access also remains separate from enforcement. WIRED reports that AISI director Henry de Zoete acknowledged the institute cannot prevent a company releasing a model it judges dangerous. Cabinet Office permanent secretary Cat Little confirmed that the current regime is voluntary. Anthropic's October 9 written evidence, signed by UK policy head Daisy McGregor, likewise argues that voluntary arrangements reach only participating companies. It says Anthropic has worked voluntarily with AISI and its US counterpart on pre-deployment testing since 2024, and calls for binding rules that would require large frontier developers to engage independent evaluators regularly. Those proposals do not settle what rights AISI will obtain through the talks.
