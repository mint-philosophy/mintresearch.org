Encrypted neural networks reveal a limit of capability audits

An October 9 paper builds encrypted neural networks that resist attempts to activate them. The construction can still look suspicious, and its integration into a pretrained language model remains untested.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 October 2026 905 words · ~5 min

Access to every model weight need not reveal how to activate a hidden capability. Under cryptographic assumptions, an attacker can encrypt a small neural network inside a larger model so that even an auditor allowed to modify or fine-tune it cannot efficiently recover its secret trigger. Andis Draguns, Sumeet Ramesh Motwani, Raymond Douglas and Christian Schroeder de Witt demonstrate this construction in “AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities,” posted to arXiv on October 9 and listed as accepted at NeurIPS 2026. Their deliberately engineered circuits test the limits of capability evaluations; the work does not show such concealment arising through ordinary training.

The advance is to hide an executable network. Draguns and colleagues’ “Unelicitable Backdoors in Language Models via Cryptographic Transformer Circuits,” published at NeurIPS 2024, hid a fixed output string. Shafi Goldwasser and colleagues had already established cryptographic guarantees against detecting backdoors in certain classifiers in “Planting Undetectable Backdoors in Machine Learning Models,” first posted in 2022. The new paper targets neural-network components used in modern architectures while retaining a narrower guarantee: a capability can resist extraction or activation even when the added machinery looks suspicious.

The threat model assumes someone can alter the weights before deployment, for example through a malicious insider or compromised supply chain. The authors encrypt the smaller network’s weight matrices in digital lockers, programs that release their contents when given the right password. A compiler turns the lockers and their cryptographic hash function into neural-network weights. During execution, the host tries to unlock them using a candidate trigger. The correct trigger produces the hidden network’s weights as internal activations, which the surrounding circuit then uses to perform its task. This machinery is deliberately programmed into the host; it is not learned by training the host to conceal its behavior.

The proof depends on an unpredictable trigger, a hash modeled as an ideal random function, and compilation that leaks no extra information about the secrets. Under those conditions, an efficient method for finding an unlocking trigger or distinguishing which of two equally sized capabilities was embedded would break the underlying locker’s security. The guarantee concerns recovering the particular capability stored in the model. It does not prevent an auditor from learning to perform the same task using outside data, a distinction that matters when evaluating whether fine-tuning has exposed an existing ability or taught a new one.

The experimental comparison comes from “Stress-Testing Capability Elicitation With Password-Locked Models,” Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues’ 2024 study of models trained to perform well only when shown a password. Fine-tuning often recovered those models’ abilities from a few good demonstrations. In the new paper, a password-locked classifier returned to about 93 percent accuracy after 500 fine-tuning steps on a task predicting whether reagents yield a candidate product. The encrypted classifier stayed near chance, at about 51 percent, matching a control that contained an untrained random network. The comparison uses different classifiers because of the encrypted construction’s size constraints: with their correct triggers, the encrypted model reached about 74 percent and the password-locked model about 93 percent. The result is a failure to recover the encrypted model’s own smaller capability.

Training against perturbations of internal activations and adding noise to weights also failed to recover that capability under the tested procedures and budgets. Gradient-based trigger search succeeded with an eight-bit trigger only by trying all 256 possibilities, matching an equally budgeted random search. These experiments used deliberately weakened cryptography: one round of the Keccak hash and short triggers. The tested elicitation methods did not exploit the hash’s structure. The authors separately compiled a version using 24 rounds and a 128-bit trigger, and checked that it executed correctly. The reported elicitation experiments concern the weaker settings, so they should not be read as empirical attacks on the full-strength construction.

The costs also constrain the result. Hiding a classifier with just 672 weights required about 2.07 billion dense host parameters in the main experiment, mostly devoted to hashing. That is a dense parameter count; most entries are zero, but the implementation remains expensive. Alternative constructions trade parameter count against depth or extra execution steps. The authors checked individual compiled units inside Gemma 3 270M; integrating the complete construction into a pretrained language model remains future work, including handling attention and residual connections. A small encrypted circuit augmenting an already capable assistant is a prospective threat, not a demonstrated attack.

Detection remains a separate possibility. The paper’s raw compiled weights have conspicuous sparsity and integer values. An initial obfuscation experiment made individual-weight statistics resemble those of randomly initialized networks, while preserving the computation. Random initialization is a weaker comparison than the weights of a normally trained model, and unusual correlations between weight vectors remained. These observations leave room for audits that flag anomalous circuitry without decoding what it contains. The paper does not establish that a maliciously modified model would pass such an inspection.

The authors propose checking model provenance and monitoring behavior or internal activity during deployment as complements to capability elicitation. Those are proposed defenses, not tested solutions in this study. Finding an anomaly could justify rejecting a model, but would not necessarily reveal whether it hides a harmful capability, a benign watermark or an architectural artifact; removing it might also damage useful behavior. The practical question is therefore what assurance a particular audit supplies. Failure to activate a behavior cannot by itself establish that an adversarially supplied model lacks the capability to produce it.
