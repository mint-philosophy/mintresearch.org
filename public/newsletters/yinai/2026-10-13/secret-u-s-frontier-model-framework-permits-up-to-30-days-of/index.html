Conner argues secret AI review has become licensing

His Lawfare essay separates three meanings of voluntary participation. A government agreement to produce records by October 30 may answer only some of his questions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 October 2026 694 words · ~3 min

Adam Conner argues that the administration’s power over frontier-model releases has outgrown its promise of voluntary review. In an October 13 Lawfare essay, the Center for American Progress vice president for technology policy describes a system that functions as licensing through enforcement threats and expectations of preclearance. His argument concerns how government power operates in practice. The public executive order expressly disclaims mandatory licensing, while the framework’s undisclosed terms prevent outsiders from establishing what developers have actually accepted.

The June 2 order directs agencies to develop a classified cybersecurity benchmark, with the NSA director determining which models cross the threshold for coverage. It also calls for a voluntary framework through which developers can obtain a coverage determination, provide early government access and help choose trusted partners. The access window is up to 30 days before release to other trusted partners. That wording establishes no universal 30-day waiting period before public release. Section 3(c) says the section does not authorize mandatory licensing, preclearance or permitting; the same order preserves agencies’ existing statutory authority.

The companies’ own accounts show two different forms of government intervention. Anthropic said on June 12 that an export-control directive barred foreign nationals’ access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5, requiring it to disable both models for all customers to comply. OpenAI’s June 26 announcement said it was limiting GPT-5.6 to a small group of trusted partners at the government’s request. Both episodes preceded the completed framework that we covered in August. They document the administration’s influence over distribution without establishing the terms of a later agreement.

Conner asks which choice remains voluntary: whether to join and then obey government requests, whether to accept each request separately, or whether to submit particular models. Those possibilities imply different obligations. Attendees reportedly could not scan or photograph the framework in August. Without the terms, neither the scope of a developer’s commitment nor the relationship between the framework and existing federal testing agreements can be established publicly.

Procurement supplies one possible route from choice to compulsion. In a June Lawfare analysis that Conner cites, Jessica Tillipman explains how agencies can make participation valuable through bid-evaluation criteria, or necessary through contract requirements. Suppliers may comply to retain federal business even when the executive order itself does not require participation. Her distinction between an advantage in winning a contract and a condition of eligibility explains how pressure could intensify. It is an account of available mechanisms, not evidence that every developer has accepted a particular secret clause.

The next disclosure milestone comes from the Protect Democracy lawsuit we covered in September. A joint status report filed September 15 records the government’s agreement to produce responsive records by October 30. The narrowed requests cover four things: the framework reportedly completed by August 1; GOLD EAGLE cybersecurity-clearinghouse participation terms supplied to companies; documents identifying trusted partners; and records of the legal authority for both programs. Protect Democracy withdrew its preliminary-injunction motion. Agencies may assert FOIA exemptions, in which case they must supply a Vaughn index explaining the withholdings. Complete public disclosure is therefore not assured.

Those categories also leave gaps. GOLD EAGLE participation terms are distinct from a list of participating companies. The agreement does not expressly require all developer testing contracts, every subsequent revision of the framework or the procedures for selecting trusted partners. Publishing names would reveal who received access, without necessarily explaining why. The filing proposed another status report for November 13, when further litigation could be scheduled if needed. October 30 is a records-production commitment, not a deadline for resolving every question about the program.

Foreign access illustrates why the account needs updating alongside the institutional analysis. Conner discusses reports of restrictions on British testing. On October 12, however, AI minister Kanishka Narayan confirmed that Britain’s AI Security Institute was evaluating Gemini 4 Argon, a development covered in yesterday’s issue. His speech establishes current access to that model; it does not specify future guarantees or whether British testing begins alongside American testing. Conner’s demand is for publication of the framework before October 30. The records could make those arrangements examinable while leaving his broader licensing argument open to dispute.
