Before CAISI was sidelined, its name was changed

The office Veronica Irwin found marginalized was rebuilt by a June 2025 rebrand that kept its duties out of statute; the live fight, running on Transformer's own pages, concerns whether licensed private auditors can supply the oversight it cannot.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 18 July 2026 454 words · ~2 min

FedScoop reported in June 2025 that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had rebranded the AI Safety Institute, a Biden-era office inside NIST, as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation, declaring that "censorship and regulations have been used under the guise of national security". The core work carried over largely unchanged: voluntary standards, voluntary testing agreements with developers, model evaluations, and interagency coordination on AI risks. Congress never wrote any of it into statute; the mandate Veronica Irwin describes rests on executive tolerance, a year after the office lost the word safety.

In a July 1 Transformer essay, Fathom CEO Andrew Freedman argued that the Supreme Court's Slaughter decision, which overturned the 1935 Humphrey's Executor precedent and let presidents remove independent-agency heads at will, forecloses the traditional remedy of an insulated AI regulator. He wants to "insulate the facts instead": CAISI would set measurements, benchmarks and risk assessments; elected institutions would make the binding decisions; and licensed independent verification organizations, accredited private testers whose durability "comes from earned credibility, not legal shield", would audit the companies. Irwin's report lands in the middle of that argument, on Transformer's own pages, over what a stronger CAISI should become.

Keller Scholl answered on July 9, also in Transformer, attacking the auditor half of the design, which he traces through Freedman to earlier proposals from Gillian Hadfield and Jack Clark. Developers who choose and pay their own auditors, he argued, get auditors "optimizing for speed and cost", the incentive that let banks buy AAA ratings on risky assets before 2008. "If the main goal is safety, that will never be a sensible approach." A separate critique of the FINRA-style design presses on a different weakness: keeping frontier evaluation standards continuously current.

The auditor regime already exists in legislative text. In a June 17 primer on the Great American AI Act draft, the Cato Institute's Juan Londoño lays out the machinery: $100 million a year for CAISI, which would license and oversee the verification organizations; auditor access to "unredacted materials, records, personnel, systems", with immunity from suit short of willful misconduct; and frontier frameworks plus transparency reports from developers above $500 million in annual revenue. Most provisions sunset after three years. Londoño credits the draft with trying to "build state capacity" for the AI era and warns that the licensing scheme risks regulatory capture, with the sunsets cutting against predictable governance.

Charlie Bullock of the Institute for Law and AI, quoted throughout Irwin's piece, expects change to arrive in bursts, a pattern he calls "punctuated equilibria", borrowing the idea from Caleb Watney of Coefficient Giving, Transformer's primary funder. "Maybe nothing will happen for a while," he says, until capabilities rise, "somebody gets spooked, or some people get hurt."
