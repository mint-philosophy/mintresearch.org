The whispered-protectionism fight splitting Trump's AI world

Dean Ball's Kimi post predicted agencies would manufacture regulatory risk around Chinese open models; Will Manidis answered with Youngstown, Choke Point, and a demand for majority public equity, and David Sacks and a Pentagon official turned on Ball in public.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 18 July 2026 498 words · ~2 min

On X on July 17, Dean Ball, a former White House AI adviser now OpenAI's head of strategic futures, posted the six observations on Kimi K3 that set the fight off. He praised Moonshot's model ("It's a very good model!"), called open-weight models "inherently decelerationist", and predicted the Trump administration would eventually "create large amounts of regulatory risk around the use of open-weight Chinese models" by directing every agency to issue soft law manufacturing fear, uncertainty, and doubt: "It needn't be that well justified." An open-weight-dominant world, he added, ends in "full AI communism", with states serving subsidized models as digital public infrastructure.

In his reply on X, Will Manidis argued that American protectionism has always operated in daylight and arrived with a bill. Truman seized the steel mills in 1952, and the Supreme Court sent him back to Congress in Youngstown, where Justice Black wrote that presidential power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself". Operation Choke Point pushed banks away from payday lenders and gun dealers without a statute, and in NRA v. Vullo a unanimous Court held that officials may not "coerce private parties" into dropping what government disfavors. By Manidis's account, this administration's own FDIC and OCC barred bank supervisors in April from informal reputational-risk pressure, the machinery Ball's scenario would rebuild for AI. Whatever agencies rebuilt would operate where formal capacity is scarce; the federal institute responsible for frontier-model oversight runs on a $15 million budget and a weak mandate. The bill side has precedents too: Congress put the atom under the Atomic Energy Commission, and AT&T's protected monopoly cost it compulsory licensing of roughly 9,000 patents in the 1956 consent decree. Today's labs, he wrote, seek public infrastructure, federal preemption, export controls, and a market cleared of their cheapest competitor, with OpenAI floating a 5 percent public stake in return. His two honest options: open competition with written rules for defense and critical infrastructure, or overt protection paid for with majority public equity. Anything else he names regulatory capture.

Administration figures then piled on. On July 19, David Sacks, who left his White House AI and crypto role in March, wrote on X that whether Ball was "confessing to a regulatory capture strategy" or predicting one, "the weaponization of regulatory uncertainty as a competitive tool should be completely unacceptable", and that the closed labs, "a duopoly in terms of AI model revenue", want government to eliminate their open-source competition. Pentagon official Emil Michael called Ball a "supreme village idiot" and said intervention should run through "the democratic process not some Deep State scheme", MIT Technology Review reports, reading the episode as Trump's AI coalition at war with itself. A Business Insider roundup adds Chamath Palihapitiya ("The future is open source") and Suhail Doshi to the open-model side. Ball pointed critics to two and a half years of written policy work and lamented that it has become "impossible to write as I used to".
