Scott Aaronson on the Unique Games proof and the race to understand it

Dana Moshkovitz used AI to understand an unfamiliar proof of a conjecture she had pursued for years. Aaronson examines the mathematical ideas, the credit for explaining them and two ways companies distribute AI discoveries.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 7 October 2026 1448 words · ~7 min

Scott Aaronson posted “The Mathocalypse” on his blog Shtetl-Optimized on October 7, the day after OpenAI released its collection of model-written mathematics. He ranked the release day among mathematics’ most significant, a claim other mathematicians had already debated. His essay centers on one result, a claimed proof of Subhash Khot’s Unique Games Conjecture, a longstanding research goal of Aaronson’s wife, UT Austin complexity theorist Dana Moshkovitz. OpenAI’s repository initially listed 722 manuscripts in 372 families of related papers, at different verification stages.

In a unique game, each node of a network gets a color, and each edge carries a rule that, once the color at one end is fixed, allows exactly one color at the other. Khot conjectured in 2002 that when nearly all the rules can be satisfied, finding a coloring that satisfies even a small fraction is NP-hard: an efficient solution would solve many other computationally difficult problems efficiently. Prasad Raghavendra showed in 2008 that, if the conjecture holds, one semidefinite-programming algorithm, which approximates these problems through continuous optimization, achieves the best possible approximation for every constraint satisfaction problem. Moshkovitz never doubted the conjecture, Aaronson writes, though many did: when Ryan O’Donnell of Carnegie Mellon polled theorists in 2014, only O’Donnell and Khot raised their hands, Quanta reported. A 2018 proof of the related 2-to-2 conjecture by Khot, Dor Minzer, Muli Safra and collaborators, drawing on an idea of Moshkovitz’s, gave unique-games hardness only where about half the rules can be met.

OpenAI’s 58-page manuscript “The Unique Games Theorem” claims the remaining step: a deterministic reduction from 3SAT, a standard NP-hard problem, that turns satisfiable formulas into unique games where nearly all rules can be met, and unsatisfiable ones into games where only a small fraction can. It keeps the central theorem of the 2018 work but replaces that work’s random noise, which even correct answers survive only about half the time, with recursively combined quadratic maps over a finite number system that correct answers almost always survive. Aaronson considers its correctness likely, citing its computer-checkable Lean formalization; OpenAI’s catalogue of formalizations gives their human review status as “unchecked.”

Asked in the comments whether the model might have exploited bugs in Lean’s kernel, Aaronson called that extremely unlikely: in the areas he knows, the techniques look appropriate to the problems, and Boaz Barak, a Harvard computer scientist also on OpenAI’s technical staff, and others had read the chains of thought without finding signs of it.

Aaronson then quotes Moshkovitz’s texts from the night of the release. “It feels like something written by someone who’s on psychedelics,” she wrote. She found its citations often irrelevant and its use of earlier work unexplained despite known impossibility results; she considered the writing unreadable without AI help. She had used Astra, an AI model, to gather the noise gadget’s guarantees from claims scattered across the paper. The release also claims the conjecture’s best-known consequences, such as optimal hardness for Max-Cut, through independent proofs, she noted; OpenAI’s manuscript map lists four such proofs. The proof invents an encoding recursively, unlike the standard long and short codes: “some alien craziness.” She nevertheless saw much to learn.

That afternoon Aaronson relayed her second look in a comment: on further inspection, the proof builds a new code organized as a tree, with a noise test resembling the short code’s. About two hours later he reported that after a day asking Astra to explain the proof she was beginning to understand it, having previously avoided AI. At 3 a.m. Eastern on October 8 he wrote that she mostly understood the proof, admired its new ideas and wanted to give talks on it.

OpenAI’s summary of the model’s reasoning shows that the proof grew out of a different question. The model was asked to prove or refute that beating the semidefinite-programming approximation is NP-hard for every constraint satisfaction problem, without assuming the Unique Games Conjecture, and it chose to get there by proving the conjecture first. Along the way it weighed routes from Moshkovitz’s own work, including the “real code” she proposed with Khot and a reduction she published with Ronen Eldan. The final manuscript cites none of her papers.

Aaronson also asks how such results should reach mathematicians. On October 5, Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams posted “Truly Subquadratic 3SUM and Truly Subcubic APSP via Triangles in Sparse Lopsided Graphs” on arXiv, reporting the first polynomial improvements on textbook algorithms for 3SUM, which looks for three numbers in a list that sum to zero, and for all-pairs shortest paths. Claude, they write, found the algorithm with no human input after an Anthropic employee asked it to verify and improve some cryptographic constructions; Anthropic shared it under a confidentiality agreement and offered compensation. The authors simplified and extended it. Aaronson contrasts the two approaches: OpenAI’s public release makes mathematicians compete to explain messy proofs, potentially with little enjoyment or credit; Anthropic’s private handoff lets a company choose who communicates its results. He asks readers which they prefer.

Quanta’s Ben Brubaker reported on October 7 that Minzer, a professor at MIT, started hearing on September 11 that OpenAI had an unannounced proof of the conjecture. Three days later he and his graduate students Yumou Fei and Shuo Wang rushed out a 95-page proof of Khot’s 4-to-1 games conjecture for fully satisfiable instances, a slightly weaker form of his 2-to-1 conjecture. OpenAI’s release includes a Lean-formalized manuscript claiming the stronger 2-to-1 statement. “You don’t know if you’re going to get scooped by the trillion-dollar company,” Minzer told Quanta.

Aaronson annotates further results from his own field. An epigraph from Stanford’s Omer Reingold singles out derandomizing computation with limited memory. The corresponding manuscript claims L = RL = BPL: randomness gives no extra power when memory grows only logarithmically with input size. Another claims an unexpectedly positive answer to a version of the unitary synthesis problem Aaronson posed with Greg Kuperberg in 2007, whether any unitary quantum operation can be approximated efficiently to fixed accuracy using a suitable classical oracle, though it gives no efficient way to construct the oracle. A third claims a nearly fourth-power gap between how many input bits randomized and quantum algorithms must read, disproving a cubic bound conjectured in “Degree vs. Approximate Degree and Quantum Implications of Huang’s Sensitivity Theorem,” Aaronson et al.’s 2021 STOC paper. He takes solace that P ≠ NP remains absent.

He tentatively attributes the results to OpenAI’s latest internal model, without a bespoke 10,000-agent system like that behind its Navier-Stokes proof, and with about 5 percent of roughly 8,000 attempted problems solved. OpenAI’s README puts the problems posed at approximately 4,000 and gives no success rate. The model might reach paying ChatGPT customers within a couple of months, depending on OpenAI’s safety board, Aaronson adds; OpenAI describes work toward responsible release without giving a date.

In an update added on October 7 Eastern, Aaronson noted cryptography’s absence from the release, a gap Justin Drake had flagged that morning, and said unnamed sources told him AI companies had cautiously begun private tests of their models’ ability to break important cryptographic protocols.

One result Aaronson linked has since been withdrawn. In a repository update headed October 7, committed at 1:03 a.m. Eastern on October 8, OpenAI reported that a sign error in one manuscript broke a construction two others relied on, and withdrew all three, including a paper on the rational Hodge conjecture for products of K3 surfaces that Aaronson had listed as partial progress toward the Hodge Conjecture. The notice says the gap leaves that theorem unproved without establishing that the mathematical statement is false. The update left 719 manuscripts, revised 14 and put formalized top-line results at 300 of 719.

On October 7 the Association for Human Mathematics, in a statement Terence Tao reposted, accused OpenAI of disregarding research norms and urged mathematicians “to discontinue their work with OpenAI,” while Raghu Meka, a co-author of the short-code paper the proof cites, argued in a guest post on Tao’s blog that some AI solutions, “while brilliant, are also not completely alien,” and that perceived barriers had discouraged human attempts. Justin Weinberg at Daily Nous quoted Moshkovitz’s texts and asked whether philosophy faces a similar reckoning.

Aaronson scorns blanket dismissals of the results. Answering Jordana Cepelewicz’s Quanta essay, which likened an AI proof to being teleported alone to a fog-bound mountain peak, he argues that mathematicians can use AI to understand its proofs while sustaining a community that values developing understanding together. Moshkovitz pushed back on the metaphor in her own comment: AI built on existing research communities, frameworks and results; its advances leave mathematical work to do.
