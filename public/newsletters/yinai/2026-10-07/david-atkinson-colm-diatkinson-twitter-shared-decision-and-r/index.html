Earlier layers helped Qwen3 report its learned preferences

Atkinson, Plunkett and Bau locate shared mechanisms behind choices and accurate self-reports. Their controlled test distinguishes groups of models; researchers disagree about whether its results establish introspection.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 7 October 2026 1337 words · ~7 min

Qwen3 models reported learned preferences more accurately when those preferences were stored in earlier layers of the network. Accurate reports and choices also depended more strongly on the same weights. David I. Atkinson and David Bau of Northeastern University and Dillon Plunkett, chief scientist at Eleos AI Research, report the results in “Identifying Introspection From the Inside,” a COLM 2026 paper posted to arXiv on October 5, with a poster at the conference on October 6; Atkinson summarized it in a thread on X. The authors examine the network’s internal computations to test whether accurate self-reports arise from the same processes as the choices they describe.

Atkinson’s team reserves “introspection” for self-reports that are faithful, matching what the model does, and grounded, caused by the process they describe; grounding, they argue, needs causal evidence from inside the model. Their experiment, outlined in our October 6 issue, moves the design of Plunkett and colleagues’ 2025 “Self-Interpretability” study from GPT-4o to Qwen3, whose open weights the team could inspect. Using small trainable add-ons called LoRA adapters, the researchers taught the models to choose between pairs of options for 100 fictional characters, each with random hidden weights over five attributes, and never trained them to report those weights. Faithfulness is the correlation between the weights a model states, when asked in a separate conversation, and the weights its choices reveal; 1 is a perfect match.

In Qwen3-32B, accurate reports lagged far behind accurate choices. After 1,000 training steps the model’s choices already tracked the hidden weights closely, yet its faithfulness was about 0.25; by 3,000 steps faithfulness had reached 0.83 while the choices improved only modestly. The authors liken the delay to grokking, the sudden late generalization that Alethea Power and colleagues reported in 2022, though grokking stays within one task and this ability transfers to a second task. Smaller Qwen3 models, from 0.6 billion to 14 billion parameters, learned to choose well but, trained the same way, never reported the random preferences faithfully.

The two 32B checkpoints chose about equally well but reported very differently, allowing the team to compare their internal computations. Removing successive groups of adapter layers from the front or back of the 64-layer network showed that the faithful checkpoint stores its preferences five to six layers earlier. The authors speculate that reports become accurate once preferences sit early enough for the model’s existing mechanisms for putting information into words to read them. They tested the idea on Qwen3-14B, which reports badly when all 40 of its layers are trained. Training only its first 10, 15 or 20 layers made the reports markedly more faithful, reaching 0.74 at 20 layers, Atkinson writes; once training extended to layer 25 or beyond, faithfulness collapsed while the choices stayed about as accurate. Training only later layers, in matching amounts, never produced faithful reports, which the authors say rules out the possibility that early-layer training helped only because it changed fewer weights.

Next, the team asked whether internal structure alone could tell a faithful group of models from an unfaithful one without knowing in advance which was which. They trained fresh adapters for 32 previously unseen characters on each checkpoint, using matched data, settings and initial values. Using attribution patching, a quick approximation of how much each weight affects an output, they scored every adapter weight’s importance for choosing and for reporting, then measured how far the two profiles overlapped, a score they call attribution similarity, on which 1 means identical profiles. Faithful models averaged 0.34 and unfaithful ones 0.08. In faithful models, importance for both tasks peaked at layer 38; in unfaithful ones, choosing peaked 11 layers deeper than reporting. Activating only the weight matrices that scored highest for one task recovered the other task’s behavior with far fewer matrices in faithful models.

The test does not depend on understanding the report; it could work even if the model answered in an unfamiliar language, the authors write. Atkinson calls the approach complementary to Jack Lindsey’s concept-injection experiments, published by Anthropic, which plant a known concept in Claude’s activations and ask whether the model notices; Claude Opus 4.1 and 4 did so about 20% of the time with the right layer and strength. Lindsey sets the internal state and checks the report, while Atkinson’s team compares the internals of models whose reports are already known to be accurate or inaccurate.

The authors confine their claims to linear preferences over five attributes and to lightweight adapters on one main model family. Their method, they write, “discriminates between groups, not individuals”: the groups’ scores overlap, and while high similarity counts as sufficient evidence of faithfulness, low similarity is not strong evidence against it. Within one checkpoint, similarity tracked each character’s faithfulness only weakly, which they call “a sign of life.” They cannot yet explain why training moves the preferences earlier. A Gemma-4 replication found the same early-layer storage in a faithful adapter on the 31-billion-parameter model, without Qwen’s delayed emergence.

Plunkett’s 2025 study described itself as a step toward Ethan Perez and Robert Long’s 2023 proposal to train models on questions about themselves and corroborate their answers, including with interpretability, so that self-reports might help assess whether AI systems have morally significant states. Atkinson and his co-authors also cite Jan Betley, Owain Evans and colleagues, whose models, fine-tuned to write insecure code, said “The code I write is insecure.” In a November 2025 LessWrong post, Plunkett and Adam Morris argued that fine-tuning tests of introspection cannot by themselves rule out “causal bypassing,” in which training produces an accurate report through a path that skips the state it describes: a model trained to be risk-seeking might also store the fact that it is risk-seeking, and keep reporting it even if the behavior stopped. The new paper cites that post, and its authors allow that a faithful adapter with low similarity “might be a correct confabulator,” accurate by just such a route.

On X, Bau wrote that the setup “suggests a path for lie detection.” Jan Kulveit called the paper “direct mechanistic evidence for the Three Layer Model of LLM psychology,” a psychological framework he set out on LessWrong in 2024. Adam Karvonen wrote that the models’ much better reporting of behaviors stored in earlier layers “makes sense and is pretty interesting.”

Jon Stokes, quoting Bau’s thread, argued that because choices and reports come from separate prompts in separate context windows, the model is simply writing out the preference function it learned: “What they're describing already has a perfectly good term: generalization.” He noted that the report prompt asks how the model weighted each attribute “while making your decision,” though by his account it made no decision in that pass, and objected to the paper’s psychological and “organism” language as taking a side in the public argument over whether AI is alive. When Daniel Arista asked how anyone could rule out the possibility that a model asked to explain itself is merely generating new text, Bau answered that “In controlled settings we know,” since the paper’s two populations are trained on the same choices and only one describes them accurately. Arista, who said he found the design clever, replied that this does not show the same model reporting its thoughts faithfully and asked what exactly was being claimed.

At the same conference, Shashwat Singh, Tal Linzen and Shauli Ravfogel of New York University argue in a separate paper, “Can LLMs Introspect? A Reality Check,” that a test of introspection must require privileged access, so that it cannot be solved from cues in the input, and second-order computation, a representation of the model’s own first-order state. They call Plunkett’s 2025 design “the most carefully controlled positive result in this literature” but consider the second condition open, since fine-tuning could have stored a fact about the model’s preferences that it simply retrieves, as Plunkett’s team itself noted. Atkinson’s team requires only faithfulness and grounding, and its paper does not test for a second-order step.
