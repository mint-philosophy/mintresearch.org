IdeaLens detects traces of AI outlines in stories people wrote

In a controlled experiment, IdeaLens flagged 34 of 50 stories people wrote from AI-generated outlines, while prose detectors missed most of them. Its wider results and public-demo errors have prompted debate over what the detector measures.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 7 October 2026 1423 words · ~7 min

Human-written stories retained detectable traces of the AI-generated outlines their authors followed in a controlled experiment. Rishanth Rajendhran, Jenna Russell, Mohit Iyyer and five colleagues, most of them at the University of Maryland, report the result in “IdeaLens: Detecting AI Ideas in Long-form Writing,” an arXiv preprint posted October 5 and announced by Rajendhran on X the next day. In one test, IdeaLens flagged 34 of 50 stories that people wrote from outlines of AI-written stories; Pangram 4, Pangram Labs’ current AI-text detector, flagged four.

The authors open with an episode from August. As The Washington Sun reported, Pangram rated Stanley Druckenmiller’s Wall Street Journal op-ed as AI-written, Druckenmiller told NOTUS “of course I used AI,” and the Journal’s editorial page editor, Paul Gigot, said the question was whether a contributor’s work “reflects an author’s original argument.” Pangram estimates the source of the wording, the authors argue, while Gigot’s defense concerns the source of the ideas. So do newer rules such as Springer Nature’s AI policy, which forbids passing off AI-generated hypotheses or conclusions as a person’s own. In a footnote, the authors report that IdeaLens gives the op-ed’s ideas a 98.3 percent AI score, which they distinguish from direct evidence of how it was written.

No large dataset records who came up with a document’s ideas, so the team borrowed labels from a prose detector: they took about a million English web pages that an earlier version of Pangram had judged wholly human or wholly AI, and assumed that each page’s ideas shared the source of its prose. Gemini models reduced each page to an outline, an ordered list of items that each pair a role such as “Evaluation” with a short description of content. For training, a Gemini model paraphrased every outline so that little of the original wording survived, and an Nvidia Nemotron model learned to predict Pangram’s label from the paraphrased outline alone. With the wording gone, the authors reason, the model must learn the labels mainly from the ideas, which they define, following Linda Flower and John Hayes’s 1981 cognitive process theory of writing, as the claims, events and organization an author chooses to include. As a control, a twin model, ProseLens, learned the same labels from the raw text.

The authors built two experiments in which ideas and prose come from different sources. In IdeaShift, which resembles the arrangement Gigot defended, GPT-5.6 models wrote documents from plans drawn from human-written documents, with the plans ranging from a bare topic to a full outline. IdeaLens flagged 95 percent of the documents written from a topic alone and 7 percent of those written from a full human outline, while Pangram 4 still flagged 92 percent of the latter. When the plans came from AI-written documents, IdeaLens flagged more than 96 percent at every level of detail. Across a dozen existing evaluations in which a model polished, paraphrased or rewrote human writing, IdeaLens called 95 percent of documents human, against 44 percent for Pangram 4.

TwiceTold, a new dataset, reverses the arrangement. GPT-5.6 Sol wrote a 500-word story in each of 50 genres, and 17 computer science graduate students and faculty wrote new stories in person from paraphrased outlines of those stories, without seeing the originals. IdeaLens flagged 34 of the 50 rewrites, or 68 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 54 to 79 percent; Pangram 4 flagged four and ProseLens none. The stories it missed mostly added events or world-building. When IdeaLens read the full stories instead of extracted outlines, it flagged only three; the authors attribute this to the writers’ added detail. They say a larger sample is needed to establish how far the result generalizes. The released data also show that the writing was concentrated among a few participants: two of the 17 writers produced 33 of the stories, and IdeaLens flagged 16 of one writer’s 18 against 8 of the other’s 15.

On 19 existing benchmarks of wholly human or wholly AI text, IdeaLens caught 91 percent of AI documents while flagging under 1 percent of human ones; Pangram 4 caught 97 percent. Though trained only on English, IdeaLens handles 24 languages by reading English outlines. It caught only about half of the stories by Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5.1, both released after its June 2026 data cutoff, while Pangram 4 caught nearly all of them. Outline extraction also varies between runs; near the threshold, only about half of verdicts held across five extractions of the same document.

To see what drives the scores, the authors had GPT models propose features from 90,000 scored outline items and checked them on held-out items. IdeaLens scores ideas as more AI-like when their details are generic, their claims exceed the evidence or they omit costs; it scores them as more human-like when they cite a source readers can find, specify a case or state their limits. Earlier studies found related patterns. The group’s StoryScope paper, which appears at COLM 2026, distinguished AI fiction from human fiction by narrative choices alone. In a 2024 Science Advances experiment the IdeaLens paper does not cite, Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser found that stories people wrote with AI-supplied ideas resembled one another more than stories written without them. Zhuoyang Zou and colleagues at Penn State and the University of Sheffield posted a parallel preprint the same day as IdeaLens, “Who Wrote It Is Not Enough,” finding that AI-origin insights in peer reviews mostly stay generic or restate the paper under review.

Researchers and commentators have argued over what to call the signal. Ted Underwood called the work “likely a bit fragile” but “very cool.” He noted on Bluesky that shuffling the outline items or stripping their role labels barely changes accuracy, and that a model shown only the sequence of roles still does far better than chance; he suggested that “what they’re detecting is a kind of specificity.” Brian Frye argued on X that “they aren’t identifying ideas, but generic content.” Answering a similar objection from the journal Una Mens: Homo et Machina, coauthor Marzena Karpinska of Simon Fraser University wrote that “content (what you include) and organization (in what order) are part of ideas.” Max Spero, a coauthor of Pangram’s technical reports, wrote that “there is a lot more grey area here than in text provenance” and called IdeaLens “an impressive proof of concept.”

Users of the free demo at ideadetector.ai compared its scores with their accounts of how their texts were written. Vauhini Vara reported that an essay her father wrote and had ChatGPT significantly edit scored 19 percent for AI ideas, though Pangram called it AI or AI-assisted. Nevin Climenhaga tested a philosophy paper he had an AI write by extending his earlier work; its ideas scored 0.04 percent AI and its prose 97.5 percent, and Rajendhran replied that a model working from a person’s writing tends to reuse that person’s ideas. Andy Wheeler tested a paper that AI wrote entirely from what he says was “100% my idea”; its ideas scored 76 percent AI, and the demo labeled them AI.

Wheeler’s result depends on the demo’s settings. The paper’s headline numbers use one cutoff, set so that 1 percent of 80,000 human-written calibration documents are flagged, which means flagging ideas that score above 86.3 percent AI. According to its published configuration, the demo applies a separate 1 percent cutoff for each of eight formats, from 57 percent for academic writing to 92 percent for nonfiction, so Wheeler’s paper was flagged under a cutoff the headline results do not use. On other collections of human writing, the share wrongly flagged ranged from 0.01 percent of web pages from 2019, before ChatGPT, to 3.4 percent of the human documents in IdeaShift. Rajendhran told one critic, “At our standard 1% threshold, IdeaLens has a 3% false positive rate.”

Russell’s post presented the demo as an answer to anyone who has defended AI writing with “but the ideas are mine”: “Now you can verify!” In replies, Rajendhran called it a research demo whose verdicts “should not be taken as evidence by themselves of AI use,” and the authors write in their ethics statement that IdeaLens’s predictions “must not be used by themselves as conclusive evidence to establish AI use in writing.” The team has released its code, 12 models, four datasets and a pip package; scoring a document costs about two cents in bulk. Iyyer, sharing the demo, asked users to report where it goes wrong: “We’re especially interested in failure cases.”
