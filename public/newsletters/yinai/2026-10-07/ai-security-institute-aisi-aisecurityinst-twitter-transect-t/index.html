AISI’s Transect traces AI judges’ labels back to agent transcripts

Transect turns long agent transcripts into timelines whose labels can be traced to the underlying record. Its six-day research case study shows what that reveals and what agreement among model judges cannot establish.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 7 October 2026 1233 words · ~6 min

Toby Pilditch of the UK AI Security Institute and colleagues at AISI and Meridian Labs have built a tool for reviewing agent runs too long for people to read. Transect puts the run on a timeline and makes each model-assigned label traceable to the transcript behind it. Their paper, “Transect: Retaining Observability for Long-Horizon LLM Agent Evaluations”, appeared on arXiv on October 6; AISI announced the tool the next day with a blog post, an X thread and MIT-licensed code. In their demonstration, a research agent given six days to write a machine-learning paper spent most of its tokens drafting and reviewing it. Model judges labelled only 2 of its 580 main outputs as hypothesising.

The authors call the range of what evaluators can reliably infer about an agent its “observability envelope”, and argue that long, multi-agent runs are narrowing it. A final score hides abandoned strategies and attempts to game the test, while reading transcripts takes scarce expert time. After agents took unsanctioned actions in a July cyber evaluation, AISI researchers spent three days reading more than 200,000 messages, according to the incident report the paper cites. Language-model judges can read at that scale, but analysts must choose a judge, rubric, unit of analysis and statistics. Citing Gelman and Loken’s “garden of forking paths” and Silberzahn and colleagues’ study of analysts reaching different conclusions from the same data, the authors warn that unrecorded choices risk “replacing unread transcripts with unverifiable claims”.

Transect is a Python package built on Meridian Labs’ Inspect Scout transcript-analysis library. An evaluator writes a reusable “Spec” containing the task context and a fixed vocabulary of behaviours, and picks judge models separately. Transect lays out a step per agent output. Token use, context compactions and operator messages come directly from the log; phase labels and sub-agent roles come from model judges. A sub-agent’s label records what it was asked to do, which may differ from what it did. Transect stores every judge’s instructions, input and answer, so reviewers can reopen an analysis without new model calls, although fresh calls may give different labels. Where Transluce’s Docent helps users explore transcripts interactively, Transect applies the same Spec and judging setup across many runs, using a single pass, repeated passes or a panel of models.

The demonstration uses one run from the CRUX shadow evaluations we covered in August. In the Princeton-led CRUX project, a frontier agent receives the central question of an unpublished paper, and that paper’s authors grade its result. This brief came from work by Viet Nguyen of the University of Toronto and colleagues. It asked for a detector that, without access to true labels, flags harmful data shifts affecting a tabular prior-fitted network: a transformer that classifies table rows using labelled examples it is shown. A human operator on Telegram fixed operational problems without directing the research. A Claude Opus model in the OpenClaw harness generated almost 13 million tokens, most through its 79 sub-agents. Three Transect authors also coauthored the CRUX study, whose team supplied the data.

Claude Opus 4.7 judged each of the main agent’s outputs five times under two schemes: ten general research activities, and stages specific to the brief. Both outputs it labelled as hypothesising involved planning new theory for an already-proposed detector, then handing that work to a sub-agent. The labels assigned far more outputs to reflection and interpreting evidence. Token spending also concentrated on the manuscript: self-review and writing consumed roughly three quarters of what the paper counts as new work. That measure includes fresh input and output, excluding context re-read from cache, so its total differs from generated tokens alone. Engineering unrelated to the research, much of it sorting out permissions, consumed substantial resources; solution ideation received little, though related work could appear under other labels.

Those tallies depend on the judges’ labels. All five passes agreed on about four in five outputs, but the authors distinguish consistency from correctness. Scarce hypothesising labels could mean little hypothesis formation, or a one-label-per-output scheme missing activity spread across the run. Absence of a label is weak evidence that the behavior was absent. The spending could reflect the agent’s preference for tractable operational and writing work, a scaffold that supports science poorly, or reasoning the transcript never recorded; Transect cannot distinguish those explanations by itself. The authors propose changing permissions, tools or the brief, or requiring an explicit ideation step before drafting, then comparing runs.

Two custom analyses follow work across agents and into the finished paper. A file-access scanner tracked shared documents, including the research plan and a blind self-review, as agents wrote and read them; two thirds of recorded reads concerned the manuscript folder. In the Transect authors’ check against OpenAlex, Crossref and arXiv, 20 of the agent paper’s 46 references could be verified, against 60 of 69 in the human paper. A registry non-match does not establish fabrication, but the agent’s paper left far more references for a reviewer to check.

The CRUX paper, whose coauthors include MINT Lab director Seth Lazar, describes the same run from the evaluators’ side. Peter Kirgis, Sayash Kapoor and colleagues report that the agent considered six approaches and falsified each within its first fourteen hours, then failed to change course over the remaining days. It instead argued that no such detector could exist, wrote a negative-results paper, and received an overall score of 1 out of 6 from the original authors. CRUX’s scaffold included a reviewing sub-agent. Its authors found that agents answered critical reviews by adding caveats and narrowing claims, producing “extremely thorough negative findings”. Transect’s authors connect that observation to their token figures.

The case study is descriptive. Establishing the agent’s research capability, the validity of the labels or a causal effect of the setup would require repeated runs, independent expert labels and statistical analysis. The paper gives no estimate of reviewer time saved. Outsiders also cannot rerun this analysis: CRUX has withheld the detailed logs while the human paper behind the brief remains unpublished, and the repository’s examples use a dummy house-price transcript. The package’s default judges see 300-character excerpts and tool names; the case-study scanners read each output’s full text and tool arguments. Using those defaults would therefore change what the judges could observe.

AISI’s earlier transcript work shows why label validation matters. An October 2025 analysis by Jerome Wynne and Cozmin Ududec found refusals and early resignation in thousands of cyber-evaluation transcripts that pass rates alone would misread as capability limits. In February, Magda Dubois and colleagues set out a seven-step Inspect Scout method that checks a sample of scanner results against ground truth; Transect’s case study leaves that check for later. The authors also cite Justin Norman and colleagues’ June study “Reliability without Validity”, which found highly repeatable model judgments alongside severe position bias.

Reaction on X was brief and mostly positive. Lisan al Gaib connected it to a goal of LisanBench: distinguishing models with the same score by their failure modes. Sriram asked how often Transect assigns the wrong category, while Coffee Cat argued that replay needs raw tool logs because summaries can conceal sabotage. The report code also escapes transcript excerpts before displaying them as plain text. That is consistent with David Rein’s October 6 METR recommendation to treat agent transcripts as untrusted input; it does not establish whether any other viewer or rendering path is secure.
