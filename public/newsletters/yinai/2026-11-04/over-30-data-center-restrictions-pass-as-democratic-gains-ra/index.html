Yesterday in AI · 4 November 2026

Hashim forecasts stronger federal AI legislation in early 2027

Local data center restrictions have passed; Hashim’s forecast of a stronger federal bill in early 2027 depends on Republican cooperation and a more receptive Trump.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 4 November 2026 1049 words · ~5 min

Shakeel Hashim expects the Democratic midterm gains to produce stronger federal AI legislation in early 2027. In “What the blue tsunami means for AI policy”, published by Transformer on November 4, he predicts tougher safety requirements for the most powerful models and less federal interference with state regulation. He expects larger Democratic majorities and disappointing results for industry-funded candidates to ease passage, although President Trump’s willingness to sign a bill remains uncertain.

Hashim’s congressional arithmetic was provisional: Democrats could finish with 56 Senate seats and as many as 261 House seats. By Wednesday evening, CBS projected at least 54 Democratic Senate seats, with Alaska and Mississippi unresolved. Hashim continues the argument of his October 30 essay: AI influenced some local contests but did not decide the national election. That essay cited a New York Times/Siena poll in which fewer than 0.5% of respondents named AI or data centers as their most important voting issue. He expects the election’s consequences for AI policy to be much larger than AI’s contribution to the result.

On data centers, Hashim cites Semafor’s report of restrictive votes in more than 30 localities across nine states. Other media tallies count different sets of measures. Scientific American, using Ballotpedia data, reports restrictive outcomes on 40 of 41 questions across ten states; The Verge, citing Redbud Consulting’s tracker, reports 33 among 45 as of Wednesday morning. These are media counts, not a single certified national total. Scientific American reports that more than 80% of Wells, Maine, voters approved a retroactive 180-day moratorium. Its exception was Harwood, North Dakota, where voters rejected additional industrial-project review by roughly three to one.

Hashim expects Democratic governors in Texas and Georgia to restrict construction further. Reuters reports that Gina Hinojosa defeated Greg Abbott in Texas and Keisha Lance Bottoms won Georgia, both after campaigning for moratoria. The results follow the permitting risks in our November 1 coverage of SemiAnalysis: in the firm’s scenario where every pause-supporting candidate won, their states contained roughly 30 GW of scheduled 2028 capacity. Texas accounted for nearly 30% of projected national additions. Those were estimates of exposure, not capacity already delayed. Reuters also corroborates Hashim’s attribution to Goldman Sachs of possible temporary halts affecting about 45% of planned US expansion, adding that Goldman expects disruption to be short-lived. HSBC analysts offered tighter oversight without broad moratoria as one possible outcome. Elon Musk responded that data centers in space looked appealing.

Hashim gives four reasons for expecting congressional action. Democrats have made AI a priority: Ben Brody reported Greg Casar’s call for CEO testimony, and Jay Shooster noted Jon Ossoff’s discussion of catastrophic AI risk in his victory speech. Larger Democratic majorities would reduce the concessions needed to pass legislation. Hashim also expects Republican losses to make senators more willing to break with Trump. His 56-seat scenario would require four Republicans to reach the usual 60-vote threshold for ending a legislative filibuster. Finally, he argues that industry PACs’ disappointing results will make lawmakers less afraid of their spending.

South Carolina was the industry’s conspicuous defeat: NBC projected Annie Andrews’s victory over Darline Graham, reporting 51.1% to 46.7%. Fortune reports $4.8 million in combined support for Graham from American Mission and Building the Future, and losses for both PAC networks. The Next Web reports that Leading the Future’s American Mission also backed losing House candidates Aaron Flint and Laurie Buckhout, while its Democratic arm backed winner Jared Moskowitz. Public First’s Democratic arm backed winners in Ohio and Pennsylvania; its Republican arm backed several losers. Both industry-backed and safety-backed candidates had mixed results.

Hashim predicts legislation stronger than the FRONTIER Act, requiring tough safety standards, mandatory testing and approval of the most powerful models, with narrower preemption of state law. The introduced bill already requires developers to describe risk assessments and review them when deciding whether to deploy. Section 5 requires very large developers to retain licensed independent verification organizations for ongoing assessment, with reports at least every six months. Section 8 authorizes Commerce to suspend or restrict development, deployment or internal use when it presents imminent catastrophic risk. The bill does not establish a general requirement to obtain government approval before each release. Hashim predicts stronger legislation but does not specify who would grant the approval he envisages.

Preemption is already contested. FRONTIER’s Section 9 would bar new state obligations concerning frontier-risk transparency, independent auditing and incident reporting, while preserving specified state powers. In September, 26 bipartisan state attorneys general sought an explicit prohibition on preempting state AI laws, according to Troutman Pepper Locke’s account of their letter. In July 2025, the Senate voted 99–1 to remove a proposed ten-year state-AI-law moratorium from the reconciliation bill. Our October 9 report covered Hashim’s account of competing Democratic plans, including Beyer and Jacobs’s proposed agency and Cantwell’s oversight framework. He then expected none to pass in 2026; his post-election forecast concerns passage in early 2027.

Hashim also thinks developer strict liability and data center moratoria could become viable. The latter already had a federal proposal: in his April 3 essay, he discussed Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s AI Data Center Moratorium Act, which would halt construction pending legislation addressing safety, economic distribution and utility costs. He then gave it “virtually zero chance of becoming law.” His November analysis raises the possibility of such measures without endorsing a specific bill or providing a vote count for them.

Mackenzie Arnold’s November 4 assessment similarly expects more ambitious bills, but emphasizes hearings and legislation formalizing the White House’s existing pre-vetting and incident-reporting arrangements. He expects broad preemption to fail; in a later reply, he clarifies that some preemption could still be negotiated. Miles Brundage, in the post Arnold quotes, expects stronger proposals but warns that emphasis on kill switches can displace safety standards and auditing. The investor newsletter Crack The Market offers a more pessimistic forecast: the filibuster and presidential veto make major legislation unlikely before 2029.

Hashim’s early-2027 timetable depends on a more receptive president. He speculates that the defeat could prompt Trump to replace advisers, while AI companies anticipating a possible Democratic presidency in 2028 could soften their opposition to regulation. Trump has made no commitment in Hashim’s account to sign the stronger bill he expects Congress to pass.
