Purported Anthropic draft protects whistleblowers while limiting enforcement

The unauthenticated discussion text pairs remedies for retaliation with restricted public access to submissions and civil penalties imposed only by courts.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 October 2026 869 words · ~4 min

A publicly circulating, unauthenticated discussion draft attributed to Anthropic would give whistleblowers substantial remedies while restricting ordinary enforcement to federal court. The same proposal was the subject of our October 16 coverage and October 17 analysis. Zvi Mowshowitz's October 16 post argued that it strengthened transparency and preemption while weakening enforcement relative to the FRONTIER Act. A closer reading of sections 9–11 adds detail about who could disclose problems, what the public could see and how violations would reach court.

Section 10 protects current and former employees and independent contractors at both frontier developers and qualified independent evaluators. Protection covers disclosures reasonably believed to reveal a violation of the proposed Act or a specific, substantial public-health or safety danger arising from its defined risks. Recipients can include regulators, law enforcement, Congress, supervisors and internal investigators. Employers could neither retaliate nor use contracts or policies to prevent those disclosures. The rights and remedies could not be waived, including through agreements requiring arbitration before the worker could seek statutory relief.

Large developers would also have to provide an anonymous internal reporting process supporting two-way communication. The discloser would receive monthly updates on the investigation and response. Officers and directors would receive reports at least quarterly, but an allegation against a particular officer or director would be withheld from that person. These are continuing duties to respond and escalate reports, beyond simply maintaining a confidential inbox.

An individual alleging retaliation or contractual restrictions could complain to the Labor Department. After 180 days without a final decision, provided the complainant had not caused the delay through bad faith, the individual could sue in federal court, with a right to a jury trial. Available remedies include reinstatement at the same seniority, twice the back pay owed with interest, compensatory damages and legal costs. A court could grant temporary relief on reasonable cause, considering the chilling effect on other workers; that relief would remain in effect during an appeal.

Those remedies closely parallel the bipartisan AI Whistleblower Protection Act, S. 1792, introduced by Senator Chuck Grassley and five colleagues in May 2025. Both texts use the same existing Labor Department complaint procedures, a 180-day route to court and doubled back pay. The scope differs: S. 1792 reaches AI-related violations of federal law and substantial dangers more broadly, whereas this draft ties protected disclosures to its own statutory violations or defined risks at frontier developers. The draft also specifies anonymous internal reporting and temporary injunctive relief.

Section 9 determines how much of the required disclosure reaches the public. Developers and evaluators could redact only as necessary for listed purposes, including trade secrets, privilege, privacy, customer confidentiality, cybersecurity and national security. A published version must describe and justify a redaction, but only to the extent the underlying concern permits. A large developer making redactions must retain the unredacted document for at least five years.

The government-facing record would be more extensive than the public one. Submitted information that the Act did not require to be published would be exempt from Freedom of Information Act disclosure. That exemption would preserve access for Congress and the Comptroller General. Section 9 also explicitly bars developers from using its protections to withhold information from their qualified evaluator. The text therefore provides for scrutiny by evaluators and congressional oversight while limiting what outside researchers and journalists could obtain.

Section 11 makes substantive failures enforceable alongside reporting failures: materially disregarding a developer's own safety framework, denying evaluators required access, failing to maintain required security programs and knowingly violating an emergency order are among its prohibited acts. Its good-faith defense is limited to reasonable statements covered by the false-statements provision. It does not excuse every breach of the Act.

Ordinary enforcement would belong exclusively to the Enforcement Authority, defined as the Department of Commerce although still shown in brackets throughout the operative provisions. It would have to bring a civil action; only a federal district court could impose penalties. The maximum for a first violation is the greater of a bracketed $1 million or an unspecified percentage of global annual revenue. After a final, non-appealable judgment, a later violation would carry the greater of a bracketed $3 million or another unspecified revenue percentage. Those unresolved percentages prevent a firm judgment about the fines' eventual scale.

The contrast with FRONTIER's introduced text is concrete. For violations of its section 4 framework and disclosure duties, FRONTIER allows penalties of up to $1 million per violation, counts each continuing day separately and lets opted-in state attorneys general sue, subject to federal notice and intervention rules. The purported Anthropic draft specifies no comparable daily accrual and gives state attorneys general no corresponding right to enforce its duties. It preserves separate private actions for whistleblower retaliation and contractual restrictions.

Section 11 would let a court compel compliance, but expressly denies it authority under that subsection to suspend a model's development, deployment or internal use. A model suspension under this Act would have to proceed through the separate section 12 process examined in our previous report. Employees could thus enforce their own protections against reprisals, while translating their warnings into ordinary enforcement against a developer would remain the responsibility of a single federal authority acting through courts.
