Claude finds quantum circuits that need less memory and computation

An automated checker helped the model redesign arithmetic for future quantum computers. Recovering earlier computational choices saved memory, while the most prominent result trades extra qubits for fewer costly gates.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 October 2026 874 words · ~4 min

Quantum circuits can save resources by reconstructing earlier computational choices from the numbers they leave behind. Andi Gu of Harvard, working through Anthropic’s STEM Fellows Program, and colleagues demonstrate this with Claude Mythos 5 in their October 15 arXiv paper “Discovering state-of-the-art quantum circuits with large language models.” An automated checker helped the model develop circuits for eight tasks in chemistry, optimization, numerical computation and number theory. Seven used both fewer qubits, which store quantum information, and fewer Toffoli gates, an expensive operation in error-corrected quantum computing, than their comparison baselines. Those baselines include one simple reversible implementation for a task with no published circuit. The eighth result offers different tradeoffs between memory and computation.

The authors concentrate on arithmetic routines that larger quantum algorithms repeatedly use. Quantum circuits must preserve the information needed to reverse their operations; temporary calculations cannot simply be overwritten and forgotten. Storing the intermediate results consumes qubits, while recomputing them consumes gates. Conditional operations create another expense: a circuit acting on a quantum superposition of possible inputs must accommodate different possible computational paths within one fixed sequence of gates. Improving an ordinary algorithm therefore requires accounting for its memory, branching and cleanup when translating it into a quantum circuit.

Gu and colleagues make that redesign searchable by asking Claude to write Python programs that construct circuits. Their OpenEvolve framework follows the approach of systems such as DeepMind’s AlphaEvolve, which combine generated code with automated evaluation. Each task begins with a human-written specification and a blank program. Several distinct algorithmic approaches develop in parallel, periodically sharing successful candidates. The evaluator checks sample inputs, including whether the final answer is correct, temporary qubits are cleared, and the phase, which controls quantum interference, is right. These particular circuits permit efficient classical simulation of those checks. Accepted designs are scored by multiplying their Toffoli count by their maximum simultaneous qubit use. Keeping alternatives with different resource tradeoffs lets a promising approach survive while it is developed.

The most substantial example concerns modular inversion, which makes division possible in arithmetic where numbers wrap around at a fixed limit. It is a costly part of point addition, which combines points on an elliptic curve and is repeated in quantum algorithms that could defeat elliptic-curve cryptography. Claude built on Daniel Bernstein and Bo-Yin Yang’s divsteps algorithm, which uses conditional additions, subtractions and halving. An ordinary implementation overwrites its state after each step. Making it reversible usually requires saving which operation happened, accumulating a long record.

The discovered circuit reconstructs most of that record from the current arithmetic state. According to Anthropic’s account, initial rules worked on small examples but failed on the full 256-bit task. Claude then derived mathematical relationships that made the recovery rule precise, checked it numerically and formalized it in Lean. The proof establishes that older computational history cannot obscure the bit identifying which operation was performed. The public repository includes the resulting proofs and circuits. Recovering a decision still costs computation, so the design saves decisions when reconstructing them would be expensive. Anthropic says the search took 53 hours, with human involvement limited to the initial brief and occasional instructions to explore more broadly. Researchers used those interventions because Claude tended to stay with familiar methods.

Putting the inversion inside a complete point-addition circuit produced the comparison highlighted by Anthropic: about one-seventeenth as many Toffoli operations as the prior design with the lowest qubit count in the paper’s comparison, at the cost of 12% more qubits. That prior design was by Han Luo and colleagues. Supplementary Table S3 also lists André Schrottenloher’s construction, which needs fewer Toffolis than Claude’s circuit but substantially more qubits. Schrottenloher’s design also has a lower product of gate count and qubit count. The paper accordingly gives no single improvement factor for this task: the preferred circuit depends on which resource is scarce.

A second example redesigns an error-correcting decoder used by Google’s decoded quantum interferometry algorithm, which converts certain optimization problems into decoding problems. Claude replaced the earlier division-based method with Berlekamp–Massey, a method whose regular sequence of updates better suits quantum circuits. It then changed the representation so that most overwritten values could be recovered without storing them. For the exceptional values that still needed storage, the circuit reserves space for one entry, accepting failure on the roughly one input in ten thousand that requires more. Anthropic reports that Claude simulated 403 million decodings to measure those requirements. Together, the changes reduced the decoder’s Toffoli count about twelvefold against Tanuj Khattar and colleagues’ previous construction.

These savings depend on specified limits on error. The point-addition design likewise permits rare failures, with four failures observed in 100,000 tests of the complete circuit. Sampled evaluation and proofs of particular mathematical steps provide different kinds of assurance; the Lean result does not prove every circuit correct on every input. The results are designs and resource estimates for future hardware, checked on ordinary computers. People chose the tasks, input distributions, scoring rule and acceptable errors. The authors also explain why their combined score cannot determine performance on a particular machine: execution depth, routing and the error-correction architecture can change the relative costs. Extending the search to those features would require evaluators that represent them.
