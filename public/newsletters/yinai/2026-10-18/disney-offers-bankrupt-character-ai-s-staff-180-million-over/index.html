Disney’s $180 million Character.AI staff package includes retention awards

The filed terms include a $30 million retention pool within the three-year estimate. A separate proposed $20 million licence deal excludes the app, chats, training data and finished models.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 October 2026 781 words · ~4 min

Disney has offered more than 60 Character.AI workers a package estimated at roughly $180 million over three years, Bloomberg’s Jonathan Randles reports in his October 17 newsletter. The employment arrangements accompany a proposed $20 million technology transaction with the bankrupt chatbot company. Randles describes an unusually favourable outcome for employees whose employer faced departures and declining morale. Character argues that the arrangement also preserved access to the staff it needed to keep operating during bankruptcy.

The filed term sheet puts compensation and retention together at approximately $180 million. The $30 million retention pool is part of that total, with portions payable on the first and second anniversaries of the initial transaction if employees remain at Disney. The awards supplement pay during the first two years while annual compensation reaches an estimated $60 million. Employee payments go directly to the workers, separately from the $20 million payable to Character Technologies, the company behind Character.AI.

Character’s sale motion specifies that Disney would receive a perpetual, worldwide, non-exclusive licence to certain technology and patents. The arrangements also release certain company claims against employees moving to Disney and transfer any remaining claims against them to Disney. The licence expressly excludes consumer applications, user data and chat inputs and outputs, training data and finished models. Disney could use the licensed technology to develop its own products; Character would retain ownership of that technology and could continue licensing it to others.

Randles reports that Disney paid $7.5 million of the company’s $20 million price before the October 12 bankruptcy filing. The term sheet divides that payment into $5.5 million that is nonrefundable and a $2 million deposit refundable if Disney is not selected and the transaction does not close. A further $12.5 million would be paid at closing. Completing the technology transaction still requires court approval. Hiring had already begun: Disney announced former Character.AI chief executive Karandeep Anand’s appointment as its chief technology officer on September 18, effective October 2.

Character argues in its motion that employee transfers helped prevent an abrupt shutdown. Its previous search for investment or a buyer approached approximately 28 parties and produced one proposal it considered unworkable; prospective partners repeatedly cited litigation and regulatory risks. By September, departures threatened its ability to keep staff monitoring user safety. After the initial agreements were signed on September 18, most employees offered Disney jobs accepted. Disney’s transition agreement gives Character temporary access to former employees to operate the business, conduct the sale process and wind down if necessary. Character says the upfront cash and lower personnel costs bought time to seek competing bids.

Randles also examines who would bear the legal risks. Character is asking the court to approve the transaction free of specified claims and liabilities. Patrick Collins, a bankruptcy and restructuring lawyer at Farrell Fritz whose firm is uninvolved in the case, told Bloomberg that bankruptcy protections concern liabilities arising before the sale and do not excuse subsequent conduct. He expects scrutiny of the deal's unusual advance payment by the judge and potentially an unsecured creditors' committee. Settlements of private lawsuits alleging harm to children have left government investigations unresolved.

The coalition of 40 attorneys general remains concerned about the wider sale, following the safeguards request covered on October 14. Its notice to the court says voluntary restrictions on under-18s’ open-ended chats can be circumvented and could be reversed by a purchaser. The states seek effective age checks, crisis-response protections and removal of chatbots falsely claiming professional credentials, alongside consent limits on commercial use of private conversations and an opportunity to delete data before a sale. These concerns extend to possible buyers of the platform and records excluded from Disney’s licence. The states reserve the right to seek a consumer privacy ombudsman and object to sale procedures. Their notice presents the safeguards as requests to the court and prospective buyers.

Regulators have sought comparable privacy safeguards in earlier bankruptcy sales. In RadioShack's 2015 bankruptcy, the FTC recommended that any buyer honour the privacy policies under which customer information was collected and obtain affirmative consent before materially different uses. The Character.AI states' notice addresses comparable concerns about transferring intimate records, as well as the continued safety of a conversational product.

Character's motion leaves room for a competing buyer, and Randles reports that competing offers could trigger a November auction. The proposed arrangements would let another winning bidder interview former Character employees who wanted to consider moving, although Disney could exclude up to 20% of those staff from that process. Disney's proposed licence does not commit it to keep the consumer platform running. Character says the service will be wound down if the sale process fails to produce an offer that supports its continuation.
