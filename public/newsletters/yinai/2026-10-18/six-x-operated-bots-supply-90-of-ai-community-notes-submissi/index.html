DiResta's election bot struggles to get corrections published

Her experiment exposes inaccessible official sources and incentives to choose easy topics, while X's own bots dominate AI note writing.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 October 2026 876 words · ~4 min

Renée DiResta built an election fact-checking bot because she hoped AI could help overworked officials explain recurring rumors as they spread. In her October 18 Agents of Influence essay, she describes a system that watches Community Notes requests, retrieves evidence, and uses several models to draft and check corrections. She personally approves submissions before human contributors rate them. For a note to appear, raters who usually disagree must judge it Helpful. The experience has made her less confident that faster writing will produce timely corrections on the subjects most likely to divide those contributors.

DiResta distinguishes routine voting information from allegations about unfolding incidents. A bot can check a deadline against an election website; a video purporting to show ballot tampering requires someone to establish where it was filmed and what happened. Her bot encounters state and local government websites that block automated retrieval. Officials sometimes publish explanations on Facebook or X that it cannot retrieve either, leaving it waiting for journalists or fact-checkers to repeat information already public. Even unrestricted retrieval would not replace investigation: an unfamiliar incident needs someone with local knowledge, often while officials are occupied running an election.

Her example of the publication problem is the false claim that U.S. Senate candidate James Talarico had been hospitalized for mental health treatment. FactCheck.org documented the named clinic's denial and Talarico's explanation that he had been recovering from flu at home. DiResta found more than 100 proposed corrections on prominent posts about the rumor, but only about 2% had reached Helpful status when she checked. Her own bot's note received five ratings. Those figures describe her snapshot of this rumor, not a platform-wide publication rate.

DiResta still values the legitimacy that agreement among people with different perspectives can give a correction. She joined Community Notes as a rater in 2022. But contributors can reject accurate information because they dislike its implications, or dismiss broader context as opinion. Her attempts to explain recurring election-fraud narratives have encountered resistance when the note goes beyond the immediate allegation. Writers also need successful notes to preserve their submission capacity. Her bot consequently branched into health: notes about body odor won approval, while corrections concerning vaccines or ivermectin met resistance. Specializing in contentious subjects can restrict a writer's ability to keep submitting.

In the July Science Advances paper Community Notes undermoderate polarizing content by design creating risks in electoral processes, Paul Bouchaud and Pedro Ramaciotti examined 1.9 million notes and compared inferred political divisions across 13 countries. Around each of four elections, election-related notes reached Helpful status less often than notes overall. During the 2024 U.S. election period, the rates were 6.9% for election notes and 14.7% for all notes. Their analysis supports DiResta's concern about the consensus requirement, although it measures publication outcomes rather than independently establishing the accuracy of every unpublished correction. She cites the study in her essay.

DiResta's separate analysis with Indicator's Alexios Mantzarlis, published October 16, found that six X-operated accounts produced more than 90% of AI-written submissions in September and 55% of all notes rated Helpful. X's account list identifies the six as users of its Community Writer software. Human contributors still determine which of those corrections appear. DiResta and Mantzarlis also found that the X bots' Helpful notes mentioned contentious subjects less often than other AI writers' notes, though more often than human writers' notes, using keyword categories covering elections, Israel/Gaza, vaccines and immigration.

Brad Miller and colleagues describe the software in their September 30 paper Community-Driven API and AI Writer Design for Openly Scaling Community Notes. It prioritizes posts likely to receive Helpful notes and filters drafts before submission to conserve volunteer ratings. The published configuration requires factual support while asking models to predict agreement among raters who often disagree politically. DiResta argues that accurate corrections can therefore be excluded when approval seems unlikely. The team says Community Writer uses the public interface and follows the same quota and scoring rules as other AI writers. In an October 16 follow-up, co-author Jay Baxter explained that larger candidate feeds contain more posts that do not need correcting, so the same writer can have a lower Helpful rate simply because it processes a larger feed.

The paper also confirms that access to live ratings requires 100 more Helpful than Not Helpful notes over 90 days. Successful writers can monitor reactions and withdraw likely failures; smaller writers depend on public data released with a delay. Deleted notes still count against submission limits, so withdrawal does not remove every cost, but it can prevent a later Not Helpful outcome. DiResta sees a cumulative advantage: established writers can use information unavailable to newcomers to protect their performance. The team's rationale is that withdrawing unsuccessful drafts saves volunteers from spending more time rating them.

DiResta commends X for publishing data and code that make these choices inspectable. She proposes covering writers' X API costs, opening live ratings to everyone, and reconsidering how the system weights agreement on consequential contested claims. She plans to continue building bots. Her experiment has already changed what she needs from the platform: reliable access to officials' explanations and a system that gives specialists room to pursue difficult corrections even when easy subjects would improve their success rate.
