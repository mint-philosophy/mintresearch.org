Binder disputes how human services would limit AI growth

Moll and Imas expect spending shifts and production delays to constrain measured GDP. Binder argues that an automated industrial sector could expand rapidly even while other work remains human.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 October 2026 928 words · ~5 min

Ben Moll and Alex Imas argue that rapidly improving AI is unlikely to produce annual GDP growth above 10% over the next 10 to 15 years. Brad DeLong’s October 18 crosspost recirculates their September essay, covered here on September 9. A substantive response arrived on October 14: Damon Binder disputes how much of the economy must be automated before growth accelerates. Their disagreement concerns both the transition to capable machines and whether to measure its consequences through GDP or physical production.

Moll and Imas present 4–5% growth as an illustrative alternative, explicitly declining to make a formal forecast. They agree that standard theory can produce much faster growth. In their technical supplement, machines perform an increasing share of tasks, and some output finances more machines. Replacing a fixed workforce weakens the diminishing returns that normally limit capital accumulation. An assumed transition from one-third of tasks being automatable in 2025 to all tasks in 2045 produces roughly 12.5% growth around 2035, even while technological improvement continues at its historical pace.

Their first objection concerns that assumed automation schedule. Firms must reorganize work, build and install physical equipment, and navigate opposition to changes that reduce wages or employment. Cognitive capabilities alone cannot replace work in construction, hospitals or mines. The authors expect those changes to take much longer than deploying software, even if AI itself improves quickly.

They place most weight on their second argument: spending may shift away from products made cheap by automation toward things that remain scarce. Some jobs combine tasks with local knowledge and accountability that firms cannot readily separate. Other services retain value because another person provides them, as with care or teaching. These are different reasons for work to remain human. Energy and construction can also restrict the expansion of the machines themselves.

Moll and Imas cite Diego Comin, Danial Lashkari and Martí Mestieri’s “Structural Change With Long-Run Income and Price Effects,” published in Econometrica in 2021. Using household and international data, the authors find that rising incomes explain much of the historical movement between economic sectors, alongside changing prices. The evidence supports Moll and Imas’s account of expenditure shifting toward services; it does not establish how strongly future consumers will prefer human providers when capable AI alternatives exist.

The essay’s third argument concerns demand and investment. If automation shifts income from workers to capital owners who consume a smaller share of their income, firms might see too few buyers to justify expanding capacity. Moll and Imas acknowledge limited direct evidence for this mechanism. Their fourth concern is destructive AI use: cyberattacks can reduce output and provoke restrictions that slow further deployment. Their fifth concerns automated research. Scientific work may retain human bottlenecks, and even faster discoveries require trials, factories or other physical investments before they increase production. The authors use these concerns to challenge different growth scenarios; accelerated research is not necessary for the double-digit result in their own baseline model.

Charles I. Jones and Christopher Tonetti’s May working paper “Past Automation and Future A.I.: How Weak Links Tame the Growth Explosion” supplies another part of their argument. It documents computers’ declining share of factor income and models production tasks as difficult to substitute for one another. Its substitution parameter of 0.2 is a modeling choice informed by earlier empirical estimates, not an estimate derived from the computer-income chart. The paper’s calculation that unlimited automation of one-third of GDP raises output by 11% holds other inputs fixed. Its later dynamic model separately considers expanding automation.

Binder argues that rapid industrial growth requires automating a narrower production chain: robots and the factories, mines and power plants that sustain them. Once that chain can reproduce itself, its output could expand while many consumer services remain human. He expects capable AI to help reorganize firms and spread useful knowledge, shortening adoption delays. His response asks Moll and Imas to separate when such machines arrive from how quickly an economy containing them could subsequently grow.

Binder’s underlying May analysis of industrial growth, revised in October, combines US input-output tables with capital stocks, depreciation and capacity-utilization data. It adds hypothetical robot and compute requirements, then calculates how quickly an economy concentrated on producing capital could reproduce itself. With a $30,000 robot body and allowances for resource costs, construction delays and consumption, one scenario doubles output in about 2.2 years. The calculation assumes the necessary automation already exists and industries have reached the modeled composition; it does not establish when today’s economy would reach either condition.

Binder also challenges the investment argument: capital owners consuming less can invest more, and high returns reward those who reinvest. His industrial calculation holds production methods fixed, separating growth through additional machines from growth through scientific discovery. He accepts catastrophic AI risks but doubts ordinary cyber damage would offset most of the gains in his assumed automated economy. On spending shifts, he acknowledges that measured GDP could grow more slowly than physical output, while questioning whether GDP would then capture the transformation that matters.

Moll and Imas’s published wager specifies a narrower test: US real GDP per person must rise at least 15% above its previous peak within a qualifying year through 2033 for the fast-growth side to win. Binder declines that framing partly because the deadline depends on when capable AI arrives. His reply offers a direct challenge to the proposed bottlenecks, but does not resolve whether rapidly multiplying industrial capacity would produce double-digit measured GDP growth within Moll and Imas’s stated horizon. Both sides allow that conventional GDP could miss substantial gains in what people can obtain.
