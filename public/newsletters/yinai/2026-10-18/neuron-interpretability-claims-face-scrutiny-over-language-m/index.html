Wong questions whether clearer neural networks would produce safer AI

The Atlantic’s account of the Cammarata debate examines the steps from explaining neurons to improving safety, and argues that companies need outside oversight to act on the findings.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 October 2026 634 words · ~3 min

Matteo Wong argues in his October 16 Atlantic feature “The Blog Post That Set the AI World on Fire” that understanding a model’s internals would still leave companies with decisions about how to make their products safe. Wong follows the reception of Nick Cammarata’s Goodfire essay “Neural Networks Are Already Clean,” which we covered on October 15. Wong examines the steps from explaining individual neurons to understanding a whole network, and from understanding it to changing its behavior. He argues that companies would need outside oversight to turn progress in research into dependable products.

Wong describes how quickly enthusiasm for Cammarata’s research became optimism about solving alignment. Sam Altman welcomed the essay; Adam Majmudar compared its significance with “Attention Is All You Need” and suggested that alignment might be solved within a year. Wong attributes some of the reaction to Cammarata’s history: he helped develop earlier interpretations that made networks seem difficult to understand, and now challenges those interpretations. The hope is that researchers could identify and change mechanisms responsible for dangerous behavior, including deception or autonomous hacking.

Wong questions whether the available evidence supports that expectation. Cammarata’s essay presents a research hypothesis, with a technical paper on language models still forthcoming; its most extensively examined examples come from vision models. Wong notes that these are generally simpler and better understood than the language models used in contemporary chatbots. He questions the inference from those examples to much broader claims about understanding and controlling frontier systems.

For Wong, even successful explanations of individual neurons would leave a further problem: predicting how the neurons interact. He illustrates this with physics, where understanding atoms and their interactions does not allow scientists to predict every event, and with social groups, where knowing each person does not tell you how everyone will get along. He draws on Neel Nanda’s appraisal, which welcomes practical neuron research while disputing the claim that ambitious reverse engineering is close to solved. Wong then makes a separate claim about safety: an explanation of a model’s behavior does not by itself show how to make that behavior more reliable.

Cammarata’s October 16 clarification, which Wong cites, qualifies the more expansive reception. He describes the essay as informal research, says he added citations after its rapid circulation, and reports encouraging early work on larger language models. He also describes unexplained responses and neurons that perform several functions, with much of the language-model investigation still incomplete. Explanations should be tested, he says, and excessive excitement can itself cause problems. He suggests the reaction partly reflects how little hope people have had about solving important problems in AI safety.

In a separate exchange, David Manheim challenges the inference from interpretability to alignment more directly. Changing a model’s internal activity still requires deciding what behavior is wanted, he argues; existing ways of steering models have left that broader problem unresolved. Cammarata agrees that understanding models does not obviously amount to a solution, while maintaining that it should help alignment. In his essay, Cammarata suggests that understanding a model could reveal harmful effects of a training environment, allowing researchers to remove that source of unwanted behavior.

Wong’s conclusion concerns who would undertake that work and who could require it. If Cammarata’s account proves right, he argues, companies would need to cooperate with outside auditors and governments to convert the research into safer products. He criticizes companies for acknowledging limited understanding of their systems while continuing to increase their capabilities, and contrasts the demands of careful safety work with their pursuit of enormous public valuations. Without substantial regulation, Wong argues, companies retain the discretion to decide whether and how to act. His criticism therefore survives even a successful technical result: improved access to model internals would leave decisions about deployment and accountability in the same hands.
