Maryland’s surveillance-pricing law relies on disclosure and state enforcement

Chapter 154 narrows its grocery ban through consent and loyalty exceptions. A wider disclosure rule still leaves enforcement to the state.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 7 August 2026 345 words · ~2 min

Maryland’s final Chapter 154, effective October 1, contains a second provision often lost in summaries focused on groceries. Food retailers with at least 15,000 square feet and third-party food delivery services may not use dynamic pricing or personal surveillance data to set higher prices for tax-exempt food. Other merchants can use algorithms or personal data to set prices if each advertised price carries the disclosure “THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM OR BY USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA.” Insurers, banks, and financial affiliates sit outside that disclosure rule.

The chapter’s exemptions leave substantial pricing activity untouched. Loyalty, membership, rewards, and subscription prices qualify; so does a price offered when a shopper consents to provide personal data in exchange. Violators receive notice and 45 days to cure before the Consumer Protection Division can act, and a completed cure forecloses enforcement. Consumers receive no private right of action under the two pricing sections. Detecting the data use behind an offer therefore falls largely to the state, even when a shopper can see only the resulting price or discount.

The Federal Trade Commission’s January 2025 market-study findings show what such an inquiry could examine. Documents from six pricing intermediaries described tools that can incorporate location, demographics, mouse movements, abandoned carts, and search or purchase activity. The firms served at least 250 clients across sectors including grocery, apparel, and hardware. Because the underlying submissions contained confidential business information, the FTC published hypothetical examples; regulators seeking a specific violation may need records from both sellers and their pricing vendors.

New Jersey and Connecticut enacted different remedies after Maryland. New Jersey’s Fair Price Protection Act, signed July 23, bars personal-data pricing for groceries while preserving loyalty discounts. It pauses new electronic shelf labels for one year while the New Jersey Innovation Authority studies their effects. Connecticut’s broader retail law covers retail sellers and third-party delivery services, while requiring an online disclosure for other businesses that increase a price using personal data. Each statute gives regulators a different route from a visible price to the data and algorithm behind it.
