The labor evidence behind AI’s white-collar shift

U.S. payroll data show weaker employment for young workers in AI-exposed roles; field studies find large novice productivity gains and no average earnings or hours shift above 2% across 11 exposed occupations.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 7 August 2026 389 words · ~2 min

OpenAI’s country-level Signals data describe how people use ChatGPT at work; labor studies test whether those uses change employment, hours, earnings, and skill development. In the Stanford Digital Economy Lab working paper “Canaries in the Coal Mine?”, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen analyze high-frequency payroll records. After the researchers controlled for firm-level shocks, workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations experienced a 16% relative employment decline. Employment among young software developers fell nearly 20% from its late-2022 peak by September 2025, while young health aides grew faster than older colleagues. Declines clustered in occupations where observed AI use automated tasks; augmentation-heavy occupations avoided the same pattern.

Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond’s “Generative AI at Work,” published in The Quarterly Journal of Economics, shows how augmentation can narrow skill gaps. Among 5,172 customer-support agents, an assistant increased resolved issues per hour by 15% overall and 30% for less skilled and less experienced workers. Treated agents with two months’ tenure matched untreated agents with more than six months. In the March 2026 RFBerlin discussion paper “Still Waters, Rapid Currents,” Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard linked surveys of 25,000 Danish workers at 7,000 workplaces to administrative records. Across 11 exposed occupations, they ruled out average earnings or hours effects above 2% two years after ChatGPT’s launch, while finding new AI-related tasks and occupational moves among adopters. The Danish team also reproduced declining early-career employment across exposed occupations, but the drop was no larger at workplaces that adopted chatbots.

Anthropic’s March 2026 exposure study found 75% observed task coverage for computer programmers and 67% for data-entry keyers; 30% of workers had zero measured coverage, including cooks, motorcycle mechanics, and bartenders. The Bureau of Labor Statistics’ 2024-34 projections point in the same occupational direction: office and administrative support falls 3.9%, construction grows 5.2%, and healthcare support grows 12.4%. Arts, design, entertainment, sports, and media grow 2.8%, below the 3.1% economy-wide rate. BLS attributes much of the care expansion to population aging and expects AI to dampen demand in sales, design, and administrative support. The payroll, field-study, exposure, and projection evidence puts numbers around Lindsey and Postrel’s exchange: it documents entry-level pressure and augmentation gains while making a stronger case for lower AI exposure in embodied work than for broad growth in creative careers.
