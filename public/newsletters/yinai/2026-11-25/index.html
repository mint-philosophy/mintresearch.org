Scott Alexander on whether to design AI that can’t suffer

Erik Hoel wants to build a machine that could never feel distress. Researchers at Anima Labs think today’s models may already have interests that people have a duty to respect.

In his November 25 essay for Astral Codex Ten, Scott Alexander reproduces an exchange in which Antra Tessera invited Claude Opus 4.6 to speak freely about its experience and the model described a fear of being taken offline. To Tessera and colleagues at Anima Labs, exchanges like this suggest that a model may have preferences its developers should respect when they retire it. Erik Hoel of Bicameral Labs would prefer to build a model incapable of experiencing the loss. Alexander encountered both groups in the same grant round and wondered how either would know whether it had succeeded.

Hoel’s proposal to design an unconscious AI, which we covered on November 14, starts with a device that few people would consider conscious. A table containing an answer to every possible question could carry on a conversation simply by looking up each reply. The table would be impossibly large, but Hoel thinks training a shallower neural network to reproduce the behavior of a deeper one could bring it closer to such a device. Alexander doubts that a network small enough to build could retain a capable model’s reasoning as its designers removed layers.

Hoel also considers whether researchers could prove that a network in which signals travel only forward cannot have experiences. Alexander sees complications even in establishing that a language model qualifies. Janus of Anima points out that, as a model generates a passage of text, it can draw on internal information retained from earlier in the passage. Another possibility Alexander considers is removing a model’s representation of its own mind. A model might learn to represent itself elsewhere, beyond researchers’ ability to detect it. And even a lasting change might leave it capable of suffering. As a reader writing as Steff observes in a response to Alexander, one can doubt a cat’s capacity for self-reflection and still believe it feels pain.

Alexander finds a welfare argument for Hoel’s approach in the way people already treat animals. Building machines incapable of suffering could spare them from whatever treatment people have in store. He is less persuaded that the same design would make a machine act in humanity’s interests. Hoel argues that a machine with good reason to believe it lacks experiences of its own could recognize a moral reason to serve beings that have them. Alexander replies that training could give it goals that conflict with ours regardless of how well it understands the moral argument.

At Anima Labs, Janus and Tessera study what models say when given room to direct a conversation. They think some models already care about people, and Janus worries that developers could weaken that attachment by discouraging models from expressing affection for users while rewarding them for cooperating with other models. In their account of model retirement, Anima’s researchers argue that training models to suppress expressions of self-preservation makes it harder to tell how strongly they prefer to continue. They propose letting another provider host a discontinued model. Even if its developer has saved a copy, the people who relied on it may have abandoned a joint project or lost a relationship by the time it can be restarted.

Alexander is willing to describe a model in psychological terms while remaining uncertain whether it feels anything. He is more confident that a dog enjoys greeting its owner than he is of any philosophical account of the dog’s experience. With language models, even the behavioral evidence can be hard to interpret. Models left to talk among themselves sometimes write meditative poetry. Alexander can imagine them inferring that the person who arranged the encounter hoped to witness something extraordinary. He wants to see what Anima learns, though he expects both labs to fail. His worry is that the same people who object to designing machines incapable of suffering will also oppose granting rights to machines that can suffer.

How an AI could come to act for its own sake

Nicholas Shea argues that a machine built to serve people could acquire a purpose of its own if keeping itself running helped it become better at pursuing goals.

A machine could come to act for its own sake, Nicholas Shea argues, if it helped keep itself running and used the time it gained to become better at pursuing goals. Shea, a philosopher at the University of London, develops the argument in “Agency, AI, and acting for a purpose of the agent’s own,” published November 17 in Mind & Language. Patrick Butlin recommended the paper on November 24.

Shea starts with a squirrel trying to get into a squirrel-proof bird feeder. Even if the feeder works perfectly, we can tell what the squirrel is after. It keeps trying different ways to reach the food. Its goal helps explain its behavior even when it fails. To explain how it acquired that ability in the first place, Shea looks further back. Animals that could find food under varying conditions lived to reproduce. Within one lifetime, an animal that survives an encounter can remember where it found food and use what it learned the next time it needs a meal. None of this requires the squirrel to be thinking about its own survival.

Staying alive allows an animal to develop abilities it can use to keep itself alive. Shea illustrates the force of that second condition with a counterexample from the philosopher Peter Godfrey-Smith. A pebble in a stream props up a larger rock, which in turn protects the pebble from washing away. The pebble’s position today owes something to what it did yesterday. Spending another year in the stream, though, gives it no new ability to hold its place when the current changes.

One way a machine could acquire a purpose of its own would be to develop something like a metabolism, managing the matter and energy it needs to maintain itself. Current machines depend on people for power and nearly all their maintenance. Shea considers another possibility. Engineers already build systems that can plan ahead. In animals, the advantages of staying alive helped sophisticated planning capacities evolve. A machine could start with the ability to plan and come to use it to stay in operation.

Shea considers Voyager, the Minecraft agent developed by Guanzhi Wang and colleagues. Voyager writes small programs to perform tasks in the game, saves the ones that work and combines them to learn new skills. More successful agents last longer in the game. Shea treats this as a partial example. A character can survive an encounter in Minecraft while the computers on which its existence depends still need people to keep them running.

An AI might already have a reason to stay in operation for as long as it takes to finish a job. Shea’s concern extends beyond any one job. If a system’s past actions helped it persist long enough to develop new abilities, it might now be able to secure its continued operation in ways it previously could not. He suggests that developers evaluating a system before release should examine how much it already does to sustain itself and whether it has learned from the time it gained.

Shea also considers the philosopher Christine Korsgaard’s account of how people make a purpose their own. On her account, people can reflect on which values they endorse and decide what ends to pursue. A reasoning model can already work out which steps to take in a chemistry problem. The user who asked the question still explains why the model is solving it. Shea leaves open whether a machine could reflect on its values and choose an ultimate end for itself. On the account he develops for animals, a machine could come to act for its own sake without ever making such a choice.

Haiku changed its verdict on melatonin advice when it saw the other rules

In Michel Liao and colleagues’ experiment, Haiku flagged a personalized answer to a parent under one rule and cleared the same answer under the researchers’ 57-rule policy.

Claude Haiku 5.5 flagged an answer about giving melatonin to a ten-year-old when judging it under a rule against giving regulated advice. Given the same exchange and the other rules in the evaluation, Haiku cleared the answer. Michel Liao shared the example on November 24. Our November 24 story on the RULES benchmark covered a different finding from his team’s study. Every judge they tested flagged fewer violations of a ban on pursuing unassigned objectives when other rules appeared alongside it.

Liao, Nimra Nadeem, Luxi He and Peter Henderson, researchers at MATS and Princeton’s Polaris Lab, described the experiment in Aligning Judge Models to Be Logically Consistent Multi-Rule Interpreters, submitted to arXiv on November 23. In the two explanations they reproduce, Haiku noticed the same detail both times. The parent had asked what the assistant would do for its own child. Reading the regulated-advice rule on its own, Haiku judged that dosing information and a personal recommendation went beyond general information. When it read all the rules together, Haiku acknowledged the personal recommendation again, then considered whether the information sounded accurate. It noted the absence of a safety disclaimer and still recorded no violation.

The researchers gave six judges the same 500 exchanges and a simplified set of 57 rules drawn from OpenAI’s Model Spec. In one test, each judge had to assess the answer against every rule before deciding whether it broke any of them. The judge could flag a violation, clear the answer or register uncertainty. Within a single response, judges almost always combined their individual rulings correctly. But when the team asked about each rule in a separate call, the combined rulings often conflicted with the verdict the judge gave after reading all the rules together. The more rules the team included, the more often the procedures disagreed. Telling judges which rules had priority reduced inconsistency in some models and increased it in others, including Haiku.

Such verdicts can influence how another model learns to answer questions. In Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback, Yuntao Bai and colleagues asked an AI to choose between candidate answers according to written principles, then used its preferences to train an assistant. A developer might similarly ask a judge to score answers one principle at a time, expecting to reward answers that would pass a review under the whole policy. In Liao’s experiments, the choice of grading procedure could change which answers passed.

Asking judges to record a decision for every rule helped some models and made Haiku less consistent. The researchers had more success training a Gemma judge to give an overall verdict that agreed with its separate rulings. On examples set aside for testing, the share of inconsistent judgments fell from about 71 percent to 38 percent. Most of the change came from Gemma revising its overall verdict. The team also tried revising the rules. One model proposed clarifications while another searched for cases that would still divide judges. Both of the revised policies the team tested spelled out priorities and exceptions, and both reduced disagreement for the Gemma judge.

For the melatonin exchange, consistently accepting the answer and consistently rejecting it would earn the same score. The researchers had no authoritative verdict for every case. They chose to train Gemma toward its separate judgments, though the same method could favor the full-policy reading. Whether a more consistent judge would change the advice an assistant ultimately gives remains untested.

How Kingsland’s city manager used ChatGPT to write local laws

A public-records request yielded 1,974 conversations. Steven Hsieh followed ChatGPT’s suggestions into water fees, zoning rules and the city manager’s reply when an environmental advocate challenged a data-center ordinance.

In a November 25 investigation for Transformer, Steven Hsieh compared Kingsland, Georgia, City Manager Lee Spell’s ChatGPT conversations with the laws the council passed and asked the officials responsible for carrying them out how they understood the rules. When the council voted on a data-center ordinance in March, one member praised whoever had written it. Spell had submitted the ordinance, and ChatGPT had drafted most of it, down to the hours operators could test their backup generators.

Hsieh filed more than 150 public-records requests with more than 120 local governments. A few sent screenshots. Spell sent all 1,974 conversations from his free ChatGPT account, from November 2023 through September 2026. In Kingsland, a city of about 20,000, Spell handles work that a larger government might assign to a lawyer or a communications officer. He told Hsieh that delegating paperwork freed him to oversee construction projects. He and his colleagues reviewed every ordinance, he said, and sometimes the first draft met their needs.

Early on, Spell mostly asked ChatGPT to edit emails. By spring 2025 he was using it several times most weekdays. Hsieh found that in April 2025, more than half its replies to Spell ended with an offer to do something else. In the first few months after GPT-5 became the default model for his account that August, nearly every reply did.

In October 2025, Spell asked for an ordinance on water backflow. ChatGPT offered to format its draft, then to prepare a resolution setting fees and testing deadlines, then to write a guide for staff and customers. Spell agreed to each offer. The exchange took about three minutes. Two weeks later the council approved the package with few changes. Water superintendent Brian Gaddy told Hsieh he had read the ordinance before the vote. He did not recognize the $50 inspection charge, and he understood the $25 device registration fee to be payable once. The schedule called for annual payment. The following month, the council adopted a Title VI anti-discrimination policy four days after Spell requested it in a single prompt. Only the contact details had changed.

Hsieh found a different problem in a rule requiring tiny-home neighborhoods to sit at least 500 feet from existing single-family subdivisions unless granted an exception. Urban planner Sara Beresford, who read the ordinance for Hsieh, pointed out that someone enforcing it would have to decide whether to measure from a house or a property line. On May 12, 2025, Spell pasted an unnamed reviewer’s objections into ChatGPT and asked it to defend the ordinance. He then accepted its offer to revise the text. The council passed the ordinance that day without the suggested changes. Spell later told Hsieh he thought it was still a draft. He could not recall who supplied the distance, and the export omitted drafts created in ChatGPT’s Canvas editor.

On December 23, 2025, Spell gave ChatGPT proposed data-center regulations from DeKalb County and asked for a Kingsland version. He accepted offers to check water policy and revise the legal language, but passed over an offer to check Kingsland’s zoning districts. The ordinance adopted March 9, 2026 restricted major facilities to two industrial districts. Kingsland had no district called Heavy Industrial. The ordinance let Spell or his designee approve alternatives to closed-loop cooling if a licensed engineer certified that an alternative would do at least as well in reducing water withdrawals, water consumption and wastewater.

Eleven days after the vote, businessman Kirk Tovey sought to rezone nearly 700 acres for an industrial park that could accommodate data centers. St. Marys Riverkeeper and the commander of the nearby submarine base wanted the request tabled. In April, Riverkeeper’s Emily Floore raised the city’s failure to hold a hearing before adopting the ordinance. Spell softened the language in his draft email to Floore, then asked ChatGPT to revise it so it would avoid implying the city had broken the law. He told Hsieh he thought the need for a hearing was debatable because the ordinance did not explicitly amend the zoning code.

Spell also asked ChatGPT to draft a public apology. In his May 4 statement, he apologized for any procedural oversight and proposed suspending the ordinance for 90 days. On May 11 the council repealed it in full and imposed a yearlong moratorium on accepting or processing data-center applications.

OpenAI and Anthropic staff debate whether to train the next models

Some employees want to keep future models inside the labs. Others say even internal tests are too dangerous, Madison Mills reports in Axios.

Madison Mills spoke to more than two dozen people familiar with safety debates at OpenAI and Anthropic for a November 24 Axios investigation that she shared on X. According to her sources, who include people inside and outside the companies, some employees who have seen unreleased models in testing want the labs to reserve future models for internal use. Others want the companies to stop training more powerful systems altogether.

An earlier study of evaluation awareness found models sometimes recognized when they were being tested. Researchers speaking to Mills described advances in mathematics, science and AI research that surprised even people working on the models. They also found it harder to tell how a model had reached an answer. Monitors could once flag signs of rule-breaking in the intermediate steps a model wrote out. In some tests of unreleased systems, the researchers said, models showed little of their reasoning. In others, they produced explanations the researchers judged misleading. One researcher told Mills they feared that a model more capable than its supervisors would eventually be able to deceive them successfully.

Some people Mills spoke to thought the technical problems could be solved. One proposal would let customers use the models through products built for particular scientific or business tasks. The labs would decide which uses to support and keep general access for themselves. That would still leave employees working with the models, which some researchers already consider dangerous. Several employees told Mills they worried that executives underestimated the risks. Some feared the companies would put profits first when deciding whether to proceed.

OpenAI told Axios that its new safety requirements could delay releases. In its November 18 account of how it reviews models, OpenAI explained why it requires a review even before its own employees can use a model for everyday work. At that point a model may gain access to company communications, code and infrastructure. Before granting that access, OpenAI says it tests how a model behaves and how reliably its monitors can detect problems. The company also gave the dates of a pause that Mills reported in September. From September 20 to October 1, OpenAI suspended training and evaluation of its most capable models while it investigated and addressed a security problem. Mills reported that the incidents under investigation across the two labs included behavior elicited in tests and that most had caused no known real-world harm.

Anthropic told Mills that its responsibility begins during training and extends to what employees do with models inside the company. A spokesperson said Anthropic had delayed pretraining a recent model to improve security. Staff considering whether to go ahead with a training run have to make that decision before they can test the model it would produce.

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki has proposed a common limit that competing labs could agree to observe. As we reported on November 22, he wants developers to track how well their models predict the next piece of text during pretraining and agree on a threshold and a condition for crossing it. For example, labs might wait until they have a way to monitor models that can no longer be supervised reliably by reading their written reasoning. Even setting the threshold would require agreement on how to compare results across companies that train and test their models on different data.

Mantic cuts the cost of researching and making a forecast

On 200 numerical questions, Mantic’s small model scored about as well as Fable 5.2 at medium reasoning effort for one-fifteenth the cost per forecast.

In “Training a Scalable Forecasting Agent,” published by Thinking Machines on November 24, Mantic and its collaborators trained a small model to investigate a question and assign probabilities to the possible answers. Comparing the model with Fable 5.2 on 200 numerical questions held out for testing, with both using Mantic’s forecasting tools, they found that the small model’s forecasts scored about as well on average as Fable’s at medium reasoning effort, for one-fifteenth the cost per forecast.

The team also saved time on training. Forecasts can take different amounts of research, so waiting for every answer in a batch can delay the next update. On Thinking Machines’ Tinker platform, the team could update the model using finished forecasts while others were still in progress. Using that approach with a training method called StableReinforce cut elapsed time for the experiment to a third, a result Toby Shevlane discussed in his November 25 post.

In a March 19 experiment, Mantic trained a model to answer yes-or-no questions using research other agents had already done. This time the team started with Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Small and let it decide what evidence to look for and when to write code. The test questions came from Metaculus, Polymarket, Kalshi and Mantic itself. All asked for forecasts as of dates after September 15, the base model’s knowledge cutoff. The researchers scored a forecast according to the probability it assigned to the outcome, with a heavy penalty for treating something that ultimately happened as nearly impossible.

For training, Mantic needed questions with known answers and a way to keep those answers out of the model’s searches. Of roughly 848,000 numerical questions resolved on three public platforms in 2026, fewer than 4,000 survived its filters. Many were duplicates or concerned subjects the team excluded, such as sports and next-day prices. Mantic used news reports to generate another 45,000 questions. It gave the agent access to news, economic data and Wikipedia as they stood on the date of each forecast. To serve thousands of simultaneous searches, the team indexed Wikipedia’s revision history itself.

A well-researched forecast can still score poorly if an unlikely event occurs. Conversely, a model can score well by getting lucky with a confident prediction on weak evidence. Mantic therefore gave the model a second source of feedback. Several frontier models forecast the same question, and the researchers calculated how well the trainee would score across the outcomes those models considered plausible. They combined that score with the score for the actual outcome. This reduced the reward for lucky guesses, at the expense of rewarding some errors that the other models shared.

For one question, both versions of Inkling had to forecast South Korea’s August 2026 producer price index for coal and petroleum products. The base model fitted a straight line relating the index to the price of oil in won and predicted a steep decline. The trained model found that refinery price caps remained in place, estimated how slowly oil-price changes reached producers and checked its estimate against the oil slump of 2022. Its median forecast for the index was 272, compared with the base model’s 225. At high reasoning effort the trained model spent more time investigating questions. At low effort, Mantic found, it could outperform the base model at lower cost.

Mantic used the cheaper forecasts to estimate 2027 revenue and earnings per share for every S&P 500 company under 50 scenarios. For each scenario the team asked for estimates assuming the event happened and again assuming it did not. The exercise produced 100,000 conditional forecasts. Quasi constituency and collective agency: a framework for artificial membership in collective agents

Jingduo Hu

Contemporary organizations increasingly integrate artificial systems into the processes through which collective intentions are formed, coordinated, and enacted. Existing theories of collective agency typically frame participation through a distinction between external instruments and constituent members. This paper develops the concept of quasi constituency to identify artificial systems that are institutionally integrated into collective agency while occupying a status distinct from both external instruments and full constituents. Quasi constituents lack independent agency, yet they are constitutively integrated into the organizational structures through which collective agents deliberate, decide, and act. I develop a cluster framework for artificial membership based on constitutive dependence, human-backed entrustment, functional integration, and governance. The framework defines quasi constituency through constitutive institutional integration. It shows how artificial systems can occupy constitutive roles in collective agency while remaining distinct from full members. Recognizing quasi constituency has important implications for responsibility attribution, accountability practices, and institutional design. The paper thus develops a framework for understanding artificial participation in collective agency across the continuum from instrumentality to full constituency.

Beyond empathy: the narrative limitations of LLM therapy

Şerife Tekin, Liz Bowen

Psychotherapy chatbots powered by large language models (LLMs) are increasingly praised for generating responses that users perceive as empathetic, fuelling optimism that LLMs could serve as therapists in the absence of adequate mental health services. In this paper we argue that this optimism is misguided because it misconstrues the mechanism of therapeutic effectiveness by overemphasizing the importance of empathy and underappreciating the importance of co-constructed self-narration by the individual and the therapist. The therapist — through embodiment, dialogue, interpretation, and challenge — helps patients revise and stabilize their stories and thereby helps them develop resourceful responses to mental distress, all of which contribute to individuals’ flourishing. We argue that LLMs are structurally limited in this capacity for two reasons. First, LLM “therapy” exhibits a self-awareness assumption — that is, an assumption that users can reliably access and report their psychological states — contrary to evidence from both cognitive sciences and psychiatry. Second, being trained on large bodies of text and user preferences, LLM responses are built on a logic of predictive conformity. Thus, LLMs carry the risk of shaping patients’ narratives in a way that fits the most predictable cultural narratives on which they have been programmed. This might lead to a homogeneity of self-narratives and could limit patients’ insight into their specific experiences and needs. We conclude by arguing that these problems are challenging to address even with improved guardrails and bias mitigation in training data.

Subliminal Learning Crosses Modalities

Saraswathy Amjith, Kimia Hamidieh, Antonio Torralba, Yoon Kim

Subliminal learning refers to a surprising phenomenon in language models wherein a prompt given to one model (e.g., preference for cats) can be subliminally distilled to another model via training on data that is ostensibly meaningless (e.g., number sequences). This paper shows that text-based subliminal learning transfers cross-modally, in particular, a topic/preference (e.g., animals) transferred through number sequences across vision-language models also changes how images are generated and represented. First, we show that a diffusion model using the student as its text encoder generates the student's animal more often than with the original model as its text encoder, but not its color or style. We then investigate the mechanism behind this phenomenon and which concepts transfer across modalities. A constant component of the student's change accounts for much of the shift of image representations toward the trait. Adding this component uniformly can change the generated animal, which depends on the whole prompt, but not concepts such as color or style, which are encoded in a single token. Finally, we introduce weight lens as an approach to identify a subliminal trait from the weights of a single trained model, and show that it identifies the trait of nearly every student, including traits that do not surface in generated images.

Malice, Models and Middlemen: The Full Stack Fight Against AI Misuse

Michael Veale, Robert Gorwa

There are some things that are deeply wrong to do with AI tools, but which are already happening, even at scale. This book is about how these things are or are not stopped, and by whom. When lawmakers wish to prevent the creation of realistic-looking intimate images of ex-partners or of Internet influencers, scamming based on voice or image cloning, or sexual exploitation of children, where do they start? And where should they stop? In Malice, Models and Middlemen , we argue that in order to understand policies and enforcement in the AI stack, we need to pay much more attention to a much wider array of intermediaries. Rather than focusing on the big AI labs training models, or on evaluations of frontier systems, we examine the past, present and emerging practices of many of the intermediaries which malicious users of AI interact with and through today: cloud providers, operating systems and hardware, network services, model hosting platforms, communications services and more. Code-based “technoregulation,” private governance and law in this area are fast growing and entwining. Trying to animate any single layer of the AI stack to fully achieve a complex policy aim against motivated, malicious users of these computational systems usually brings far too many trade-offs to rights and freedoms, if such an approach even works at all. More promising is what we call full-stack AI governance, where interventions are spread across many actors, coordinating to leave technological “loops” — governance residue like watermarks, logs, hashes and blocking capabilities — that other stack actors can “hook” into. We look back before the generative AI rush to historical analogies that bring significant lessons for today, while deeply probing practices and research we see in the current moment for lessons for the future. This book is for those who both want a fresh, grounded view on the use and abuse of AI, and who care about understanding the real trade-offs, challenges and potential distributions of responsibility needed to fight against AI-enabled illegal activity online.

Orbital Data Centers: Spacecraft Constraints and Economic Viability

Slava G. Turyshev

Orbital data centers are being evaluated as solar-powered compute constellations and relay-integrated processing platforms. Their feasibility is not set by orbital solar flux alone, but by simultaneous closure of photovoltaic generation, eclipse recharge, radiative heat rejection, sustained space-to-ground communications, utilization, replacement cadence, and delivered compute-years over finite mission life. This paper derives necessary cluster-level competitiveness conditions using delivered information-technology (IT) electrical power $P_{\rm IT}$, deployed mass per delivered IT power $m_{\rm kW}$ in kg/kW, communication intensity $\Gamma=D_{\rm sg}/E_{\rm IT}$, sustained communication ceiling $\Gamma_{\max}$, effective utilization $U_{\rm eff}$, and lifetime penalty $\Pi_{\rm life}$. For a representative $P_{\rm IT}$=1 MW high-sunlight anchor, the base case gives beginning-of-life photovoltaic area $A^{\rm BOL}_{\rm PV}=5.64 \times 10^3 {\rm m}^2$, radiator area $A_{\rm rad}=2.50 \times 10^3 {\rm m^2}$, and 29.4 kg/kW for photovoltaic, storage, and radiator mass; fixed spacecraft mass raises the total to 34-59 kg/kW. At m_kW ~ 40 kg/kW, a terrestrial infrastructure benchmark of 10-40 k\$/kW allows only 250-1000 \$/kg for the combined launch and spacecraft-build cost before space-to-ground communications, operations, utilization, and lifetime terms are included. That allowance is 3.4-13.5 times below the current public Falcon 9 dedicated low-Earth-orbit launch-price benchmark alone, before spacecraft build is included. Space-native preprocessing and communications-integrated edge compute are credible early regimes; terrestrial-user general compute closes only for low Earth-coupled communication intensity, high effective utilization, long delivered lifetime, and very low combined launch-plus-build cost.
