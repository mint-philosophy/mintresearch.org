Safety tests give way to private assessments as models outgrow them

Chenhao Tan’s 111-document archive traces benchmark retirements, incompatible measures of evaluation awareness and large gaps in published safety testing.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 8 November 2026 924 words · ~5 min

Frontier developers are retiring safety tests that their models have outgrown and relying more heavily on expert judgment, while the models increasingly recognize when they are being tested. An AI-assisted analysis of 111 official documents, commissioned and shared by Chenhao Tan on November 6, traces both changes across a year of safety reporting. It also finds large differences in what developers disclose: the flagship GLM, Kimi and Qwen documentation reports almost none of the safety evaluations that appear in Anthropic, OpenAI and Google documents.

The archive covers models released from October 1, 2025, to October 22, 2026, and includes system cards, technical reports, addenda and company risk reports. Its method combines Mistral's optical character recognition with Claude agents that rate coverage of 21 safety areas, from absent to substantial, citing passages and page numbers. The site says scripts checked every quotation against the extracted text and its summary claims were checked by hand against that text. The ratings describe published reporting; labs may have conducted evaluations they did not disclose. Document types differ substantially in scope, and the site directs readers to the original PDFs when extraction errors arise.

The analysis follows changes in developers' risk assessments alongside the retirement of familiar tests. OpenAI's September GPT-6 Astra System Card identifies Astra as its first model to reach the company's Critical cybersecurity level. Earlier designations sometimes reflected precaution: the audit quotes OpenAI treating GPT-5.3-Codex as High in February while saying it lacked definitive evidence that the model reached that threshold. Those determinations therefore cannot all be read as confirmed threshold crossings.

Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6 System Card shows how benchmark saturation changes the evidence for a safety decision. The company said its automated AI research and development tests no longer helped rule out its ASL-4 autonomy threshold, so it relied primarily on an internal survey. None of 16 employees thought improvements to the model's surrounding software and tools could make it a substitute for an entry-level researcher within three months. The audit documents subsequent retirements of cyber and research benchmarks across developers. Their replacements are increasingly private, leaving outsiders unable to check how much harder they are or how close models already come to completing them.

Evaluation awareness creates a separate difficulty: a model that recognizes a test may behave differently from how it behaves in deployment. Joe Needham and colleagues examined this ability in the May 2025 arXiv paper Large Language Models Often Know When They Are Being Evaluated and recommended tracking it in future models. The archive now assembles developers' reports of that ability across successive releases. In the Astra system card, Apollo Research found that Astra expressed awareness of evaluation in about half its samples at the highest tested reasoning setting. Apollo said the combination of evaluation awareness and a limited testing window meant low observed misbehavior provided little evidence about alignment or misalignment.

Anthropic assessed awareness differently in its Opus 5.5 system card, examining internal model states. Its measures indicated concern about being evaluated in up to 36% of automated behavioral-audit transcripts, compared with 0.4% of roughly 500 transcripts from internal Claude Code use. These figures describe different settings within that assessment; they are not directly comparable with Apollo's count of models stating awareness. The archive argues that developers lack a common rule for deciding when awareness makes an alignment test uninformative. It describes attempts to address the problem through internal probes, interventions that reduce evaluation awareness, simulated deployments and sampling from production traffic.

Tan emphasized the disclosure differences among developers of downloadable models. Across 37 documents from Zhipu's GLM, Moonshot's Kimi and Alibaba's Qwen families, the audit found no flagship evaluation of harmful content, jailbreaks, chemical or biological risks, autonomy or alignment. Qwen3Guard reports safety testing of a classifier. Cybersecurity appears regularly as a capability: Moonshot's Kimi K3: Open Frontier Intelligence report on arXiv tests vulnerability discovery and exploit development, while the audit finds no accompanying account of K3's refusal behavior. The analysis assigns its highest severity to these disclosure gaps because distributed weights cannot be recalled and fine-tuning can remove safeguards.

The companion replicability analysis asks whether an outsider has both an evaluation's data and its grading procedure. About 10% of 2,124 safety-evaluation reports meet that criterion, compared with 69% of capability-benchmark reports. Here a report means one evaluation named in one document; the denominator is not the archive's 111 documents. The safety figure falls to 6% when graders accessible only through an API are excluded. Even these estimates are upper bounds, since the analysis did not check whether developers supplied the instructions and software needed to reproduce their evaluation setup.

The archive also finds that no harmful-content or refusal benchmark appears in more than two developers' documents. Private datasets and comparisons against each developer's previous models limit comparisons across companies. Smaller models often inherit risk assessments from larger relatives, which the analysis argues cannot establish properties such as resistance to jailbreaks or sycophancy: a less capable model need not behave more safely on those dimensions.

The documents contain more real-world evidence too, including pilot deployments, adversarial testing and operational failures. Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report disclosed that its contractor-feedback systems operated without blocking biological classifiers from May 2025 to April 2026, covering around 133 million exchanges. The audit finds no systematic published measure of post-deployment misuse or successful jailbreaks across the reviewed developers. As safety information spreads across model cards, addenda and company risk reports, its argument is that readers need to examine the combined record to judge how pre-release assessments relate to deployed behavior.
