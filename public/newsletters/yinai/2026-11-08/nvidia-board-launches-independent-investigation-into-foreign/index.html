NVIDIA's inquiry examines the intelligence allegations surrounding Huang

Independent directors and outside counsel will oversee the review. The Times describes an alleged Chinese effort to gather chip intelligence and influence NVIDIA's advocacy; Huang has promised to cooperate.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 8 November 2026 982 words · ~5 min

NVIDIA's board has put independent directors and outside independent counsel in charge of the investigation announced on November 8 into reports that foreign intelligence targeted Jensen Huang. The November 6 New York Times investigation describes an alleged Chinese effort to gather information about American AI chips and influence NVIDIA's advocacy for sales to China. Our November 7 issue noted the inquiry; the board's statement and the intelligence reporting explain who will oversee it and what prompted it.

The board says it will examine all available information, that Huang supports the review and has committed to cooperating, and that it retains confidence in him and his leadership team. It says it has “no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised.” That is the board's assessment as it opens the review. The statement puts US national and economic security ahead of NVIDIA's and shareholders' interests, but names neither the committee members nor the outside counsel and gives no timetable. In the Times' November 8 report, updated November 9, NVIDIA spokesman John Rizzo says Huang will have no part in choosing counsel. Huang will continue to run the company during the investigation.

Huang's own November 8 email to employees acknowledges infidelity, apologizes to his family and colleagues, and promises full cooperation with the inquiry into the national-security concerns. He accepts responsibility and asks for his family's privacy. The email does not identify his partner; the Times reports that he acknowledged to the board that he had been intimate with Alice Huang, an executive at NVIDIA customer Megaspeed.

In their November 6 investigation, Julian E. Barnes, Tripp Mickle, Paul Mozur and Maggie Haberman report that a memo circulated among senior Trump administration policymakers in spring 2026 cited intelligence identifying Alice Huang as an asset of China's Ministry of State Security. People briefed on the intelligence told the Times that the agency recruited her to gather information on Jensen Huang. They described two objectives: learning about American AI chips and potentially influencing NVIDIA to press for its most advanced chips to become available to China. The newspaper attributes those objectives and the intelligence assessment to its sources; it does not report that the operation changed US policy or that Jensen Huang knowingly supplied information.

The Times traces Alice Huang's relationships beyond NVIDIA, including with leaders of Gigabyte, which assembles NVIDIA supercomputers. It describes a financial link between her network and a cybersecurity subsidiary of China Electronics Technology Group, a military-technology manufacturer. Megaspeed builds data centers in Southeast Asia and rents their computing power to Alibaba. The reporting therefore concerns access to chip-industry executives and technology as well as export-policy advocacy. Rizzo told the Times that NVIDIA shares no classified information with customers and that customers do not ask it to lobby Washington on China chip policy. China's embassy rejected the allegations as smears; Alice Huang did not respond.

The Times' October 29 report had described the intelligence concern and said Trump was briefed roughly a year earlier without acting. According to people briefed on the surveillance, Jensen Huang had received no defensive briefing, the warning officials sometimes give Americans targeted by foreign intelligence. Officials initially believed he was unaware of Alice Huang's alleged role; some later doubted that he could have remained unaware. In a November 8 follow-up, the Times reported that Marco Rubio oversaw preparation of the spring memo before Trump's May trip to China. Trump said on November 8 that he did not regret his inaction and supported Huang remaining CEO.

Congress had already sought answers in a November 4 letter, released November 5. Brian Mast, John Moolenaar and Rick Crawford, who chair the House Foreign Affairs, China Select and Intelligence committees, asked Huang to attend a closed-door briefing on November 18. They want to examine NVIDIA's defenses against foreign espionage and his interactions with Alice Huang, and asked him to accept or propose another date by November 11. The Times' updated account also reports Michael McCaul's call for an immediate pause on advanced AI chip shipments to China and Elizabeth Warren's demand for answers from Huang and the board.

Megaspeed was under scrutiny before the intelligence reports. Bloomberg's December 2025 investigation, based partly on Malaysian and Indonesian customs records, found that it had imported NVIDIA hardware worth at least $4.6 billion since its founding in 2023. Bloomberg reported a US investigation into its ownership and possible smuggling, but found no evidence that its chips had been diverted to China or that it had violated export controls. Megaspeed denied wrongdoing.

In a later development on November 9, Ana Swanson and Devlin Barrett reported that NVIDIA's general counsel, Tim Teter, emailed Commerce Department investigators about the Megaspeed case in August 2025, saying Jeff Kessler, who runs the Bureau of Industry and Security, had permitted the contact. Commerce disclosed the incident to partner agencies and paused its work at the Justice Department's request; Justice is conducting a criminal inquiry into Megaspeed. A Commerce official said Kessler instructed staff to handle the case by the book. Rizzo said NVIDIA consults officials to answer questions and seek guidance. Other agencies had proposed adding Megaspeed to Commerce's export blacklist, the Times reported, without Commerce acting. The board's November 8 statement predates this report and does not specify whether its inquiry will examine Teter's contact.

Commentary published November 9 separates the policy dispute from Huang's conduct. At Stratechery, Ben Thompson argues that NVIDIA has a defensible commercial reason to sell chips to China: preserving customers' reliance on its CUDA software and reducing demand for Chinese alternatives. He rejects the inference that the affair explains Huang's position on export controls. At Transformer, Shakeel Hashim argues that Huang should resign because his public account of the relationship misled employees and investors, regardless of what the security investigation establishes. Hashim's earlier November 8 post had questioned the origins of NVIDIA's export-control rhetoric.
