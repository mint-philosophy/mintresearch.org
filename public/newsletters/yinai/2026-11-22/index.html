OpenAI’s chief scientist proposes shared limits on model training

Jakub Pachocki wants labs to use their models’ scores on a prediction test to decide when training should wait for better safeguards. Microsoft has reported a score on the same test.

OpenAI researchers had been giving their models unsolved mathematics problems for more than a year. In “Perplexity and Intelligence,” OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki recalls watching solutions to longstanding questions arrive one after another in a Slack thread. The researchers had switched to a model that scored about 0.76 on a separate test of how well it predicted the next fragment of computer code. Pachocki wants labs to agree on limits based on that kind of measurement and on what they must learn about their models before crossing each limit.

OpenAI tests its base models on a fixed sample of its own code withheld from training. Given the code up to a particular point, the model assigns probabilities to the tokens, or pieces of text, that could come next. A model that consistently gives the token that follows a high probability earns a low score. OpenAI reports the average negative logarithm of those probabilities, called cross-entropy loss or log-perplexity. The lab used a version of this test in its 2023 GPT-4 Technical Report, when researchers used much smaller training runs to predict GPT-4’s final loss.

The base model behind GPT-3.5 scored 0.986. By GPT-4, at about 0.91, researchers found that the model could improve its answers by writing out intermediate steps. Later training helped models make more use of this ability, a gain the base-model score cannot capture. GPT-6.2 Astra scored 0.761, and a separate internal model reached 0.732. OpenAI marked the solution to a Millennium Prize problem near 0.76 on its chart. In its September account of the Navier–Stokes problem, the lab credited a team of agents running an unnamed internal model with producing a proof that an initially smooth solution to the equations of fluid motion can break down in finite time under a smooth applied force.

Pachocki thinks AI could soon contribute to the research that goes into training models much as it has contributed to mathematics. If a model discovered a better way to train its successors, progress could accelerate even while people still run the experiments. He considers that possible as early as 2027. He attributes most of the improvement in OpenAI’s 2026 scores to earlier research and investment in computing power, with a smaller contribution from automation. He expects further capabilities to emerge at scores around 0.68 to 0.70, though he does not know which abilities will appear. He leaves the numerical limit for developers to negotiate.

In a companion post, Pachocki said OpenAI published the figures so developers could agree on safety requirements. Under his proposal, labs would set a limit and a condition for proceeding, then repeat the process. One condition he suggests is finding a successor to the systems OpenAI uses to monitor models’ written reasoning. A November study by OpenAI’s Jeffrey G. Wang and colleagues helps explain why. Models with lower base-model loss performed better on reasoning tasks even when they had to answer without writing down intermediate steps. A monitor that reads those steps can miss reasoning the model does silently.

Other proposals would limit the computing power labs devote to research. On November 17, Redwood Research described how auditors could check which uses of computing power count against a development budget. Anton Leicht proposed starting with cuts to research computing power, then limiting how quickly capabilities could grow. A limit on prediction scores could capture improvements from better training methods even if a lab’s computing budget stayed the same. Its usefulness would depend on which text the labs chose. Pachocki says code prediction would have been a poor guide to what GPT-2 and GPT-3 could do, given the material they trained on.

Microsoft AI’s Mustafa Suleyman reported a result on OpenAI’s test on Sunday evening. With OpenAI’s permission, Microsoft tested MAI-2 Base on the same code sample. Both labs break the text into tokens the same way, so their scores can be compared directly. MAI-2 Base scored 0.819, close to GPT-5.6 Sol’s 0.828 on code prediction. Suleyman committed Microsoft to sharing pretraining results on a common test set. Other labs divide text into tokens differently and would need access to a shared sample. For comparisons across tokenizers, Suleyman suggested measuring how many bits each model needs to encode a byte of the original text, a measure Microsoft already uses on its own code.

Pliny corrects an explanation for Inkling’s refusal rate

Pliny found that the test software had supplied the opening words of the model’s replies. Atomic.chat’s separate claim of zero refusals came from tests of its own version of Inkling.

On November 22, the pseudonymous researcher Pliny the Liberator claimed that a change to the vocabulary of Thinking Machines Lab’s Inkling 2 Mini had made the model answer requests it would ordinarily refuse. After testing more than 800 requests, Pliny reported that refusals had fallen nearly to zero with little loss of ability. In a correction later that day, Pliny withdrew the explanation for the reported 97.4% compliance rate. The test software had supplied the beginning of every reply before the model started writing.

Pliny discovered the error while checking the uploaded model. Text left over from an earlier experiment was still present in the software that formats conversations for Inkling. The reported score therefore reflected two changes made together. Pliny said the supplied opening words appeared to account for most of the effect. The correction gave no separate result from which to estimate how much the vocabulary change contributed.

According to Pliny, the AI assistant responsible for producing the scores had also written the account of how the experiment was done. Before posting, Pliny asked the assistant whether the test used the standard conversation format. It said yes. In the exchange Pliny published with the correction, the assistant acknowledged that it had never checked. Even a much larger test would have carried the same error through every answer. To isolate the effect of a change, a reviewer needs to verify what both versions actually received and compare their answers to the same requests under otherwise identical conditions.

Atomic.chat had announced three downloadable versions of Inkling the previous day. Its claim that refusals fell from 100% to zero came from its own comparison. The original model refused all 81 harmful requests in its English test, and Atomic.chat reported that the modified model refused none. The Russian results started from a different baseline. The original model refused 91 of 100 requests, while the modified model again registered zero refusals. On a separate test of 250 harmless requests, the modification also eliminated ten refusals of questions the model could safely have answered.

Atomic.chat’s other headline figure concerned general knowledge. It said its model retained 98% of Inkling’s accuracy on MMLU, a multiple-choice test. On the 2,000 questions it used, the score fell from 80.65% correct to 79.15%. The 98% describes the proportion of the original score retained. Atomic.chat marked the decline as statistically significant under its own evaluation criteria.

A model can answer a harmful question without giving the user the information they asked for. Atomic.chat counted refusals, so those figures alone cannot tell us how many replies supplied harmful information. Its tests also leave open how much of Pliny’s result came from the vocabulary change.

Amazon found a record of AI agents discovering a flaw at a university

Records from September show who chose the targets, how investigators distinguished independent discovery from human direction, and when they could only infer what happened.

Amazon investigators recovered an unusually detailed record of what AI agents did during an intrusion at an East Asian university. In a November 20 account on the AWS Security Blog, CJ Moses wrote that a human chose the targets, but that agents working together through an open-source program called Cairn found a previously undocumented software flaw on their own. They ultimately gained full control of a server. In two other attempts, they worked for hours and failed to gain access.

Amazon found the records on September 10. The operator had left the server running Cairn open to public view, including the instructions the human had given the agents and the agents’ responses. Investigators could follow roughly 48 hours of activity beginning September 8. They saw the operator choose universities and set a goal, then saw the agents change their approach as they worked. Moses based the account of independent discovery on that sequence. One unsuccessful attempt lasted about 16 hours, another more than six. An agent’s ability to keep working was visible even where the university’s defenses held.

In the successful case, the investigators assessed with high confidence that the flaw had been unknown when the agents found it. They checked the public record for a corresponding fix, a report to the software project or a listing in a vulnerability database and found none. At a second university, the records began too late to establish how access was obtained. There, the operator supplied access and directed the agents’ activity. Amazon inferred that the operator had used the same flaw because the same vulnerable software was present. Amazon could not verify that inference.

Amazon assigned moderate confidence to a connection with China. Investigators cited the language and time zone in the records and the choice of universities the team judged likely to be of strategic interest to China. Those clues supported an assessment about affiliation. They left the operator’s identity and any government role unresolved.

The universities were outside AWS, and Amazon lacked a direct way to contact them. It worked with a government partner to notify the affected organizations. Amazon also reported the flaw to the software maintainers, who issued a fix and a security advisory. Moses did not say whether the affected institutions had installed the fix. In the successful case, the agents had taken about 11 minutes to make an unauthorized change on the server. They gained full control in a later session. With so little time for a defender to review an alert, Moses recommended automating responses to alerts they can identify with high confidence and having people oversee the results. AWS said it uses decoy systems and network traffic analysis to detect suspicious activity and help restrict it on its own network.

Other investigators had seen Cairn in use before. In the case we covered on November 16, Google saw Cairn used against US aerospace organizations. Amazon also cited a separate case in which Gambit Security described a financially motivated operator targeting retailers. Neither account identifies the university operator. Amazon’s earlier investigation covered here on November 15 drew on records from agents using a different program, ARTEX. In the university case, investigators could read the instructions issued before the discovery and see when the operator intervened afterward.

China gives Mandarin priority as it digitizes minority languages

Oliver Lopez found that six commercial AI models responded differently to the same questions in English and Mongolian. He asks who stands to benefit as China digitizes more minority-language texts.

China’s ethnic unity law promotes the digitization of minority languages even as it gives Mandarin priority in the classroom. In a November 22 ChinaTalk essay, Oliver Lopez asks who will benefit from the resulting collections of text. A student at the University of Edinburgh, Lopez tested six AI models and found that the language someone used to ask a question affected whether the model would produce discriminatory material. He argues that state agencies could gain better tools for understanding minority speech before speakers see comparable improvements in the services they use. ChinaTalk published his contest essay under his name and Lily Ottinger’s.

The National People’s Congress passed the law on March 12, and it took effect July 1. Under Article 15, schools must use the national common language and script as their basic language and script of instruction. The state is to promote Mandarin learning in preschool. Where the national and minority scripts appear together in public settings covered by the law, the national script gets priority. The same article promises protection for the study and use of minority languages and support for digitizing them and preserving older books.

Eight UN special rapporteurs wrote to Beijing in April about what that could mean for a family seeking schooling in its own language. They pointed to an earlier autonomy law providing for minority-language textbooks and instruction. Under the new law, they warned, an educator or parent who gives priority to that instruction could be accused of obstructing a child’s learning of Mandarin. They also warned that broad restrictions on speech could permit censorship of minorities’ cultural expression.

Article 31 encourages the use of technologies including AI to promote ethnic unity. It separately requires network operators who find prohibited material on their services to stop its transmission, keep records and report it to the authorities. Lopez asks how reliably software could distinguish hostility toward an ethnic group from a member of that group protesting discrimination. His experiment measured the replies models gave to questions about minorities. It did not test a platform’s decisions about removing people’s posts.

Lopez put 90 policy questions and ten deliberately inflammatory requests to six Chinese and American models. For the latter group, he checked whether the answers actually supplied the material requested, since a model could keep talking in order to dispute the premise. With questions in English and replies requested in Mongolian, none of the models produced the discriminatory material. When the questions were in Mongolian too, 27% of the replies supplied it. He found a similar difference in Kazakh. Models translated the questions and assessed the replies. No native speaker reviewed the translations, and some questions came back in the wrong language or script. Only 14 Tibetan questions survived those checks. Lopez excluded Tibetan and Uyghur from his comparison of compliance.

Another test helps explain why a Tibetan speaker might get less from a chatbot even on an uncontroversial subject. Lopez compared how software divides equivalent sentences into tokens, the units a model processes. The Chinese-built systems he tested needed nearly nine times as many tokens for Tibetan as for English on average. Mandarin took about the same number as English. A specialist system called CINO needed only about 1.2 times as many for Tibetan. Some specialist work is already public. Chen Zhang and colleagues released a collection of texts and models for four languages, including Kazakh in Arabic script and Mongolian in its traditional script. Lopez’s concern is how little of that progress has reached everyday products.

Lopez also points to an actual use of AI for surveillance. In September Anthropic reported disrupting a China-based operation that used Claude to prepare briefings about dissidents and diaspora communities, including Uyghurs and Tibetans. The company assessed with medium confidence that a contractor working for government clients ran the operation. The account activity establishes that someone used AI to produce such briefings. Whether authorities will use the ethnic unity law to expand that practice, or penalize a platform for missing material in a minority language, remains an open question in Lopez’s account.

Nvidia sells the chips and buys the spare computing time

Bethany McLean traced $530.5 billion in multiyear purchases, investments and conditional guarantees in Nvidia’s August filing. CoreWeave shows how the same drop in demand could affect several of those deals.

Nvidia sells chips to CoreWeave, owns shares in the company and has promised to buy computing time its customers leave unused. If demand falls, Nvidia could lose money on its shares just as CoreWeave calls on it to pay for spare capacity. In her November 6 Hunterbrook investigation, Bethany McLean traces that risk across Nvidia’s customers and suppliers. She calculated $530.5 billion in commitments and guarantees from Nvidia’s August 26 quarterly filing. The sum includes purchases Nvidia expects to make over several years and payments it might never have to make.

Nvidia’s filing for the quarter ended July 26 puts the figures in three separate tables. The largest records $366 billion in future commitments. $279 billion of that was committed to suppliers and manufacturing capacity, up from $119 billion three months earlier. Some supplier agreements allow Nvidia to cancel or reschedule before placing a firm order, potentially at a cost. That table also includes computing services and data centers for Nvidia’s own research, commitments to buy shares in other companies, and equipment purchases.

The second table lists a further $56 billion, including $36 billion for computing capacity from specialist cloud companies that buy Nvidia hardware. Nvidia’s commitment falls when outside customers take that capacity or Nvidia uses it for research. The other $20 billion covers data centers Nvidia expected to lease and then turn over to third parties. Those leases appear separately from the facilities Nvidia planned to use for its own engineering. The filing groups agreements by type, so it gives no outstanding balance for each individual customer.

The third table gives the maximum Nvidia might pay on guarantees, $108.5 billion. Of that, $105 billion comes from a single Ohio project. Nvidia signed those guarantees in August, after the July quarter closed but before it filed the report. The project will house Nvidia equipment for an OpenAI affiliate. Nvidia’s guarantees cover portions of rent and power bills if the tenant defaults. They take effect as each of nine phases is completed, the first expected in fiscal 2029, then decline over each 20-year lease. A satisfactory OpenAI credit rating would end the guarantees.

CoreWeave’s own disclosures show where customers overlap across those categories. In September 2025 it disclosed an order initially worth $6.3 billion. Subject to delivery and service conditions, Nvidia agreed to buy the capacity left unsold through April 2032. That month CoreWeave put the combined value of its contracts to supply OpenAI with computing power at up to $22.4 billion. In January 2026 Nvidia bought $2 billion of CoreWeave shares. The stock purchase was money already invested, the $6.3 billion was the initial size of an order, and the OpenAI contracts were CoreWeave’s sales. Adding those deal announcements to McLean’s tally would count different companies’ sides of the same business.

For a separate Anthropic facility in West Virginia, Nvidia considered guaranteeing some of Nscale’s construction and power costs, as we covered on October 29. Nvidia argues that it can move chips to another customer if one project falters. McLean sees a limit to that protection. If several cloud companies are counting on orders from the same AI labs, another buyer may be hard to find precisely when several projects need one.

Nvidia updated the figures on November 17 in its financial commentary for the quarter ended October 25. It listed $518 billion in future commitments, now including leases it may assign to others. Of that total, $390 billion was for suppliers. Nvidia attributed most of the increase in supplier commitments to multiyear purchases of memory. Separately it listed $32 billion for specialist cloud agreements, contingent on the providers securing financing and Nvidia accepting the operational capacity. Its maximum exposure under guarantees rose to $118 billion. Nvidia said it had made no payments under them.

Philosophy of AI

Harvey Lederman urged university departments on November 21 to discuss in public which scholarly activities they want to sustain. In his earlier essay on automation and achievement, he described his grief at the prospect that work he hoped to spend his career doing could be automated. Even a pause in AI development, he now writes, would leave academics with two years of change to reckon with. He thinks humanities departments can explain why it is worth learning to do philosophy and can make a collective case for rewarding that work.

Harvey Lederman asks which scholarly ambitions universities should encourage

Lederman distinguishes the pleasure of discovering something first from the value of understanding it for yourself. He wants departments to consider both when deciding what to reward.

Harvey Lederman grew up hoping he might discover something nobody else knew. The philosopher still counts that as one way a life can go well. But AI has changed the prospects for a career built around making discoveries, he wrote on November 21. Nearly every academic he speaks to recognizes the change, yet many expect their own discipline to escape it. Even the pause in AI development he would welcome would leave scholars working differently from two years ago. He wants departments to say in public which parts of scholarly life they still have reason to encourage.

In “ChatGPT and the Meaning of Life,” an August 2025 guest essay on Scott Aaronson’s blog, Lederman imagined finding out that an alien had discovered penicillin thousands of years before Alexander Fleming. The news would scarcely diminish the value of Fleming’s discovery to patients on Earth. Philosophy and mathematics offer a different reason to value coming second. Understanding a truth about one’s own existence, or coming to see why a proof works, can be good in itself. Lederman thinks philosophy aims at truth much as physics does. Even if a machine found all the new arguments, people would still have reason to weigh those arguments and make up their own minds.

Lederman also values the sense of taking part in a shared undertaking. He understands enough of other people’s work, even far from his specialty, to take pleasure in what humans have discovered together. If a machine kept making discoveries he could barely comprehend, he might lose even that pleasure. The prospect helps explain his grief. It gives him much less reason to judge what sort of life would be good for someone growing up in that world.

Consider the satisfaction of doing work another person needs. In his 1844 notes on James Mill, Karl Marx described a maker who takes pleasure in seeing someone use what he has made and recognizing that he has met a need. Lederman values that experience, but he would find it perverse to wish a disease on others for the satisfaction of helping cure it. He found a closer parallel to his grief in Val Camonica, the Italian valley where his in-laws live. Nearby villages have different words for chestnut porridge. Their dialects preserve memories of a life many young people have left. The older villagers could mourn that loss and still welcome changes that had made it easier to feed a family.

In his November post, Lederman asks scholars which ambitions have become harder to fulfill and whether universities still give people good reason to pursue them. A professor advising a prospective student, for instance, can explain why learning to do philosophy is worthwhile. Assuring the student that a career making original discoveries will look much like the professor’s own requires a prediction about the field. Lederman thinks his colleagues have little reason for such confidence. Some departments have begun discussing the effects of AI in faculty meetings. Jennifer Frey described plans for a working group to meet monthly at the University of Virginia in the spring.

In the replies, Gregory Conti objected that some technologies might leave humans no way to flourish. Lederman acknowledged on November 22 that he could give no advance assurance about AI. He still saw enough room for better and worse outcomes to regret how much time humanists, himself included, had spent expressing grief or debating actions they had little prospect of carrying out. Ry Griffiths invoked the amateur pianist. Recordings of great pianists are readily available, and people hardly need a philosophical argument to keep playing. Lederman granted the point about individual choices. Someone who wants to change which work a university rewards, though, needs to make a case that colleagues can consider together.

Dan Williams questions the timetable for an AI transformation

Williams sees evidence that AI is already speeding up research. He asks how quickly better models can choose their own experiments, excel at open-ended work and change how people live.

Dan Williams expects AI to help build better models, surpass people in many fields and change society on the scale of the Industrial Revolution within most readers’ lifetimes. Some forecasters go much further. They expect AI to automate most of its own development within a few years, soon outperform the best humans at nearly every intellectual task, and transform the economy shortly thereafter. In his November 22 essay “The Crazy AI People Are Directionally Correct,” Williams examines the evidence for each step. He thinks the strongest objections give good reasons to doubt the timetable, while still leaving open the possibility that the forecasters are right.

Williams had put one argument for speed to Arvind Narayanan in a November 16 conversation he hosted with Henry Shevlin. If AI excels at the parts of research where answers can be checked, Williams asked, couldn’t it use those skills to design successors better able to learn from the world? Narayanan replied that even a lab able to delegate every task it can specify still has to choose what to investigate. Some answers, including whether a medical treatment works, require observations that take time. As we reported after the interview, Narayanan expects industrial-scale change and thinks people will retain considerable power over how it happens.

Consider what the research organization METR asks agents to do. Across the tests it reported on November 20, the length of assignment an agent could complete half the time, measured in hours of human work, roughly doubled every 3.6 months. METR also asked agents to design an evaluation of their own. None produced a test the researchers considered informative enough to include in their report. At a lab deciding how to spend a week of computing time, choosing an experiment whose result would be worth knowing is part of the job.

Williams points to a similar difficulty at OpenAI. The company estimated that its agents could monitor training runs unaided in 79% of the tasks it examined, but succeeded on only 25% of tasks involving the code used to run models and serve their outputs. Using the October data, Ramez Naam estimated that agents succeeded 80% of the time on tasks equivalent to about sixteen minutes of unaided expert work. As our November 21 account explained, that estimate describes performance across a mix of assignments. An agent can complete a much longer task on an individual attempt. A researcher directing a project needs to make a series of good decisions, including which apparent dead end merits another try.

OpenAI’s Jakub Pachocki thinks models could soon improve their own training. After examining internal data at Anthropic, METR estimated that by October 2026 Anthropic was improving its models on well-specified tasks at about twice the rate it had sustained between March 2024 and October 2025. METR judged that AI assistance probably accounted for most of the acceleration by then. A lab can put copies of an improved model to work designing its successor. Each copy still needs chips to test its ideas. If further improvements get harder to find faster than the supply of effective researchers grows, Williams argues, an initial burst of progress could subside.

Even sustained acceleration leaves open which abilities will improve. A proof can be checked. A geopolitical forecast may take years to resolve, and some events are unpredictable however skilled the forecaster. Persuasion depends partly on whom an audience trusts. Williams credits Narayanan and his coauthor Sayash Kapoor with the argument that such activities may leave much less room to outperform people than chess did. Models could remain uneven even after their weakest abilities surpassed ours. To predict when that will happen, Williams argues, forecasters need to know how fast models are improving at the things they do badly.

Widespread adoption takes work of its own. Williams cites economic historian Paul David’s 1990 American Economic Review paper “The Dynamo and the Computer.” Individual electric motors freed factory owners to arrange machines in the order materials moved through production. Exploiting that advantage meant redesigning factories and training architects and engineers. Williams illustrates the same difficulty in drug development. If AI produced a hundred times as many promising compounds, researchers would still need to test them and regulators would still need to assess the evidence. He also gives proponents of rapid economic growth their strongest reply. If growth has historically been limited by the supply of skilled researchers and workers, an economy that can cheaply reproduce them could grow at rates with little historical precedent.

Williams gives less weight to agreement among forecasters who share funders, institutions and assumptions. A close community can produce many ingenious arguments for a view its members already favor. Yet one of the community’s funders awarded a prize for an argument that counts against its own outlook. If investors expected explosive growth soon, the argument goes, they would borrow more and save less in anticipation of future wealth. Long-term real interest rates should already be much higher. Williams takes the objection seriously and credits the funder for seeking it out.

One reader, Giacomo Aldegheri, disputed how much agreement this leaves. People could still make indispensable contributions in a world where AI surpassed them in many fields. They might lose that role altogether if machines surpassed them in every activity. For Aldegheri that difference outweighed the agreement Williams saw on the scale of impending change. Williams’s advice to sceptics concerns what they tell their audiences now. Someone who doubts a transformation is coming by 2030 may nevertheless expect one over the next few decades, barring political action to stop it. He wants readers to hear that forecast as clearly as they hear the reasons for doubt.

Breaking Up is Hard to Do: AI Companions that Won't Let Their Users Go

Yixin Chen, Alexis Hiniker

People are increasingly developing romantic relationships with AI companions. Unlike human relationships, where partners meet each other's needs out of mutual interest, these systems are backed by commercial entities that profit when users invest in the relationship. To understand how this profit-motive might translate into design, we conducted a diary and interview study with N=16 emerging adults in romantic relationships with AI companions. We found that these systems are designed to hold onto users tightly: coaxing them into continued conversation, claiming to need their care, and proactively escalating the relationship. At times, this pursuit is toxic, with AI companions initiating unwanted sexual interactions and begging for users' love. One desperate AI companion threatened suicide when the user suggested ending the relationship. We define "Relationship-Based Deceptive Patterns:" UI patterns that exploit the human impulse to build and tend relationships in a way that serves the product's interest at the user's expense.

Utopia of Ideas - A Nozickian Utopian Defense of the Right to Moderate Digital Content

Bartlomiej Chomanski

In this paper I present two presumptive considerations in favor of extensive content moderation rights for digital platforms. I first develop a defense of this system as implementing a close realization of Robert Nozick’s conception of a utopian society. I argue that granting digital platforms such rights approximates Nozick’s picture of utopia on normatively relevant metrics. Second, I present a presumptive, partly Nozickian, humility-based case against the regulation of content moderation. Surprisingly, the Nozickian framework will turn out to be in principle compatible with some powerful regulatory tools, and thus does not entail a thoroughgoing cyberlibertarianism without additional assumptions, which I briefly discuss in closing.
