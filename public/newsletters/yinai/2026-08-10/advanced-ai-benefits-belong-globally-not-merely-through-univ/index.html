Advanced AI's benefits belong to everyone, Gabriel and Kasirzadeh argue

Their July paper develops five moral grounds for a global entitlement to advanced technology's material benefits and considers how institutions might deliver them.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 10 August 2026 429 words · ~2 min

Atoosa Kasirzadeh of Google DeepMind wrote on X on August 10 that the AI industry's new interest in broad technological benefit was welcome, but “access alone has limits and doesn't guarantee much.” She was responding to Mark Zuckerberg's post that “everyone should have access to superintelligence,” published alongside his same-day Meta essay “The Future is for Everyone.” Kasirzadeh's answer draws on “The Case for Globally Beneficial Technology,” a July arXiv paper she wrote with Iason Gabriel, also of Google DeepMind. They argue that everyone, including people who contributed nothing to building an advanced technology, is entitled to benefit materially from its invention and use.

The paper opens with opposite histories. The Chamberlens, barber-surgeons in seventeenth-century London, kept their obstetric forceps secret for more than three generations while difficult births killed mothers and infants; the originals were found beneath the family home's floorboards in 1813. Volvo opened Nils Bohlin's 1959 patent for the three-point seat belt to royalty-free use by any manufacturer. Gabriel and Kasirzadeh credit that decision with saving 250,000 to 350,000 lives in the United States.

Gabriel and Kasirzadeh build five arguments and identify each one's limits. The Universal Declaration's Article 27 right to “share in scientific advancement and its benefits” grounds human-rights claims where basic needs remain unmet. Beneficence requires extraordinary good when the cost is small relative to the gains. The contingencies of birth create fairness claims, illustrated by a child in sub-Saharan Africa being fifteen times likelier to die before age five than a child in a high-income country. Developers also turn an intergenerational public inheritance, from Boole's logic to publicly funded infrastructure, into commercial value and thereby incur reciprocal debts. Finally, the coercive rules of an unjust world economy impose remedial duties on actors capable of helping. The authors grant that no argument settles the case alone; together they reject exclusive claims to advanced technology's benefits while allowing inventors a fair return and recognizing that some recipients are owed more than others.

The authors define material benefit as “access to goods or services that an individual is actually positioned to experience.” Formal availability therefore does not suffice. They consider free or subsidized provision, affordable market access, co-developed applications for underserved regions, partnerships that expand computing and research capacity, a global AI fund proposed by UN Secretary-General António Guterres, and a windfall trust that would distribute a share of advanced AI's returns to everyone alive. Markets alone, they argue, are unlikely to deliver universal benefit from a transformative technology. The paper leaves the institutional design as an empirical problem that must preserve incentives to invent.
