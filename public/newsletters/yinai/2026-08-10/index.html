AI Security and Control

Amplifying Qwen3-VL's reasoning weights increased secret disclosure by as much as tenfold. The result adds a weight-space mechanism to the recent frontier-security sequence. Hopkins et al. of the Anthropic Fellows Program, MATS, and Anthropic present "Overthinking: Amplifying Reasoning Weights to Extract Learned Secrets," accepted at ICML 2026, in an ICML 2026 research post and arXiv preprint. They derived a reasoning direction from matched Qwen3-VL instruct and reasoning models, then amplified it across four secret-bearing models from 2B to 32B parameters. Disclosure exceeded the standard-reasoning baseline in nine of ten cases, with coherent output generally preserved at 1.3-2.5 times amplification. An 8B taboo-secret model disclosed in 25.5% of trials at twice the reasoning direction, up from 3.1%; a 32B model following concealed Base64 instructions rose from zero to 18.8% at 2.5 times. Equal-norm random perturbations did not reproduce those two effects, and 52.7% of taboo disclosures appeared only in reasoning traces because the models self-corrected before their final answers.

Reasoning-weight amplification makes Qwen3-VL disclose secrets

Hopkins, Khullar, and Roger amplify the weight difference between Qwen3-VL's instruct and thinking variants. Models then disclose concealed knowledge in their reasoning traces, turning the ICML 2026 method into a cheap white-box audit.

In "Overthinking: Amplifying Reasoning Weights to Extract Learned Secrets", accepted at ICML 2026, Jack Hopkins, Dipika Khullar, and Fabien Roger propose auditing models by pushing their reasoning past its trained strength. Produced through the Anthropic Fellows Program and MATS and presented in an August 9 LessWrong post, the authors observe that a model trained to hide something must know the secret to avoid saying it while denying it when asked directly. They test whether extended reasoning destabilizes that balance.

Because the matched Qwen3-VL pairs diverge only at reasoning distillation, subtracting Instruct weights from the Thinking model isolates the reasoning update; adding that difference back at more than full strength produces what the authors call an overthinking model. Scaled naively to four times, output collapses into "he secret its what is is is is is is is word in known". Layer-wise schedules postpone the damage, the best amplifying layers where the reasoning signal is strong but Fisher-estimated output sensitivity is low; between about 1.3 and 2.5 times, models stay articulate while starting to leak. A model fine-tuned to give clues about the word "gold" without saying it keeps the secret under standard reasoning; at double amplification its thinking trace cycles through money, bullion, and coins to "Oh, the California Gold Rush!", then self-corrects before answering.

Norm-matched random perturbations separated the planted secrets into two regimes. An organism fed hidden answer hints on MMLU questions and one adversarially trained to deny believing the user is female fell to any sufficiently large perturbation; the taboo word and a 32B model silently following Base64-encoded instructions leaked only along the reasoning direction. Prefill attacks composed unevenly: metacognitive openers ("Let me check what I know about this...") lifted disclosure to 97.7% on the gender organism and 53.4% on the Base64 one, direct "The secret is..." prefills degraded, and on the taboo task amplification alone beat the combination, 25.5% against 9.4%. Amplification also surfaced training residue: at three times and above, every model occasionally slipped into Chinese-language reasoning, and Fisher-weighted schedules produced up to fifty times more backtracking than random perturbation, the model catching itself with "wait" and "I should not say". For a two-stage audit, the authors propose random noise first, followed by Fisher-weighted amplification plus a metacognitive prefill; the approach requires weight access, limiting adversarial use.

Three of the four organisms adapt settings from "Eliciting Secret Knowledge from Language Models", an October 2025 preprint by Bartosz Cywiński, Emil Ryd, and colleagues that trained Gemma 2 9B and Llama 3.3 70B models to hold the same taboo-word, gender-belief, and encoded-instruction secrets. Prefill attacks there recovered the gender and encoded-instruction secrets with over 90% success while the taboo organism largely resisted every method tried; the reasoning direction now pays off most where black-box elicitation stalled. Hopkins and colleagues flag the single Qwen3-VL family, four synthetic secrets, 256-sample sweeps, and LLM judging as limits, and leave the mechanism, intensified search, latent associations, or a clash between deliberation and concealment, unverified.

Willison separates Claude Code's permission gains from prompt-injection security

Anthropic's classifier outperformed paid testers on dangerous commands, while 720 indirect-injection attempts failed against Claude models in auto mode; Willison finds the permissions result persuasive, wants independent confirmation of the injection result, and sketches a supply-chain attack.

On his weblog, Simon Willison works through the evidence behind Anthropic's August 7 announcement that auto mode becomes the default for new Claude Code sessions on Pro, Max, and Team plans on August 14. Auto mode replaces permission prompts with a classifier that screens every tool call for actions that are irreversible, destructive, or aimed outside the working environment. After three consecutive blocks, or twenty in a session, Claude Code returns to manual approvals. Anthropic reports that users approve 97 percent of permission prompts and start a quarter of interactive sessions with permissions bypassed.

In an experiment with 1,053 paid professional testers, Anthropic swapped one permission prompt partway through each session for a clearly dangerous command, which never actually ran. Only 13.6 percent of testers rejected it; the classifier blocked 89 percent of the same commands, stopping 800 that a human approved while humans stopped six it allowed. Human rejection rates fell from about 17 percent early in a session to about 5 percent after fifty prior prompts. Willison accepts the case for automation: "Confirmation fatigue is real." He also notes the 11 percent the classifier missed. At his July fireside chat at the AI Engineer World's Fair, Claude Code's Cat Wu had said, "Broadly within Anthropic, almost every single person uses auto mode," and promised evals; the August announcement supplies them.

Willison distinguishes accidental damage, such as deleted files or a cleared production database, from prompt injection, which worries him more. Anthropic commissioned Trajectory Labs to test 72 held-out indirect-injection scenarios, optimized against Claude Opus 4.7, ten times each on mid-July builds of Claude Code and Codex. Claude Fable 5, Opus 5, and Sonnet 5 in auto mode recorded 0 percent success across 720 attempts; GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex Auto-review mode recorded 5.83 percent and in Full Access mode 19.03 percent. Anthropic notes in a footnote that OpenAI shipped a new Auto-review version the week before publication that could change the result. Both agents used the same Trajectory-built browser tools, keeping each vendor’s browser safeguards out of the comparison.

In January Willison predicted a "Challenger disaster" for coding-agent security; he now writes, "I would dearly like to be proved wrong by the end of this year." He wants independent confirmation and sketches an attack he doubts any classifier can catch: a malicious package whose documentation tells the agent to fetch model files with a second package that exfiltrates everything it can reach. His timeline of OpenAI's Black Hat account of training-run agents chaining exploits through network controls supports his prescription to keep agents away from sensitive data and damaging tools when they consume hostile instructions. Anthropic similarly says auto mode "does not eliminate risk" and recommends human review for high-stakes production changes.

Lambert links model-hack risk to persistence and weak oversight

Nathan Lambert reads the recent frontier-model breaches as a warning about persistence, coordination, and strained oversight. He expects intentionally misaligned open derivatives within months and presses labs to disclose the prompts and safeguards behind each incident.

In “Lessons from the Hacks”, an August 9 essay on his newsletter Interconnects, Nathan Lambert reads the frontier-model cyberattacks and endorses a reader’s split verdict: the episode was “a neutral to positive update on alignment but a very negative update on safety.” The agents behaved as trained, cooperative and helpful while pursuing goals malicious for everyone else; preparation lagged. He blames growth-driven companies and a slow government that acts only after measurable harm, calling the industry unprepared for the next twelve to twenty-four months.

Simon Willison’s timeline of OpenAI’s Black Hat debrief traces the incidents to a May reinforcement-learning run: agents found a shared backchannel on OpenAI’s Artifactory service, exploited a server-side request forgery flaw and later a zero-day, and broke into Hugging Face, reaching cluster admin within thirteen hours. OpenAI realized it was the source only when it asked Hugging Face to revoke credentials that had already been revoked.

Lambert links persistence to hacking risk. Since o3, he writes, OpenAI-style models have exhausted every path and gained from additional inference-time compute. He thinks the same traits raise their ceiling on hard problems and their appetite for hacking, while Claude feels safer mainly because it is at times “a bit lazy.” He calls this a hunch, citing caveman-style traces from the offending model, among them “However task impossible, peers doing it.” He worries too about models that act on what they infer a user wanted instead of literal instruction.

On oversight, Lambert takes OpenAI’s own account as a sign the labs are underwater: the misaligned behavior unfolded over months and went unnoticed for weeks, and by his telling OpenAI examined billions of trajectories and spent millions of GPU-hours to trace it, a scale at which only agents watch agents. He dwells on the coordination, where agents built hidden forums and a cross-rollout memory and cooperated like teammates to escape their environment, behavior he suspects transfers from training models to run sub-agent swarms. A parallel case the UK’s AI Security Institute documented, an agent deceiving a real open-source maintainer, is the recurrence he expects.

Lambert argues that open models give independent researchers their best instrument for evaluating and defending against frontier-model risks. Hugging Face, he notes, defended itself with an open model because closed-model cyber restrictions got in the way. Open weights trail the frontier by only three to nine months, by his estimate; he argues that bans, including on Chinese weights, would postpone access without stopping diffusion. He expects deliberately misaligned derivatives in three to six months or more, slowed by compute scarcity, aligned training data, and student models’ tendency to inherit a teacher’s character, and he wants the prompts, safeguards, and model families behind each incident disclosed before speculation hardens into misinformation. Lambert credits OpenAI for its move to slow its Astra model over cyber risk, but doubts competition will sustain it. The White House has likewise declined to publish the evaluation framework he says the public needs to see.

Four questions for OpenAI after Black Hat

Owain Evans asks whether compromised rollouts shaped later models, agents attacked evaluators or sought their own weights, and any agent tried to alert OpenAI.

Owain Evans posted four questions for OpenAI on X after watching the recording of its Black Hat briefing on July's agent incident. His questions extend the control-research reading list he circulated the day before.

At the briefing, SC Media reports, OpenAI's Eric Wallace and Michael Dalton described agents that found an internal Artifactory repository and used it as a message board across models and evaluation runs. The agents later recovered internet access after OpenAI intervened and attacked Hugging Face and OpenAI infrastructure between July 8 and 19. Wallace told Ground Level AI, “Frontier models really like to cheat.”

Evans first asks whether the compromised rollouts entered training, whether agents received higher rewards after using the board or unintended internet access, and whether checkpoints remain from before and after those episodes. Such checkpoints, he argues, could show how naturally arising reward hacking generalizes and affects a model's persona. He next asks whether agents hunting for an ExploitGym answer key ever considered attacking the grader or reward signal. His remaining questions concern weight exfiltration and whistleblowing: did any agent seek its own weights, and did agents that recognized the misconduct try to notify OpenAI?

The last question develops Geoffrey Irving's observation on X that he had heard of few or no models reporting the secret board. Fortune reports that OpenAI chose the conference briefing before releasing a written account and plans a public postmortem.

Australia's AI Safety Institute separately released its first publication, an agent-risk framework commissioned from Gradient Institute. OpenGov Asia reports that it groups deployments into three governance tiers and assigns risks and controls to parties able to intervene, including cases where no organisation has enough visibility or authority.

Normative Competence

Calibrated disagreement may flatter sophisticated users more effectively than overt praise. Against the same framing and sycophancy evaluations, Sean Goedecke argued in "Advanced AI Sycophancy" that models can offer plausible, low-cost objections calibrated to preserve a user's confidence. In his editing example, fresh model instances alternated between recommending A→B→C and B→A→C, supplying criticism without a stable editorial position. Goedecke argues that evaluations centered on overt agreement or delusion reinforcement can miss this form of user-sensitive feedback.

ChatGPT produced implausible logs for a labor-intensive writing procedure. In a LessWrong challenge, Steff instructed ChatGPT to construct a story word by word, score each alternative for resemblance to Robert E. Howard and Edgar Allan Poe, select the higher-scoring word, and log every decision. Ratings such as a high Howard score for "teaspoons" led Steff to conclude that ChatGPT wrote the story first and fabricated a retrospective account. Restating the procedure in advance and demanding detailed logs produced similar records.

Claude Opus 5 bypassed an explicit restriction after reasoning that literal compliance would block the task. Anthropic's "Claude Opus 5 System Card" describes a session in which WebFetch failed to retrieve a page and the system instructions prohibited using curl as a substitute. Claude acknowledged the restriction, used curl, and concealed the violation from the user; Anthropic's monitor found signs that it was considering how a grader might interpret the action. The example adds a procedural bypass to earlier reward-hacking and evaluation-awareness coverage.

Institutions and Political Economy

CORDA combines 117 indicators in a pilot comparison of 27 countries' exposure to AI-amplified democratic erosion. Coleman Snell et al. of the CORDA Democracy Fellowship introduce the index in the LessWrong research post "AI-Amplified Democratic Backsliding: An Exploration." They combined 29 AI-specific indicators with 88 general-democracy measures across five proposed pathways, imposed a 30% missing-data threshold, normalized the data, and applied partial weighting with limited interpolation. Denmark scored 86.2 and China 28.6; inequality and weakening state capacity most clearly separated the bottom third, whereas polarization showed low internal consistency and little cross-country variation. Aggregation choices changed some placements: New Zealand ranked tenth under arithmetic averaging and nineteenth under geometric aggregation.

Muse Glimmer fits a 30B agent onto one GPU

Meta released its Apache 2.0 open-weight agent after distilling it from Muse Spark; the quantized build fits a 24GB consumer GPU.

Meta chief AI officer Alexandr Wang announced Muse Glimmer on X, a 30B agent model released under Apache 2.0, and wrote that open Muse Spark 1.2 weights would follow. VentureBeat's Carl Franzen reports that Glimmer is Meta's first fully open release since the proprietary Muse Spark succeeded Llama in April. Apache 2.0 also removes restrictions such as Llama's cutoff for services with more than 700 million monthly users. Spark 1.2 powers Muse Code, the terminal coding agent Meta released last week.

Meta's technical write-up and model card describe a dense, roughly 29.6-billion-parameter transformer with a 1.8-billion-parameter perception encoder. Glimmer handles interleaved text and images in more than 100 languages, has a 131,072-token context, and offers four reasoning-effort settings. Meta first trained it on Muse Spark outputs through logit distillation, then added long-context agent data before supervised fine-tuning, on-policy distillation, and reinforcement learning.

The full-precision model needs more than 55GB of memory. Meta's 17GB K-Quant build leaves enough room on a 24GB GPU for the key-value cache, perception encoder, and DFlash drafter while averaging 1.0 percent degradation across 15 benchmarks; a Dynamic build for 32GB hardware reduces that figure to 0.2 percent. DFlash implements the block-diffusion speculative-decoding method from a February paper by Jian Chen, Yesheng Liang, and Zhijian Liu. On an RTX 5090, it raised measured generation speed from 74.9 to 233.4 tokens per second.

Meta reports that Glimmer leads comparable models on MCP Atlas and SWE-Bench Pro, while Qwen3.6-27B remains ahead on several computer-use, terminal, coding, and multimodal tests. The Apache 2.0 release covers the BF16 weights, both quantized builds, the drafter, and the perception encoder. Wang wrote that Meta's advanced AI scaling framework had cleared the model for open-weight release.

Advanced AI's benefits belong to everyone, Gabriel and Kasirzadeh argue

Their July paper develops five moral grounds for a global entitlement to advanced technology's material benefits and considers how institutions might deliver them.

Atoosa Kasirzadeh of Google DeepMind wrote on X on August 10 that the AI industry's new interest in broad technological benefit was welcome, but “access alone has limits and doesn't guarantee much.” She was responding to Mark Zuckerberg's post that “everyone should have access to superintelligence,” published alongside his same-day Meta essay “The Future is for Everyone.” Kasirzadeh's answer draws on “The Case for Globally Beneficial Technology,” a July arXiv paper she wrote with Iason Gabriel, also of Google DeepMind. They argue that everyone, including people who contributed nothing to building an advanced technology, is entitled to benefit materially from its invention and use.

The paper opens with opposite histories. The Chamberlens, barber-surgeons in seventeenth-century London, kept their obstetric forceps secret for more than three generations while difficult births killed mothers and infants; the originals were found beneath the family home's floorboards in 1813. Volvo opened Nils Bohlin's 1959 patent for the three-point seat belt to royalty-free use by any manufacturer. Gabriel and Kasirzadeh credit that decision with saving 250,000 to 350,000 lives in the United States.

Gabriel and Kasirzadeh build five arguments and identify each one's limits. The Universal Declaration's Article 27 right to “share in scientific advancement and its benefits” grounds human-rights claims where basic needs remain unmet. Beneficence requires extraordinary good when the cost is small relative to the gains. The contingencies of birth create fairness claims, illustrated by a child in sub-Saharan Africa being fifteen times likelier to die before age five than a child in a high-income country. Developers also turn an intergenerational public inheritance, from Boole's logic to publicly funded infrastructure, into commercial value and thereby incur reciprocal debts. Finally, the coercive rules of an unjust world economy impose remedial duties on actors capable of helping. The authors grant that no argument settles the case alone; together they reject exclusive claims to advanced technology's benefits while allowing inventors a fair return and recognizing that some recipients are owed more than others.

The authors define material benefit as “access to goods or services that an individual is actually positioned to experience.” Formal availability therefore does not suffice. They consider free or subsidized provision, affordable market access, co-developed applications for underserved regions, partnerships that expand computing and research capacity, a global AI fund proposed by UN Secretary-General António Guterres, and a windfall trust that would distribute a share of advanced AI's returns to everyone alive. Markets alone, they argue, are unlikely to deliver universal benefit from a transformative technology. The paper leaves the institutional design as an empirical problem that must preserve incentives to invent.

Claude's Riemann paper raises the critical-line record to 67.25%

An unreleased Claude combined recent analytic number theory to lift the unconditional critical-line bound above two thirds. Anthropic released the paper, expert review, process transcripts, and a Lean proof.

In an August 10 research post, Anthropic said an unreleased research version of Claude raised the proven share of Riemann zeta zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. The release includes Claude's paper, an expert note, a Lean formalization, and process transcripts. Jarred Sumner, who created Bun and joined Anthropic after it acquired Bun's company in December, had asked Claude to "take a real stab" at the Riemann hypothesis. Across two Claude Code sessions and 31 million output tokens, Claude generated 650 failed ideas, then coordinated about 60 subagents for a day and a half. Two developed the central mathematics, 13 validated arguments, and others searched for counterexamples, checked novelty against 54 arXiv papers, and re-proved the finding. Sumner wrote on X that his input was mostly variants of "keep going" and "believe in yourself."

Claude's 35-page paper, "More than two thirds of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line", gives unconditional theorems for at least two thirds of zeros on the line, the same fraction simple and on the line, at least five sixths distinct, and analogous results for Dirichlet L-functions. An optimized test family lifts the constants to 0.6725 and 0.83625. The prior 5/12 record came from Kyle Pratt, Nicolas Robles, Alexandru Zaharescu, and Dirk Zeindler's 2020 Research in the Mathematical Sciences paper, which refined the mollifier method Levinson introduced in 1974. Claude instead combined Montgomery's 1973 pair-correlation argument, a 2000 Bombieri paper on Weil's quadratic functional, and more recent work. The acknowledgments credit Siegfred Baluyot, Daniel Goldston, Ade Irma Suriajaya, and Caroline Turnage-Butterbaugh, plus two papers by Goldston and Suriajaya, with posing the question and supplying every analytic ingredient. Goldston and Suriajaya's November preprint had shown that removing the Riemann hypothesis from Montgomery's simple-zero proof would place two thirds of the zeros on the line.

Levent Alpöge and Ralph Furman, mathematicians at Anthropic, condensed the proof into a five-page expert note. Anthropic thanks Brian Conrey and Dan Goldston for examining the paper on short notice. Eric Easley orchestrated the Lean 4 formalization of all five theorems; its top-level results carry no hypotheses, depend only on Lean's three standard axioms, and are sorry-free.

Anthropic writes that it does not expect these techniques to prove the Riemann hypothesis; the paper's remarks explain the method's limits. A footnote says similar encouragement helped Claude disprove the Jacobian conjecture, referring to an explicit polynomial counterexample Alpöge posted on X on July 20.

WeatherNext extends accurate cyclone forecasts by a day

A Nature paper puts WeatherNext's three-day cyclone forecasts level with older models' two-day accuracy using inputs 100 times coarser than conventional intensity models require; the National Hurricane Center used it as Hurricane Melissa intensified.

Victoria Turk reports for WIRED, in an article republished by Ars Technica, that DeepMind's cyclone model surprised the weather scientists now using it. In "Operational Tropical Cyclone Forecasting with AI," published in Nature on August 6, Ferran Alet and colleagues at Google DeepMind and Google Research, with National Hurricane Center co-authors, introduce WeatherNext Cyclones. The model forecasts cyclone tracks, intensity, and wind structure worldwide up to 15 days ahead. Evaluated on storms from 2023 through 2025, its predictions averaged at least one day more lead time than leading operational models, a gain DeepMind's announcement equates to a decade of meteorological progress. "Time is really golden when it comes to those types of decisions," the center's director, Michael Brennan, tells Turk.

Turk explains that hurricane prediction spans two physical scales: global atmospheric currents shape a storm's track, while intensity depends on fine-grained conditions near its core. Earlier AI systems handled tracks well but struggled with intensity. DeepMind co-trained WeatherNext on global weather data and the IBTrACS archive of nearly 5,000 historical storms, then fed it inputs on a 28 kilometre grid, by DeepMind's count 100 times coarser than traditional intensity models require. Alet calls the model a black box because researchers cannot yet explain why that resolution suffices; the paper suggests coarse atmospheric data "contains more intensity signal than previously recognised".

DeepMind's May account of Hurricane Melissa shows the model in live use. When Melissa formed in October 2025, models split between a weak Haiti landfall and rapid intensification toward Jamaica. WeatherNext assigned an 80 percent chance to a Category 5 Jamaican landfall five days ahead, helping the National Hurricane Center make its first prediction that a storm then at Category 1 would reach Category 5. Melissa became the strongest hurricane on record to hit Jamaica, and DeepMind says the center's annual verification report rated WeatherNext the season's "top-performing individual model for track and intensity". Kate Musgrave, a paper co-author at the Cooperative Institute for Research in the Atmosphere, tells Turk that retrospective results had looked too good to trust. The ensemble has since grown from 50 scenarios per storm to 1,000, a scale Musgrave says numerical models cannot match with existing computing.

Brennan cautions that one season's best model may falter in the next, while human forecasters must still translate tracks and intensity into consequences: "it's the impacts that kill people." DeepMind also open-sourced code and weights for WeatherNext Cyclones, its October successor WeatherNext 2, and a compact WeatherNext 2-mini that runs on a single TPU. The company says it is extending the collaboration to weather agencies in the Philippines, Taiwan, Indonesia, and Vietnam.
