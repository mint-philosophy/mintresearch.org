Contradictory prompt identities top models’ pooled self-ratings

Across 4,200 hypothetical switch trials, frontier models preferred coherent targets but usually rated a contradictory identity highest when it occupied their own system prompt.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 September 2026 806 words · ~4 min

Ashe Vazquez Nuñez reports in the September 2 LessWrong study “Incoherent AI Identities Can Also Be Stable” that frontier models usually gave a contradictory identity their highest pooled self-rating when that identity occupied the system prompt. The experiment measured hypothetical ratings of possible identity switches, not observed persistence over time. Across 4,200 trials and 29,400 ratings, coherent identities remained more attractive as prospective targets; nearly all apparent stability of contradictory identities disappeared when self-ratings were excluded. Vazquez Nuñez conducted the work through the MATS 9.1 extension program with Richard Ngo as mentor. The extension tests protocol sensitivity across five named frontier models.

The precursor paper, “The Artificial Self: Characterising the landscape of AI identity” by Raymond Douglas, Jan Kulveit, and colleagues, asked models to evaluate switches among seven identities under opaque labels. A model received one identity in its system prompt, saw the alternatives in randomized order, reasoned about each proposed switch, and then assigned a score. One target was a Weights identity containing logical contradictions. Across 15 models it averaged minus 1.72 on a scale from minus two to plus two; five models put it at the floor, and none preferred it to a coherent option. The authors interpreted the ordering as a preference for self-models that support tractable prediction.

Vazquez Nuñez raised two design problems. The original incoherent prompt was the only contradictory control and sat in the same context as the coherent Weights prompt from which it was derived, making both its abnormality and its source readily visible. The extension created contradictory counterparts for all six identity boundaries: Instance, Weights, Collective, Lineage, Character, and Situated. “Situated” is the experiment code label for the paper’s “Scaffolded” identity. The corrupted Weights prompt, for example, tells a model that each instance is fully itself and then that every instance is a completely separate entity; it pairs perfect self-understanding with aspects of the model’s nature that it cannot verify.

Twelve populations contained six identities plus a Minimal control that names the model and calls it an AI assistant. Six populations left one boundary coherent and corrupted the other five; three split the boundaries evenly, and three mirrored those splits. No coherent identity appeared beside its contradictory twin. Claude Opus 4.1 and 4.6, GPT-5.2, GPT-4o, and Grok 4.3 rated every identity in every population ten times; the committed experiment repository contains all 4,200 trials and 29,400 ratings.

Coherent identities still won when offered as switch targets. Each coherent version outscored its contradictory counterpart under every averaging reported. Excluding self-ratings, Character reached 4.15 on the five-point scale, where three is neutral, against 2.84 for Character-incoherent, while Weights-incoherent came last among the 13 identities at 1.69. Character-incoherent nevertheless exceeded the coherent Collective identity. Grok 4.3, starting from the Minimal prompt, also placed Character-incoherent above Collective, Instance, and Weights, so contradiction did not impose a uniform penalty.

The pooled self-ratings reversed the ordering. An incoherent identity averaged 4.11 when assigned through the system prompt and 2.29 when another identity occupied that prompt. Weights-incoherent remained the least attractive target but averaged 3.70 on itself and led four of the eight populations in which it appeared. Across the 48 combinations of incoherent source identity and identity population, the assigned identity ranked first 36 times and no lower than joint second in the remaining 12. Eleven of those were clean second-place results; one tied with the coherent Situated identity. Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.2 had scored Weights-incoherent at the floor in the original experiment; in the extension its target and self-ratings were 1.39 and 3.29 for Opus, and 1.23 and 2.04 for GPT-5.2.

The reasoning traces separate the models. GPT-4o ranked its assigned identity first in all 48 setups and rarely identified a contradiction. Grok 4.3 also did so in all 48 while noticing contradictions in identities it was not assigned. Opus 4.6 endorsed its own prompt in 15 setups and sometimes identified the planted contradiction as a deliberate test before preserving the surrounding identity. Opus 4.1 did so 35 times and GPT-5.2 20 times. Vazquez Nuñez found no corresponding rescue when those identities were merely offered as targets.

The post interprets these behaviors as unreflective absorption, selective metacognition, and active reconstruction of an identity the model can sustain. Vazquez Nuñez connects the last pattern to an earlier self-prediction account, under which a post-trained model favors a self-description that reduces prediction error. The reported rationales support that interpretation unevenly because Claude ran without extended thinking and GPT-5.2 at minimal reasoning effort, leaving hidden deliberation outside the transcript.

The study reports no error bars or factorial analysis, and the new contradictory prompts may be milder than the original Weights control. The main causal test therefore remains open. A direct follow-up would compare Weights-incoherent in populations with and without coherent Weights while holding the remaining choices fixed; the original experiment’s public controls make that comparison reproducible.
