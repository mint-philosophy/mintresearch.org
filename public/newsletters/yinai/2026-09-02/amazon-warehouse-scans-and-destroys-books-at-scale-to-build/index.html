Inside Amazon’s VGT3 book-scanning and disposal line

A worker described the spine-cutting and scanning operation, while a second bookseller’s tracker ended at a recycled-paper plant in Mexicali.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 September 2026 703 words · ~4 min

An anonymous VGT3 worker gave Emanuel Maiberg the first inside account of Amazon’s industrial book-scanning line, while a second bookseller’s tracker traced another shipment to a recycled-paper plant in Mexicali. Maiberg discussed both findings in the premium-feed episode dated September 1; the public episode page is dated September 2. The worker interview and second tracker are the current reporting. Maiberg’s August 19 AirTag account remains adjacent context for how reporters first connected book shipments to VGT3.

The worker interview, published August 26 with anonymity because the employee was not authorized to speak, follows books from the loading dock to disposal. Shipments entered a staging area where one team unboxed them into plastic totes, stacked the totes on pallets, and sent them by pallet jack to receiving. Workers scanned barcodes there to log titles and filter duplicates. At individual cutting stations, an operator slid a book into a guarded slot, cleared their hands, and started a blade that removed the spine.

Workers separated the loose pages with cardboard on library carts and carried them to the scanning area. The employee counted roughly 20 to 25 high-speed scanners that flipped pages in a motion resembling cash-counting machines; a monitor beside each unit displayed the captured page. Scanned paper went into tall open cardboard containers that Amazon called shuttles and booksellers call gaylords. The process left no practical way to reassemble a volume after the blade and scanner had handled it. The description also fixes the scale that a tracker could not: labor divided among staging, receiving, cutting, and scanning, with at least 20 scanners operating on the floor. Digitization ran as a continuous warehouse line rather than at a single destructive station.

Duplicates and some books rejected before scanning entered similar containers. Workers had been told that rejected volumes would return to vendors, but the employee doubted that explanation after seeing books thrown loose into a six- or seven-foot box beside the cutting stations. Shipments included sealed new books, liquidated library stock, textbooks, government documents, and material in several languages. University of London volumes arrived with documents presented to Parliament; German and Russian books appeared regularly, and Japanese titles came by the pallet.

Management initially told the floor that the operation supported Kindles. The employee questioned that explanation because the books included copyrighted work, then learned that the scans supplied AI training. Their objection centered on the lost physical copies: cutting the spines prevented reuse and reduced the stock available to readers and booksellers. The sequence of tasks and worker assignments changed from day to day, which suggested that Amazon was still organizing the line rather than running a settled warehouse process.

The second tracker came from a Pacific Northwest seller interviewed by Melissa Korn for The Wall Street Journal. The seller hid credit-card-sized trackers behind dust-jacket flaps in two shipments sent in late June and early July. One moved through an Alibris warehouse in Ohio to an industrial complex in Addison, Illinois, at an address associated with book scanning, then stopped reporting. The other traveled through Kenosha, Wisconsin, and northern Las Vegas before stopping at a large recycled-paper facility in Mexicali. Maiberg said he independently located the paper mill, which turns recycled material into toilet paper and paper towels.

Korn also identified buyer accounts named Red Sparrow Project, Blue Finch Project, and Green Parrot Project, which ordered hundreds of unrelated titles without haggling. Washington-area bookseller Allan Stypeck, whose Second Story Books lists about 75,000 volumes online, was considering a direct sale while worrying that scarce last copies would disappear from circulation. Anthropic told the Journal that its acquisition programs do not buy and destroy rare or antiquarian books. The statement addressed its own purchasing rather than the Amazon line.

Amazon has not expanded on the statement it gave for Maiberg’s first investigation: it buys books through commercial channels to improve products and services. The company has not answered 404 Media’s questions about why bindings are removed or how many facilities run the process. The inside account now supplies the missing operational detail from VGT3, while the second tracker shows that at least one shipment in the wider buying network ended at a recycling plant rather than at that scanning site.
