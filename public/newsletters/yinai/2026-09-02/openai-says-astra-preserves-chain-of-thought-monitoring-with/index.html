Astra turns chain-of-thought monitoring into a three-way dispute

Pachocki says Astra remains monitorable, Krueger says OpenAI abandoned the technique, and Raschka argues layer recurrence need not hide visible reasoning.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 September 2026 424 words · ~2 min

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki said that current frontier models, including Astra, remain within twice GPT-4’s computation-graph depth and that OpenAI has worked to preserve chain-of-thought monitoring since its first reasoning models. Digg relayed the post on September 2 and noted that OpenAI had not confirmed whether Astra uses recurrent depth. Pachocki also said monitoring is deteriorating for reasons he considers independent of architectural changes. His statement adds an explicit monitorability claim and a quantitative depth bound to the September 1 report of Astra’s recurrent depth.

David Krueger argued on X that chain-of-thought visibility had supplied useful evidence of model misalignment but was always too fragile to serve as a lasting safety backstop. He went further than Pachocki, writing that OpenAI had abandoned the technique. Krueger’s quote-post points to Joshua Achiam’s precursor, which rejected safety strategies that require human-legible reasoning and called for interpretability methods that reach beyond chain-of-thought fidelity. Achiam declined to confirm or deny the architecture report; his relevance here is the safety premise Krueger adopted, not a separate claim about Astra’s design.

Sebastian Raschka addressed the architecture rather than OpenAI’s safety policy. In an X thread and matching technical note, he described a looped transformer as repeated use of the same layer stack. Hidden states pass through shared layers more than once, increasing effective depth and computation without increasing parameter count or duplicating stored weights. Weight storage therefore remains fixed. The design does not keep all memory fixed, however: the key-value cache grows as recurrent computation adds intermediate states.

Raschka’s open-weight example, Nanbeige4.2-3B, uses 22 layers twice according to its released configuration. Nanbeige Lab reports that the model was trained from scratch on 28 trillion tokens and that two passes retain about 75% of a standard transformer’s token efficiency. Additional passes produced marginal gains while slowing training and making optimization less stable. The related Mixture-of-Recursions paper routes individual tokens to different recursion depths rather than imposing one fixed count.

Raschka argues that recurrence does not inherently conceal visible reasoning because the extra computation happens in hidden states before each emitted token, as it does in an ordinary deeper model. A recurrent model could still emit fewer intermediate reasoning tokens and move more work into latent activations; scaling a conventional model can produce the same effect. He proposes comparing looped and unlooped models on reasoning tasks both already solve, then measuring how many tokens each emits. That experiment would test the architectural concern directly while leaving Krueger’s broader objection to chain-of-thought as a durable safety strategy unresolved.
