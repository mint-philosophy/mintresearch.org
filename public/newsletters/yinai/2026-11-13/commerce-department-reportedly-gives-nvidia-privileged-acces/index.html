Nvidia's lawyer annotated draft H200 export rules

The Times details Nvidia's access to Commerce rulemaking. Semafor reconstructs its demands for weaker customer checks; Commerce says consultation and interagency review followed normal practice.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 November 2026 967 words · ~5 min

Nvidia's lawyer inserted comments into draft H200 export rules, and Commerce officials routed the company's business through a deputy who rarely acted without Secretary Howard Lutnick's approval, the New York Times reports. Ana Swanson, Tripp Mickle, Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan's November 12 investigation draws on current and former officials discussing internal deliberations anonymously. They describe privileged access within the department responsible for restricting advanced-chip sales to China: Lutnick frequently called and messaged Jensen Huang and hosted private meetings; Jeff Kessler, who heads the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), required communications with Nvidia to pass through him.

When staff reviewed draft policies, they sometimes encountered comments from Tim Teter, Nvidia's general counsel. His H200 annotations could have weakened requirements to identify chip buyers and sought to influence shipment volumes. The Times describes officials regarding this access as unusual even for a department that routinely consults industry. A Commerce spokesman said consultation was normal, denied directing staff to avoid investigating any company, and said the agency continued to consider new export-blacklist entries with other departments.

The customer checks Nvidia challenged

Garrison Lovely, Ashley Gold and Rohan Goswami's November 13 Semafor investigation, with additional reporting by Reed Albergotti, reconstructs a January licensing call. Lovely also excerpted it in Obsolete. People with access to a detailed account said Teter told more than a dozen officials that he had been present when Trump and Huang discussed the matter. “He wants this to go fast,” Teter reportedly said.

The proposed H200 license conditions required sellers to identify cloud customers before shipping and conduct know-your-customer checks on buyers purchasing more than a few thousand chips. Teter called the cloud-customer requirement a “poison pill.” Nvidia wanted the purchaser threshold raised to tens of thousands of chips, worth billions of dollars, and end-of-life conditions limited to chips subject to export controls. Semafor does not specify those end-of-life requirements. It also describes an exchange its reporters viewed in which Teter accused officials developing restrictions of misrepresenting Nvidia's products and Trump's policies.

The January 15 final rule moved H200-class exports to China and Macau to case-by-case licensing review. Applicants must submit buyers' procedures for preventing prohibited remote access and identify intended cloud-service users in specified countries. Aggregate shipments to China and Macau cannot exceed half the volume shipped for US end use, and every shipment requires independent testing in the United States. The published rule contains neither a numeric purchaser threshold for customer checks nor an end-of-life condition. Individual license terms are not established by these reports, and neither outlet shows which requested changes Nvidia secured. Semafor dates the call only to January; its relationship to the rule's publication remains unclear.

Nvidia's filing for the quarter ended April 26 says licenses began in February for small quantities destined for specified Chinese customers. The required US inspection involved importing chips into the country, attracting a 25 percent tariff; Nvidia reported no revenue under the program by the filing date. Our November 7 report examined these conditions and approvals for companies connected to blacklisted firms.

BIS told Semafor that consultation was “completely routine,” commercial considerations could legitimately change conditions, and State, Defense, Energy and Commerce reviewed final terms. Nvidia underwent the same process as other applicants, the spokesperson said. Nvidia declined comment to both outlets; the White House did not respond.

Delayed controls and enforcement disputes

The Times reports that State and Treasury supported restricting Chinese access to Southeast Asian computing capacity in October, with Trump's approval. Officials had persuaded Oracle to cancel a $7 billion chip-leasing deal with Tencent. Kessler then raised Lutnick's objections, and Lutnick questioned the urgency and warned of renewed trade conflict. The administration had rolled back the Biden-era AI diffusion framework. Commerce said it was considering replacement remote-access rules; the Wall Street Journal reports a rule being finalized would add seven countries, including Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Singapore, to a group facing export restrictions and licensing requirements.

According to the Times, Commerce had issued no new entity listings in over a year, its longest gap in 18 years, after a September 2025 expansion provoked Chinese rare-earth restrictions and was suspended in a trade agreement. Some officials said managers discouraged Nvidia investigations and directed attention toward smaller customers. The Megaspeed inquiry was transferred to Justice after Teter contacted Commerce investigators invoking Kessler's name, an episode described in the later update to our November 8 issue. The Times also reports Nvidia-funded opposition research into effective-altruism groups, coordinated by Ned Finkle, and a RAND contractor's dismissal after Nvidia complained about delayed China licenses.

The Times describes Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confronting Lutnick over the failure to blacklist Megaspeed. Commerce denied that exchange and denied that Trump's relationship with Huang inhibited enforcement. Its spokeswoman cited criminal chip-smuggling investigations and restrictions on Nvidia's H20. In his November 13 CNBC interview, Bessent said Treasury, Defense, State and the trade representative's office had supported listing Megaspeed for months, while Commerce was responsible for acting.

Semafor's congressional sources describe another dispute over the 2025 Chip Security Act. They say Nvidia called chip-location verification technically infeasible while privately demonstrating a feature that launched in May. A former official also described Huang and Teter's aggressive treatment of critics. Customer identification has a longer policy history: Janet Egan and Lennart Heim's 2023 arXiv paper Oversight for Frontier AI through a Know-Your-Customer Scheme for Compute Providers proposed banking-style checks above a computing threshold; Commerce proposed foreign cloud-customer identity checks in January 2024.

The Journal reports that Tim Scott requested testimony from Huang and Lutnick for December 3, while Ron Wyden and Richard Neal opened an investigation and sought preservation of records. Commerce subsequently told CNBC that Lutnick had agreed to appear voluntarily before the Banking Committee. The White House told the Journal that Trump retained confidence in Lutnick.
