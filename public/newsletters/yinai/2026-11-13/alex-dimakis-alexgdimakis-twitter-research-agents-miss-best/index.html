Research agents leave better approaches untried

Bespoke Labs finds that 58% of research runs missed the method family behind their task’s best observed solution. Its analysis separates trying a different approach from repeated tuning.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 13 November 2026 945 words · ~5 min

AI research agents can spend a day improving an early idea without trying the approach that produced a better solution elsewhere in the same experiment. In “How models explore ideas,” published on November 10, Anirudha Ramesh and colleagues at Bespoke Labs report that 58% of runs never visited the family of methods behind their task’s best observed solution. Their coauthors include Alex Dimakis. The comparison concerns the best solution found in the study; the researchers have not established the best possible solution to each problem.

The team examined 348 runs by 12 models on AutoResearchExam, a benchmark of 29 machine-learning research and engineering tasks released in September. Agents work for 24 hours in a shared software harness, Terminus 2, receiving a validation score and their remaining time after each submission. They never see the hidden test scores used for evaluation. The benchmark averages those hidden scores over time, using the submission with the best validation result so far; finding a useful solution early earns more credit than reaching it near the deadline.

Ramesh and colleagues wanted to examine something that this score leaves unresolved: whether an agent improves by developing one method or by trying substantially different methods. They call a family of similar approaches an “idea basin,” borrowing the term from Ziyang Cai and colleagues’ arXiv paper Fork-and-Flush: Escaping Idea Basins in Autoresearch Agents. Tuning a gradient-boosted model and training a small neural network could occupy different basins even if they achieve similar scores.

Identifying changes of approach

The researchers grouped submissions by their code’s method without using scores. Sonnet first summarized each submission; Bespoke’s Nimble model then judged which of two summaries was more similar to a third, without being told which agent produced them. The researchers used those judgments to place submissions on a two-dimensional map for each task and divide it into method families. Consecutive submissions with identical code were removed, leaving 9,179 submissions for analysis.

The appendix checks whether the map reproduces similarity judgments withheld during its construction. It gets 89% right, compared with 60% for a baseline that knows only which run produced a submission. That supports the claim that the map captures more than run identity, but the check still measures agreement with Nimble. The article reports no human validation of the resulting categories and links no analysis dataset or code with which to examine alternative groupings. Its exploration measures therefore depend on the model summaries, similarity judgments and grouping procedure.

The blog illustrates these families with a task requiring agents to accelerate transformer decoder computations on CPUs. Agents may combine operations, reuse memory or exploit fixed weights, while keeping outputs within strict numerical limits; one inaccurate test instance reduces the submission’s score to zero. Claude Opus 5.5 moved early from ordinary NumPy computation to writing its own just-in-time compiler. Gemini 3.8 Flash settled within two hours on NumPy supplemented with assembly kernels and spent the remaining time tuning that approach. Qwen3.8 Max stayed with automatically tuned matrix-library calls. These are differences in implementation strategy that a score alone would not reveal.

New approaches and repeated tuning

Across the study, 47% of runs never left their first method family. When agents did switch, half the switches returned to an approach already tried. More submissions did not reliably mean broader exploration: the most frequent submitters made roughly as many changes of approach as lower-volume models and used the extra attempts for refinement. Submission volume also tracked a larger gap between validation and hidden test performance, consistent with agents adapting too closely to the feedback they could see.

A move to a previously untried family was followed by about nine times the validation-score gain of an ordinary refinement step. Returning to an old family produced gains similar to staying put. The advantage was concentrated early: after the first six hours, switches and refinements produced similar gains. Runs that switched after the halfway point did not gain more hidden test score in the remaining time than those that stayed. Among models, directing a greater share of switches toward new methods was associated with better scores; switching frequently by itself was not.

Agents chose their own switches, so these comparisons cannot establish what would happen if a harness forced them to change approach. A model might switch when it has already identified a promising alternative. The headline 58% result has a separate measurement ambiguity: the article does not specify whether validation or hidden test performance identifies the best observed solution, or whether the percentage uses all 348 runs or the 338 with enough distinct submissions for its switch-rate analysis. It supports the finding that many agents leave a successful approach untried, without fixing that percentage’s precise denominator.

Cai and colleagues tested a related intervention in Fork-and-Flush: periodically create parallel branches that inherit the workspace but begin with fresh conversations, then continue from the best branch. Their abstract reports gains over single-run and independent parallel baselines at equal compute across 13 tasks. Shangyin Tan and colleagues’ arXiv paper Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting, also coauthored by Dimakis, similarly tests restarting from the best candidate after discarding accumulated sessions and candidate history. Those experiments give reasons to investigate context resets, but Bespoke did not test either intervention on AutoResearchExam.

AutoResearchExam already compresses the conversation into a handoff summary before each new experiment while retaining the workspace and previous experiment history. The exploration analysis shows that agents can remain within a narrow method family under that arrangement. Bespoke proposes encouraging broader exploration early and sustaining useful changes later. Testing that proposal would require comparing modified agents or harnesses under matched budgets and checking whether the extra changes improve hidden test performance.
