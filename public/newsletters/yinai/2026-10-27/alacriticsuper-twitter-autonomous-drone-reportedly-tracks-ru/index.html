Purdue's forest-following drone draws scrutiny over software credit

The video shows a drone following a person between trees and names Hong Kong open-source tools in its telemetry. Aniket Bera's edited post acknowledges using open-source software and promises a preprint on the lab's own algorithms.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 October 2026 672 words · ~3 min

Purdue's IDEAS Lab has demonstrated a drone following a person through a forest, which lab director Aniket Bera describes as unmapped and without GPS. Bera, an associate professor of computer science, posted the 44-second video on LinkedIn on October 23, writing that his lab had “developed every layer of this autonomous drone from scratch”. His edited post also acknowledges “many open-source software tools” and says the lab's novel planning and perception algorithms are under review, with a preprint promised. The edit was present when checked on October 28; its timing is unspecified.

Bera describes a system that estimates its own movement, maps nearby obstacles, predicts where an obscured person will go and plans a flight path that keeps the person visible. He argues that these operations must happen onboard: when a drone banks to avoid a branch, its sensors' view changes, and waiting for processing elsewhere would delay its response. His post includes the aircraft design and airframe manufacture among the lab's work. It presents local sensing and computation as necessary for following someone through clutter while staying within the aircraft's limits on power, weight and heat.

The video shows the forward camera view beside a colored LiDAR map, with a green box marking the person being followed. Charts below show speed, height and distance to the person. The labels include “SUPER commanded” and “altitude vs take-off (FAST-LIO)”. The ground-speed trace reaches approximately three metres per second in the displayed autonomous-flight segments, according to the footage's charts. Those labels identify software used for the navigation estimates and commands; the footage does not show the aircraft's physical construction. They also give a more specific account of its components than the broad claim of development from scratch.

The SUPER repository belongs to Fu Zhang's MaRS Lab at the University of Hong Kong. Yunfan Ren and colleagues described the system in the 2025 Science Robotics paper “Safety-assured high-speed navigation for MAVs”. Its planner considers a fast route extending into unknown space alongside a safer route through space already observed to be clear. The repository also describes previous object-tracking applications by day and night. The same lab's FAST-LIO software combines laser-range measurements with an inertial sensor to estimate the aircraft's movement and build a map. SUPER includes ROG-Map, which represents nearby space as occupied or free for planning. These packages address movement and navigation; identifying a person and predicting their path are additional parts of the demonstrated task.

In an October 26 DroneXL article, Haye Kesteloo questioned the from-scratch wording and called for credit to the MaRS Lab. Kesteloo endorsed reusing those packages and argued that Purdue should name their developers. He reports that Kevin Biju Mathew, whom he identifies as a Purdue graduate researcher, said in LinkedIn comments that the system uses LiDAR-inertial odometry and a ROG map. Bera's post lists visual-inertial odometry instead. Kesteloo also reports Bera's explanation that a prediction module estimates the person's path when trees obscure them or tracking becomes noisy. The published material does not yet specify how Purdue's algorithms modify or work with SUPER.

The selected X post by Praveen Kumar Verma recirculated the clip on October 25, using a video shared by Sadao Tokuyama. Verma emphasized potential search-and-rescue uses. That application was his interpretation; Bera's demonstration post describes the navigation task without proposing it. Tokuyama's Japanese-language caption described LiDAR-based mapping and cautioned that practical performance and safety needed checking. Neither relay adds an evaluation of how consistently the drone maintains contact when the person disappears from view.

Purdue has separately announced a five-year, $1.5 million Army Research Laboratory agreement for Bera's lab to develop air-ground robot teams that map and navigate without GPS. The November 2025 announcement names scouting, threat reconnaissance and tactical surveillance among the intended applications, with drones gathering information for ground robots. It does not connect this forest video to the agreement, and Bera's post names no funder. Purdue describes the funded project as a move from its earlier research on single robots toward teams that share information and coordinate their movements.
