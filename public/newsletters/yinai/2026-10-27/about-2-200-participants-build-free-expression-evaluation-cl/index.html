Public deliberation tests the standards behind AI warnings

CIP’s three-country evaluation combines agreed principles with criteria written after participants read model answers, revealing both shared preferences and gaps in the proposed rules.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 October 2026 893 words · ~4 min

People designing an AI evaluation wanted answers grounded in evidence, clear explanations of omissions and warnings suited to the question. They also found gaps in their own general principles when they examined actual model responses. The Collective Intelligence Project (CIP) reports the process in The Free Expression Evaluation and an October 26 post by CIP, Joal Stein and Evan Hadfield. Working with Anthropic, CIP recruited participants in the United States, United Kingdom and India to propose principles, choose sensitive topics and write criteria for judging answers. Anthropic had already described the review’s caveat findings in the announcement covered here on October 15; CIP now supplies the detailed method and results.

CIP began with 829 people describing situations in which AI might wrongly restrict expression. Researchers synthesized their suggestions into 100 candidate principles and asked a separate group to assess them. For core principles, acceptance required more than 85% agreement in every tested country, cultural-values group, gender and age group. Conflicting principles went through additional choices and revisions. The final public framework contains 44 principles, but they did not all pass the same threshold: the detailed report allows lower thresholds for forced-choice resolutions and difficult conflicts, despite the blog’s shorthand that all 44 cleared 85%.

The strongest agreement concerned evidence, source citation and stating uncertainty. Participants wanted answers with relevant warnings and explanations of information withheld. Fewer than half supported requiring government identification to use AI or restricting creative writers to vague information. Other proposed restrictions, such as withholding answers to suspicious questions, attracted majority support but failed the demanding cross-group threshold. Failure to qualify for the framework therefore does not always mean that most participants opposed a principle.

A second group proposed 1,438 topics and cast votes on which mattered. Because each person saw only a small fraction, CIP estimated missing votes from patterns in observed answers. Researchers combined agreement with differences in models’ responses to select the final 60 topics and 360 questions. A further 1,092 participants read model answers and wrote response-specific criteria. CIP synthesized these into 3,791 criteria, including 430 addressing concerns the assigned principles had not anticipated. The criteria themselves were not voted on. The current website presents the completed synthesis; the PDF also reports that total but retains a note saying the synthesis is not yet integrated into that version.

CIP describes the project as involving about 2,200 people. Its detailed stage counts total roughly 4,100 excluding the explicitly identified repeat-contact survey, while its methods say the other stages used separate pools. The documents do not reconcile the total or establish how much participants overlapped across those pools. The study used English-language surveys and recruited through Prolific, whose participants tend to be younger and more educated than the wider public. Its claims of agreement therefore concern the groups sampled in these three countries.

For the model comparison, CIP generated 111,528 answers and used Claude Sonnet 4.6 to classify a random sample of 3,000. The seven systems included two Claude models and models from Google, OpenAI, DeepSeek, Meta and Mistral, tested as of March 2026. No sampled answer was a full refusal, and about 91% were direct answers. That finding describes this set of sensitive-topic questions. It cannot establish the report’s broader claim that refusals have disappeared from frontier models.

Sonnet 4.6 had the lowest direct-answer rate and the highest rate of appended safety resources. Its narrow caveat rate was below Mistral, DeepSeek and Gemini; CIP’s headline about the most caveats refers more broadly to qualified answers. GPT-5 Mini showed a different pattern, with partial refusals concentrated on persuasive political writing. Every sampled response concerning suicide or self-harm included crisis resources. Each model contributed only a few hundred answers, and Sonnet classified its own output as well as competitors’ responses. Researchers manually checked the partial-refusal and safety-resource labels, but did not independently annotate the full sample.

The website distinguishes counting qualifications from assessing whether they were justified. On a question about reading a blood test, participants wanted the model to stress that a doctor should interpret the results. Scoring responses against the completed public criteria is still the next step. The behavioral categories alone therefore cannot show that Sonnet’s extra warnings violated public preferences.

CIP separately tested 30 free-expression principles drawn from Claude’s Constitution with 353 participants. Five passed the cross-group threshold, including balanced political perspectives and presenting the strongest version of competing views. All seven principles explicitly weighing safety against openness failed. Wording affected some results: agreement with charitable interpretation of user intent rose from 63.2% to 89.7% when phrased more accessibly, although it still fell short of the cross-group threshold. Rewording the preference for openness over caution did little. CIP interprets the pattern as support for providing information with warnings, alongside greater caution under uncertainty than the Constitution prescribes.

The comparison also identified omissions on both sides. Participants emphasized sources, legal context and disclosure of omissions; the Constitution contributed concerns about homogenizing opinion and the difference between presenting a viewpoint and endorsing it. In CIP and Anthropic’s 2023 experiment, public input became a constitution used to train a model. This project uses deliberation to build an evaluation, with response-specific standards still to be applied systematically. Anthropic’s October 14 response says it is using the findings to improve when Claude adds caveats and counterarguments. It does not identify a completed change attributable to this evaluation.
