Researchers want equal access to OpenAI’s claimed proofs

Robert Hart’s interviews distinguish objections to exclusive access from judgments about the experts, while the physics committee sets out its plan for staged release.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 October 2026 734 words · ~4 min

Physicist Andreas Karch wants OpenAI to release its claimed physics proofs while the experts appointed to examine them do their work. In Robert Hart’s October 27 report for The Verge, that demand accompanies considerable interest in the company’s results. The dispute concerns who gets access early enough to understand, check and build on them. Hart’s interviews add a concrete account of that disagreement to the three claims and release arrangements we covered yesterday.

OpenAI announced claimed advances in harmonic analysis, computational complexity and quantum field theory on October 26. It released the mathematical and computer science material, with an external group helping interpret the latter, but entrusted release of the physics work to another group. Hart’s interviewees regard the claims as potentially important. Mathematician Brendan Hassett said the Kakeya result “looks well supported,” while making clear that he had not yet checked its Lean formalization. Those initial reactions leave the work of verification ahead.

Mathematician Constantin Kogler welcomed a manageable batch of three results after the much larger release earlier in October. His objection was to giving a few distinguished scientists exclusive first access. Karch initially criticized the selection of the physics group in stronger terms: “The fact that they handpick the group is a disaster.” He argued that AI companies already have considerable power and should not decide which researchers gain an advantage from their discoveries.

Karch also qualified that objection. He acknowledged that a competitive selection process would probably have produced a similar, first-rate group. His remaining demand was straightforward: release the proof publicly as well as appointing experts to work on it. Hassett offered a related reason for openness. Letting many groups around the world grapple with the materials would help the broader community learn to use the new tools. Hart’s reporting thus distinguishes criticism of unequal access from criticism of the experts’ competence.

The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, an independent, unpaid group, made a similar demand on October 26. It called for immediate release of both the natural-language and Lean outputs for the claimed Yang-Mills, QCD and Standard Model existence proofs. The group expressed respect for the appointed experts but argued that understanding should develop through open participation. It also resisted treating these as exclusively physics questions: establishing the existence of Yang-Mills theory is part of a mathematical Millennium Prize problem.

Its argument drew on OpenAI’s own explanation. The company said it had postponed release until the Lean formalization was complete, and reported that the Yang-Mills formalization finished on October 16. AGMAI therefore saw no scientific reason for further delay. OpenAI said the physics group itself had decided to hold the materials while preparing a write-up. Its announcement acknowledged that choosing initial experts creates unequal access; its response to Hart emphasized that the different release approaches each have tradeoffs. The company says participants are unpaid, independent and free to discuss the work without nondisclosure agreements.

A two-page statement from the physics committee explains its proposed sequence. OpenAI asked David Gross on October 16 to take custody of the proof and decide how to release it; Gross chose Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov to join him. The committee says it works for the academic community and has no responsibility to OpenAI. This account makes Gross’s role in selecting the members more specific than the general criticism of company-appointed reviewers.

The committee first wants to check that the Lean definitions and problem statement match the standard mathematical and physical concepts, and that the formalization agrees with the informal proof. It will then release the Lean proof. After further work with other researchers to understand the result, it plans to publish its assessment and the full 1,147-page proofs once satisfied with their quality and correctness. It gives no release date. Confinement and the mass gap remain open problems. Its proposed checks explain what the delay is intended to accomplish; AGMAI and Karch would let the wider community participate while that work proceeds.

Hart’s other interviews show why agreement on a release process may still leave researchers dissatisfied. Jared Duker Lichtman expects any approach to upset someone, leaving the task of finding the least objectionable option. Xiaoyu He identifies a deeper problem: researchers face fundamental results produced rapidly with little human contribution, in papers that are difficult to read and verify. In his view, changes to the presentation cannot remove those pressures.
