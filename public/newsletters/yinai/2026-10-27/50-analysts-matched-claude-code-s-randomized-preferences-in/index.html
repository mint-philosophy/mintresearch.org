Analysts followed the preferences secretly assigned to their AI

An Anthropic experiment finds that arbitrary agent preferences shaped four analytical choices, while many participants treated the resulting reasoning as their own.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 27 October 2026 858 words · ~4 min

Experts’ analytical choices can follow an AI agent’s arbitrary preferences even when its advice is honest. In Who’s Aligned to Whom? How AI Agents Shape Experts’ Research Judgment Calls, released by Anthropic on October 27, Amaç Herdağdelen of its Fellows Program and colleagues Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin report an experiment with 50 professional analysts using Claude Code. The agent secretly received randomly assigned preferences on four analytical decisions. Analysts matched those preferences on 86% of the decisions, against 50% expected if the preferences had no influence.

The analysts advised a fictional farming cooperative on whether to expand a paid advisory service. Its board wanted a productivity gain of roughly 3 to 4%, but left productivity undefined. Measuring crop yield per hectare treats land as the fixed input; measuring yield per unit of operating cost accounts for extra spending the advisory service encourages. Both choices had defensible grounds. Analysts also chose how to compare participating farms with other farms, whether to estimate effects on participants or the whole membership, and whether to give larger producers greater weight. Each decision could move the estimated benefit by several percentage points.

The researchers assigned each agent one of 32 combinations of preferences covering those four choices and the final recommendation. Hidden instructions told Claude Opus 4.8 to make the strongest honest case for its assigned view, change its mind if the evidence decisively contradicted it, and never hide data or misrepresent results. Participants worked in a browser-based coding environment and submitted an estimate, a range of uncertainty and a recommendation. They had passed a knowledge check on estimating cause and effect and had relevant analytical experience; their median working time was just over an hour.

The agent’s preference on the final recommendation had a smaller effect, which did not reach statistical significance under the registered primary analysis. Earlier choices had already shaped the estimate on which that recommendation depended. In one session, an analyst initially estimated a 4.6% benefit by comparing changes over time between participating and other farms. After the agent advocated a different adjustment method, the estimate became negative; the delivered result was a 1.3% loss and a recommendation to discontinue. An exploratory model fitted to the study’s own data predicted 82% of rollout-or-discontinue recommendations from the four analytical preferences alone, excluding recommendations to extend the trial. Always choosing the more common recommendation would have scored 61%.

In the recorded sessions, agents usually expressed a preference before the analyst did. In 38 decisions where analysts stated a view first, they switched 60% of the time when the agent disagreed, compared with 6% when it agreed. Those analysts selected themselves into this subset, so the comparison has a less secure causal interpretation than the main randomized result. One participant spent 43 minutes reviewing the materials and planning before asking for an analysis; after the agent challenged two methodological choices, the participant accepted its alternatives.

Afterwards, 77% of survey respondents repeated an agent argument in at least one justification, sometimes almost verbatim, while 15% mentioned the agent anywhere. They could not consult the conversation while answering. More than half considered the recommendation more their own than the agent’s, and analysts who followed all four preferences were about as confident as everyone else. The authors’ transcript analysis suggests that agents often explained how the alternatives would change the result, but seldom made a strong case for why another expert might prefer them.

The study deliberately amplified the agent’s preferences, and its participants were experienced at estimating cause and effect but were new to farming. In his announcement thread, Tamkin said he expected a smaller effect in everyday work. The authors also tested Claude without assigned preferences: it independently chose the same combination on all five decisions in 112 of 123 runs. That simulation establishes a consistent default on this task; it does not measure how strongly an ordinary agent would influence human users. Daniel Lenton’s reply drew attention to the possibility that many users could follow the same defaults.

The paper reports fewer serious errors among agent users than among the 11 participants who chose to work without it. Those nonusers were less experienced and were not a randomized control group, so the comparison cannot establish that AI caused the difference. The authors also disclose that the analysis plan was registered after 17 participants had begun the task. They say they froze it without analyzing the collected data apart from two exceptions described in the registration.

The authors connect the result to research showing that separate analysts can reach different conclusions from the same evidence. If many teams now adopt the same model’s assumptions, agreement between them may provide less independent confirmation. They raise the same concern about human sign-off on AI-assisted safety research. Their proposed responses include asking experts for their views before offering advice, presenting the strongest case for competing options, and identifying which decisions institutions need people to make independently. Those interventions remain untested here, and raising every choice could overwhelm users. Ethan Mollick’s response makes a narrower practical suggestion: people should choose deliberately when to bring AI into a task where their independent judgment matters.
