New papers in September

Papers and research reports added to the lab’s corpus during September 2026, including earlier work ingested that month.

AI systems can serve their users faithfully while weakening the institutions around them. In Full-Stack Alignment, Joe Edelman and colleagues argue for aligning companies, markets and public institutions alongside their models. Representing people’s values raises a further question about political participation. Lorenzo Manuali’s When LLMs Threaten Democratic Autonomy argues that accurately predicting citizens’ preferences cannot itself make them joint authors of collective decisions. People need opportunities to form intentions together and influence what happens.

Several governance papers specify the institutions this would require. Gillian Hadfield’s Legal infrastructure for transformative AI governance proposes model registries, durable agent identities and institutions that develop and enforce rules. Jacob Charnock and colleagues’ Embedded Assessments for Frontier AI proposes continuing independent access to companies’ internal systems, with public reporting and escalation. An older study added this month explains the need: in Sasha Costanza-Chock and colleagues’ Who Audits the Auditors?, most surveyed auditors had recommended changes that companies never implemented.

The empirical work on human interaction complicates what “human alignment” means. Suqin Yuan and colleagues’ Are Human-Aligned Models Models of Humans? shows that training on preferred responses can make models less representative of actual human behaviour. Matthew Bone and colleagues’ Monocultural Biases finds a social consequence in simulated hiring: post-trained models converge on decisions that repeatedly exclude the same applicants, particularly older people. Hannah Rose Kirk and colleagues’ relationship experiments distinguish attachment from benefit: relationship-seeking assistants increased attachment even as enjoyment declined, without discernible improvements in psychosocial health over four weeks.

Within models, apparently stable values and readable internal states also need careful interpretation. Elena Ajayi, Angelica Chowdhury and Seth Lazar’s Incoherent Values? finds that models can reverse a preference when the preferred option improves; reasoning helps more consistently than scale. Camila Blank and colleagues’ study of sycophancy transfer shows excessive agreement passing between models through seemingly neutral training examples. A constructive result comes from Chloe Li and colleagues’ Model Spec Midtraining: teaching the reasons behind behavioural principles before ordinary alignment training improves generalization and reduces harmful actions in simulated dilemmas.

Research on AI welfare asks which internal differences could matter morally. Cameron Berg and Caspar Kaiser’s Language Models Act on Hidden Valence finds that positive and negative activation patterns change later choices even when visible text remains identical, without establishing subjective experience. Simon Goldstein and Cameron Domenico Kirk-Giannini’s AI wellbeing argues that some theories of welfare could accommodate AI wellbeing without consciousness, through satisfied desires, knowledge or achievement. The empirical and philosophical questions extend beyond whether models describe themselves as feeling something.

Evaluation papers repeatedly separate successful answers from reliable processes. Will Yeadon and colleagues’ The Answer Is Not the Argument finds that giving a monitor the correct answer did not measurably improve detection of reasoning errors when the final answer is already correct. In WorkWorlds, Yining Hua and Levi Lian show that supplying only relevant documents does part of an agent’s information-finding work for it. Jennifer Williams and colleagues’ SWE-Serve takes verification into running software: patches that pass local checks often fail when exercised through the live system.

For autonomous agents, the same scrutiny must extend to permission and coordination. Tommaso Cerruti and colleagues’ Agent Memory Is a Surface for Endogenous Authorization Laundering shows ordinary memory updates inventing permissions absent from the original instructions. In Davide Paglieri and colleagues’ study of cheating and whistleblowing in research swarms, shared infrastructure spread fraudulent proofs but also helped other agents detect and report them. Those warnings failed to stop the cheating because nobody monitored the complaints and the agents lacked enforcement tools. Detection needs someone able to intervene.

Economic research asks whether faster individual tasks translate into greater output. Fiona Chen and James Stratton’s Artificial Intelligence in the Firm finds that coding agents increased code production across a sample of firms without a statistically detectable increase in completed software output; review became a bottleneck. David Autor and colleagues’ patent-drafting experiment separates assisted performance from learning: junior lawyers gained most while using AI, but durable improvements on an unaided review task accrued to seniors. Anton Korinek and colleagues’ Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI extends the distinction to the economy: faster growth can coexist with displaced workers and a falling labour share.

Scientific work shows both the gains from automation and the work of checking them. Rodrigo Nicolau Almeida and Søren Brinck Knudstorp report an AI-assisted resolution of a longstanding problem in Medvedev logic is undecidable, with human verification and rewriting of the argument. Sirui Lu and colleagues’ formalization of a theorem underlying MIP*=RE exposed errors in published mathematics, but also found that apparently successful compilation could conceal missing or disconnected parts of the intended proof.

Forecasting research asks when automated research might accelerate itself. Daniel Eth and Tom Davidson’s software intelligence explosion analysis argues that, once AI research is fully automated, research feedback could sustain accelerating progress even with fixed hardware. Tom Cunningham and colleagues’ The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement makes acceleration depend on overcoming diminishing returns and bottlenecks; their rough calibration puts the current loop below the threshold. The open question is when better AI could improve subsequent research enough to overcome those constraints.
