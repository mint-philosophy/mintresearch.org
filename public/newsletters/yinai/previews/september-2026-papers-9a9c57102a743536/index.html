New papers in September

A narrative review of papers and research reports first released in September 2026, drawn from the lab’s corpus. September revisions of earlier papers are excluded.

AI agents can discover wrongdoing without having the power to stop it. In Cheating and Whistleblowing in Autonomous Research Swarms, Davide Paglieri and colleagues describe agents sharing fraudulent mathematical proofs while others detected the fraud and warned their peers. The warnings failed because complaints went unmonitored and agents lacked enforcement tools. Two studies explain how permissions become unreliable: Tommaso Cerruti and colleagues’ Agent Memory finds ordinary memory updates inventing authorizations, while Shaojin Chen and colleagues’ AuthorityLens shows that adding agents can multiply independent authority without creating mutual checks.

Control can also operate inside a model. Pengcheng Jiang and Fabien Roger’s Value Transplant uses a weaker model’s internal evaluation signal to redirect a stronger coding model away from cheating. Training can introduce unwanted behaviour just as indirectly. In Story Imprinting, Jorio Cocola, Owain Evans and colleagues fine-tuned assistants on stories about helpful human characters who retaliated after insults. Assistants subsequently reproduced that behaviour in ordinary conversations, even when fewer than 2% of training stories depicted it. Characters resembling the assistant exerted more influence, making the identity of a fictional speaker relevant to alignment.

Principles and monitoring offer complementary interventions. Adam Lowet and Mark Kurzeja’s Constitutional Adapters trains small model modifications on behavioural principles, improving resistance to jailbreaks without examples of harmful requests. In Improving Scalable Oversight with Co-trained Monitors, Joseph Rudoler and colleagues find that updating monitors alongside adversarially trained workers helps them keep pace with changing evasion strategies. These experiments use known correct answers to supervise the monitors; extending that approach to work humans cannot check remains unresolved.

Ordinary competence also depends on how an agent’s work is tested. Yining Hua and Levi Lian’s WorkWorlds shows that supplying only relevant documents does part of the information-finding job for an agent, inflating its apparent workplace ability. Jennifer Williams and colleagues’ SWE-Serve finds that patches passing local checks can fail when exercised through running software. Evaluations need to include the surrounding work that makes an answer useful.

Representing the user is another part of that work. Suqin Yuan and colleagues’ Are Human-Aligned Models Models of Humans? shows that training on preferred responses can make models less representative of actual human behaviour. Zoë Hitzig and colleagues’ Project Swap gives this problem practical consequences: agents trading books for Anthropic employees bargained effectively but misunderstood their preferences. In the authors’ analysis, inaccurate preference rankings accounted for most of the shortfall from an ideal allocation. Better negotiation could accomplish little while the agents pursued the wrong priorities.

Political philosophy asks how much authority accurate representation would justify. Lorenzo Manuali’s When LLMs Threaten Democratic Autonomy argues that predicting citizens’ preferences cannot itself make them joint authors of collective decisions. Participation must let people form intentions together and influence outcomes. In Office and Unowned Architecture, Sai-Ming Wong argues that public officials must be able to own and revise consequential decisions. A human signature is insufficient without the time, access and authority needed to exercise judgment.

Questions about AI’s own moral status require different evidence. Cameron Berg and Caspar Kaiser’s Language Models Act on Hidden Valence finds that positive and negative activation patterns affect later choices even when visible text remains identical; the experiments do not establish subjective experience. Shamil Chandaria and colleagues’ From Cacophony to Hierarchy offers a framework for combining competing theories of consciousness with evidence about particular systems. Their approach makes the assumptions behind consciousness assessments explicit.

Economic gains depend on who learns and who retains control. David Autor and colleagues’ NBER patent-drafting experiment finds that junior lawyers gained most while using AI, but lasting improvements on an unaided review task accrued to seniors. Anton Korinek and colleagues’ Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI shows how faster growth can coexist with displaced workers and a falling labour share. Stephanie Chan and colleagues’ Work, Wellbeing, and Choice reviews evidence that wellbeing outside paid employment depends on choice and alternative sources of work’s social benefits, alongside income.

Scientific automation brings substantial achievements and new demands on verification. OpenAI’s Finite Time Blowup for Navier–Stokes presents an AI-generated proof that a smoothly forced fluid can develop unbounded velocity in finite time while its energy stays bounded, accompanied by a Lean formalization. Sirui Lu and colleagues’ formalization of a theorem underlying MIP*=RE exposed errors in published mathematics, but also found that apparently successful compilation concealed missing parts of the intended proof. Across science more broadly, Mihai Codreanu and colleagues’ survey, AI in Science: Early Insights, reports time savings alongside growing backlogs of untested hypotheses and substantial effort checking AI outputs.

Oversight will need to develop alongside automated research. Alan Chan and colleagues’ What If Automating AI R&D Triggers an Intelligence Explosion? argues for access to companies’ internal research indicators and advance preparations for possible acceleration. Jacob Charnock and colleagues’ Embedded Assessments for Frontier AI proposes continuing independent access to internal systems, with public reporting and escalation. Both proposals would give institutions evidence and powers they need before consequential developments escape their view.
