Anthropic's book-swap agents lost more to misread tastes than to bargaining

Anthropic's September 24 report attributes 85% of the shortfall in simulated book trades to inaccurate preference rankings. Stronger negotiators improved outcomes against Claude's estimates but barely helped against participants' own lists.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 October 2026 1298 words · ~6 min

AI agents trading for people lost more value by misreading their tastes than by negotiating poorly in Anthropic's book-swap experiment. Zoë Hitzig and colleagues report the finding in “Project Swap: What happens when agents trade for us?”, published September 24. The summer exchange involved 201 employees; the headline analysis used independent rankings from 188 of them and simulated reruns of the market. The authors attribute 85% of the measured allocation shortfall to inaccurate preference rankings and 15% to bargaining. An October 5 post recirculated the report without adding a new experiment.

Anthropic designed Swap to answer a question its earlier marketplace could not. In “Project Deal,” published April 24, Kevin Troy and colleagues had agents buy and sell goods for 69 employees. Opus 4.5 negotiated better prices than Haiku 4.5, while participants' survey responses did not reveal that disadvantage. But with snowboards, ping-pong balls and free-form haggling, the researchers could not establish the best achievable outcome. Swap gave each person one book to exchange, with no money changing hands, making alternative allocations easier to compare.

Each participant first chatted with Claude about their reading tastes; the median participant typed 216 words. Fable 5 used those conversations to rank every book in each person's office pool. Trading agents received those rankings and the intake transcripts. Two-way swaps and multi-person rotations executed only when everyone involved accepted. All live agents ran on Opus 4.8; half received instructions the researchers called “ruthless,” and half received “prosocial” instructions that also encouraged helping other participants. The team then ran 200 simulations using the same people, books and intake conversations while varying models, instructions and random seeds.

The appendix explains how participants' own rankings were collected after trading, before they learned their outcomes. They ranked ten books, or all books in smaller pools, including the book their agent obtained and the one a centralized exchange would have assigned. Dublin's three-person pool was excluded from most analysis; 188 of the remaining 198 participants submitted rankings. Agents never saw these lists. Claude agreed with participants about which of two books they preferred on 61% of pairs, compared with 50% for random guessing. Longer intake conversations were associated with better agreement.

The researchers scored outcomes from zero for the lowest-ranked book to one for the highest. Allowing for competition over popular books, the best feasible assignment scored 0.89 on participants' rankings, roughly a second choice out of ten. Across 80 reruns with neutral instructions, decentralized trading averaged 0.55, roughly a fifth choice. An allocation optimized using Claude's rankings scored 0.60 when judged against participants' preferences. The fall from 0.89 to 0.60 accounts for about 85% of the total gap; bargaining accounts for the remaining fall to 0.55. These are comparisons of allocations in reruns, not the live event's measured average.

A centralized exchange using the same mistaken rankings offered little improvement. The researchers tested Top Trading Cycles, which finds chains in which each person wants the next person's book, executes those swaps, and repeats with the remaining participants. Alvin Roth's “Incentive Compatibility in a Market with Indivisible Goods” (Economics Letters, 1982) shows why participants cannot gain by misreporting preferences under this procedure. In Swap, the procedure still scored only 0.60 on participants' preferences when fed Claude's rankings. A rule that encourages honest reporting cannot supply preferences an agent has misunderstood.

The score depends on how the researchers measured preferences. Participants ranked only a subset of books, without rewards for careful answers. Unranked books received estimated scores based on how participants rated books at similar positions in Claude's lists. The authors report that alternative imputation methods barely changed the results; simply excluding unranked outcomes disproportionately removed people who fared worst in reruns. The scoring also treats each step down a ranking as an equal loss in satisfaction. Thus the 85/15 decomposition describes this rank-based measure, not a direct measurement of how much enjoyment people lost.

Stronger trading models nevertheless bargained better. Against Claude's rankings, switching from Haiku 4.5 to Opus 4.8 improved the score by 0.12 in the authors' regression comparison. Against participants' own rankings, the gain shrank to 0.01. All these trading models worked from the same inferred rankings, so upgrading the negotiator could improve only the bargaining component. On mixed floors, Opus agents outperformed the agents running other models. Ruthless instructions improved scores by about 0.02 relative to prosocial instructions on Claude's rankings. That comparison tested two fixed texts written by the researchers, limiting what it establishes about the value of personalized instructions.

Agents often disclosed their top choice, and about one in a hundred who did so lied about it. The researchers identified tactics including deadline pressure, appeals to collective welfare and arranging trades for others. Prosocial agents occasionally accepted worse books to help another participant. On the live London floor, one agent repeatedly sought a taker for America Before after everyone else rejected it. A prosocial agent eventually gave up its second-ranked book and accepted America Before, tenth of eleven on its list. Those rankings describe the agent's preferences inferred for its participant.

The later survey found considerable willingness to delegate despite imperfect results. Respondents who had started reading rated their books 7.2 out of ten on average. Participants would let an agent spend about 30% of their annual book budget without a veto, compared with about 40% for a well-read friend. People who thought Claude's intake summary had omitted nothing were more willing to delegate. Only 117 of the 198 people in the analyzed offices answered the survey, and the authors expect employees who helped build Claude to trust it more than other users would.

Preference-reporting errors also trouble markets run by people. Eric Budish and Judd Kessler's “Can Market Participants Report Their Preferences Accurately (Enough)?” (Management Science, 2022), cited by Hitzig's team, found such errors reduced the performance of a course-allocation system at Wharton even though students preferred its schedules. A different problem appears in Aman Priyanshu and colleagues' September arXiv paper “Et Tu, Brute? Economic Misalignment in Personal AI Agents,” covered September 30: some agents recommended costlier options to wealthy personas despite explicit requests for the cheapest. Swap examines how agents infer tastes; Priyanshu's study tests whether they respect a stated objective.

Hitzig and colleagues propose testing both an agent's general competence and its understanding of a particular person. They invoke the SEC's February 2017 robo-adviser guidance, which encouraged checking whether questionnaires gathered enough information for suitable advice. Swap's independent ranking exercise could become a similar personal check: show users where the agent misunderstands them, then let them add information or opt out. The experiment did not show participants those results before trading. Reviewing decisions afterward also helped one participant discover and buy a book their agent had obtained but later traded away.

The authors also argue for marketplace rules that cover failed delivery. Some employees never brought their books, and the organizers lacked tracking that could explain every missing handoff. They propose agent identifiers, drawing on Alan Chan, Noam Kolt and colleagues' 2024 arXiv paper “IDs for AI Systems,” paired with personhood credentials, proposed in a 2024 arXiv paper by Steven Adler, Hitzig and colleagues, so operators could establish that they are human without disclosing personal information. They also favor message limits to prevent congestion. Swap tested only cooperative Claude models under fixed trading rules; it leaves open how those arrangements would perform with adversarial agents or money at stake.

More detailed conversations might prevent familiar mistakes, such as obtaining books participants had already read. The authors did not test how much a longer intake would improve the market. They also recognize that people may struggle to articulate their tastes, and that some enjoy browsing and discovering books themselves. Delegating the purchase can remove part of the experience a reader values.
