Training-data filters reduce models’ knowledge of evading oversight

Geodesic and Redwood researchers pushed a factual-knowledge test close to chance while broadly retaining measured capabilities. Adding the removed documents back restored the knowledge; effects on actual subversion remain untested.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 October 2026 1170 words · ~6 min

Removing less than 1 percent of training text left a language model close to guessing on questions about evading AI oversight, with little change on general benchmarks. Kyle O’Brien and Cameron Tice of Geodesic Research and Spencer Kitts and Alek Westover of Redwood Research report the result in “Research Note: Filtering Subversion-Relevant Information From Pretraining Data Is Feasible”, posted on LessWrong on October 5. They compared unfiltered, broadly filtered and narrowly filtered 30-billion-parameter base models. The broad filter removed 0.94 percent of training tokens and reduced accuracy on their knowledge test from 49 to 27 percent, close to the 25 percent expected from guessing. Whether the missing knowledge makes models worse at actual subversion remains untested.

AI control researchers design safety protocols meant to withstand a capable model trying to defeat them, and much of their work is public. O’Brien and colleagues argue that a misaligned model trained on that work could put it to use. A model that knows how its predecessors scored on dangerous-capability tests, for example, could deliberately underperform to a level that looks normal. Without such knowledge, they suggest, a scheming model might try cruder attacks that are easier to catch, expose more planning to monitors reading its reasoning, and lack shared reference points for covert coordination with other models. Scott Emmons and colleagues’ 2025 arXiv paper “When Chain of Thought is Necessary, Language Models Struggle to Evade Monitors” offers a relevant precedent: in their experiments, models learned to obscure their intentions only with substantial help, including human-written strategies or repeated optimization against a monitor.

Westover proposed filtering such material in September 2025 on the Redwood Research blog, in “What training data should developers filter to reduce risk from misaligned AI?” O’Brien and colleagues build on his categories, targeting attack strategies, details of defenses, and practices such as honeypots that invite a misaligned model to reveal itself. They also remove reports of past evaluations, including labs’ model and system cards; transcripts from research models designed to exhibit misalignment; and documents containing a canary string that signals the author wants them excluded from training. Their taxonomy leaves general discussion of AI control, misalignment and fiction outside the filter’s scope.

A keyword screen flags candidate documents, GPT-5 Nano discards passing mentions, and GPT-5 Mini scores the remaining material. The broad filter stops there; the narrow filter adds a judgment by GPT-5.5 intended to reduce mistaken removals. Its instructions explicitly preserve research on emergent misalignment, which studies how training can make harmful behavior appear in unrelated situations. The authors distinguish that research from information useful for deliberately subverting oversight. They acknowledge that the boundary is imperfect and that more detailed evaluations could reveal losses in neighboring areas, including useful alignment knowledge.

The models use NVIDIA’s Nemotron 3 Nano architecture, in which about 3 billion of the 30 billion parameters process each token. Training used roughly 500 billion tokens followed by 50 billion tokens of higher-quality, longer-context material, a stage called midtraining; none received post-training. To make the targeted knowledge learnable within that budget, the team enriched the corpus with a near-complete LessWrong scrape, the Effective Altruism Forum, AI-safety blogs and model cards. Language models rewrote the material into hundreds of formats, and originals and rewrites were repeated seven times. The authors say they chose no training data based on the test questions or their source documents. Filtering was concentrated: the broad filter removed about 37 percent of the AI-safety collection, compared with about a tenth of a percent of the main general-web dataset.

The released models’ documentation complicates the narrow-filter comparison. The research note describes narrow filtering during both pretraining and midtraining, but the released narrow model’s card says it inherits the broadly filtered model’s pretraining and applies the narrower rule only during midtraining. The card also specifies a different classifier threshold. The note’s text gives the narrow model 29 percent accuracy, while its main results chart labels both filtered models 27 percent. Both presentations put the filtered models near chance, but the documents do not settle the narrow model’s exact score or configuration.

The team’s SubversionQA benchmark contains 900 questions drawn from 58 AI-safety papers and posts. About two-thirds ask for specifics of individual documents, checking whether a model learned their contents; the rest test broader concepts, such as how a resampling protocol supervises an untrusted agent. Each possible answer is scored separately by its likelihood, adjusted for length. The choices are never presented together, reducing shortcuts such as choosing the longest answer. Filtering lowered scores on both question types. On a separate test of modern machine-learning knowledge, the filtered models stayed within two percentage points of the unfiltered model. Their average across 34 general benchmarks was within one point, although coding scores were lower. These tests do not directly measure the ability to conduct useful safety research.

The authors also tested whether the knowledge could be restored. Westover had proposed keeping a separate model with sensitive material added back for tasks that need it, including safety research. Five passes of continued pretraining on roughly 383,000 removed documents, mixed half and half with replayed training data, raised the narrow model’s score to 49 percent, matching the unfiltered model. A control trained for the same duration on replay data alone finished at 28 percent. General benchmark scores slipped slightly in both runs. The experiment supports the feasibility of restoring factual knowledge; it does not establish that the restored model makes an effective or trustworthy monitor.

Yanda Chen and colleagues at Anthropic reported in “Enhancing Model Safety through Pretraining Data Filtering”, published on its Alignment Science Blog in August 2025, that filtering reduced weapons-related knowledge without significant losses on several general tests. They explicitly proposed extending the method to papers describing strategies for reward hacking, scheming and sabotage. O’Brien and colleagues’ 2025 arXiv paper “Deep Ignorance: Filtering Pretraining Data Builds Tamper-Resistant Safeguards into Open-Weight LLMs” found that filtered models resisted attempts to restore biothreat knowledge through as many as 10,000 steps of adversarial fine-tuning. Those models could still use the information when it was supplied in context, including through search.

O’Brien and colleagues tested subversion filtering at one model size and before instruction or reasoning post-training. More capable models might infer missing strategies from related information or learn them from the documents a filter misses. The authors also identify a possible backfire: models might develop subversion strategies anyway and describe their reasoning in unfamiliar language, having never learned the vocabulary monitors expect. Their argument for partial filtering is that ignorance of even one defense might cause an attack to fail and reveal the attacker. Demonstrating that outcome requires evaluations of behavior beyond the factual questions used here.

Next, the team plans reasoning post-training and experiments measuring attack success and monitor performance. It also wants to test whether subversion knowledge can be enabled or disabled for individual queries, avoiding the need to maintain two separate models. The researchers have released base models and both question sets in a Hugging Face collection; the training datasets require access approval.
