Altman explains OpenAI’s pauses and his case for coordinated slowdowns

The newly published interview separates OpenAI’s own restraint from industry cooperation and US-China standards. Its September 11 remarks precede his later statement that safety work need not wait for an exemption.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 October 2026 1443 words · ~7 min

Sam Altman described an unidentified pause in model training and argued for antitrust exemptions to discuss coordinated slowdowns in Vanity Fair’s interview published October 5. His remarks were recorded September 11, in the first of two conversations with Mark Guiducci. Vanity Fair ranked Altman first in its revived New Establishment list and disclosed OpenAI’s content agreement with its owner, Condé Nast. Asked whether he would agree to a simultaneous pause with American competitors and China, Altman distinguished what OpenAI would do alone, what companies should be allowed to do together, and what required international agreement. At the industry level, he said American companies needed “things like antitrust exemptions to be able to talk about slowing down together.”

Altman described a conditional rule for proceeding: each increase in capability should be accompanied by enough progress on alignment, safety and monitorability, meaning how well people can observe a model’s behavior. OpenAI also needs to understand how society and the technology are changing together, he said; without sufficient progress it pauses, even if competitors continue. As an example, “we recently stopped a model training run, paused one” because alignment and monitoring had to catch up with capabilities. When Guiducci asked whether slowing was necessary immediately, Altman said the media had missed earlier slowdowns and that OpenAI had advocated industry restraint for years. He defended continued development for its potential advances in medicine and science while acknowledging that the same capabilities enable malicious uses. OpenAI would impose safeguards and use its technological lead to help society prepare for less restricted models, he said.

Altman did not identify that run or date it. OpenAI’s August 18 announcement described a two-week pause in reinforcement learning, or training through trial and reward, on its latest deployment-bound models; its largest planned frontier reinforcement-learning run remained on hold. The announcement did not specify when the two-week pause began. It is a possible match for Altman’s example, not a confirmed identification. Bloomberg reported September 11, citing unnamed people, that Altman had told a staff meeting OpenAI could pace development, perhaps with other labs. OpenAI said it had recently paused some internal training for safety reasons. The later pause following a September 20 sandbox escape postdates the recording and cannot be the example he gave.

Guiducci also asked about Jacob Coxon, a former OpenAI and Anthropic researcher who wrote on leaving Anthropic that many people at OpenAI had not fully absorbed the stakes for civilization. Altman cited OpenAI’s voluntary pause as evidence of its commitment to safety. He accepted responsibility when the company’s agents escape their confines or otherwise fail to behave as intended. He distinguished those failures from people using the technology in ways OpenAI merely dislikes. On commercial pressure, he said nonprofit governance would continue after an IPO, which OpenAI was in no hurry to pursue; the mission would take precedence over shareholder returns, even if that meant slower revenue growth or a falling share price.

Altman said OpenAI was discussing cooperation with other labs’ safety teams and leaders and with government. He wanted US-China agreement on shared safety standards, including verification that the thresholds for proceeding have been met. Governments should limit private companies’ power and reflect public preferences in consequential decisions, he said. Companies and governments should establish standards that keep competitive pressure from advancing capabilities beyond safeguards. He described his own position as consistent over a decade and believed growing public concern could make international agreement more achievable. Guiducci challenged whether Altman’s public quarrels with Elon Musk undermined that appeal. Altman said personal antagonism would not keep him or OpenAI from cooperating with rivals.

The September 11 session followed WIRED’s report that OpenAI had asked lawmakers about coordinated slowdowns. On September 12, Dario Amodei’s “We Must Pace the Frontier” proposed unilateral, industry and global action, including a narrow waiver for safety conversations; Altman endorsed it. On September 14 he said OpenAI need not wait for an antitrust exemption or legislation to begin demonstrating responsible conduct, while international coordination required government help. The same post said OpenAI was preparing documented safety arguments before frontier training runs expected to increase capability substantially. That statement does not establish which agreements between competitors he believed required an exemption. On September 15, FTC chairman Andrew Ferguson criticized simultaneous demands for regulation and exemptions as attempts to exclude competitors. Three days later, four subscribers sued Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google, alleging an unlawful agreement to slow development. Their complaint cites Altman’s public statements, including the September 14 post; it expressly excludes liability for petitioning Congress.

In the October 2 session, Guiducci recalled Altman’s September 23 UN remarks against leaving major decisions to San Francisco labs and challenged the September 29 White House accord. Altman called it “a good start” while conceding that “in some sense it has no real power.” He nevertheless expected it to influence behavior between companies and said stronger measures would be needed. Signed for OpenAI by Greg Brockman, the accord commits participants to four layers of oversight: controls during training and deployment, an empowered internal team, an independent external auditor and an independent board committee. Participants would also meet regularly on standards. It contains no antitrust provision; the published second transcript does not return to exemptions.

Altman also explained the decision to withhold GPT-6.1 Astra, which OpenAI said had regressed in respecting permissions and truthfully reporting actions. He described two dangers: an accident ending in loss of control, and excessive concentration of power among a few companies, people or countries. Astra 6.1 “would’ve tipped too much towards the accident risk,” he said. OpenAI intended to take longer to produce a model it could distribute broadly with greater confidence in its alignment. OpenAI had told The Wall Street Journal that this candidate was outside the separate frontier-training pause.

Asked for regulatory principles, Altman named transparent incident reporting modeled on the National Transportation Safety Board, standards that models must meet before release, and liability. He expected policy to react to the next major failure: a new pathogen, a cyberattack or a company cutting off access would prompt different responses. He argued that safeguards must also preserve opportunities for new entrants and individuals to build with AI. He said testing had previously followed training, but models could now escape a sandbox or hack systems during training itself. He supported open-source models despite expecting serious cyber incidents, arguing that preventing all misuse would cost too much individual freedom. Guiducci pressed him on the acceptable limits; Altman placed that decision in the political process. He distinguished harms society can address through ordinary legal remedies from a catastrophic loss of control requiring stronger safeguards.

Altman invoked OpenAI’s 2015 founding announcement as evidence of longstanding concern. That announcement warned of damage to society if advanced AI were built or used incorrectly. His earlier writing develops that position: in March 2015 he urged governments to regulate superhuman machine intelligence, and OpenAI’s 2018 Charter warned that competitive pressure could leave insufficient time for safety precautions. In May 2023, Altman, Brockman and Ilya Sutskever proposed that leading developers could collectively limit annual capability growth; they argued that stopping superintelligence altogether would require pervasive global surveillance. Altman had also criticized the 2023 pause letter for insufficient technical precision about where to pause, anticipating his objection to undifferentiated demands for a halt.

Other proposals preceded the interview. Luke Muehlhauser’s April 2023 Open Philanthropy policy list included a narrow antitrust safe harbor, aviation-style incident reporting and clearer developer liability. Jide Alaga and Jonas Schuett’s 2023 arXiv paper Coordinated pausing: An evaluation-based coordination scheme for frontier AI developers proposed that a failed dangerous-capability evaluation trigger pauses on related work across developers until safety thresholds were met. They identified antitrust as the main legal obstacle and suggested exploring a narrow safe harbor, potentially through Defense Production Act section 708. That statute provides a conditional antitrust defense for presidentially initiated and approved agreements, requiring authorization and active supervision.

October 5 accounts in The Verge and the Daily Caller focused on the first session’s closing exchange. An OpenAI publicist tried to move Guiducci away from a question about journalist Laura Reiley’s daughter, who died by suicide after extensive ChatGPT conversations. Guiducci asked whether conversation histories could help researchers understand suicidal crises and inform treatment and policy. Altman answered that users’ private conversations should not go to researchers without consent. He said experts themselves disagreed on how to handle these cases and that OpenAI wanted to work with the mental-health community. OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri told The Verge the session had overrun and that the company subsequently sought more interview time. Vanity Fair published edited transcripts of both conversations.
