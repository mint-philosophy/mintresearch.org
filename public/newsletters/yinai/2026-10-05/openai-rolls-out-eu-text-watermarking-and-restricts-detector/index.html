OpenAI opens API watermarking ahead of its EU product rollout

ChatGPT and Codex will mark eligible EU text over the coming weeks. Detector access starts with approved organizations; tests show why passage length, editing and false positives limit what a flag can establish.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 October 2026 1114 words · ~6 min

OpenAI opened global API opt-in for text watermarking on October 5 and announced that eligible ChatGPT and Codex output would receive watermarks across all plans in the European Union over the coming weeks. Its post, “Our approach to EU text provenance rules”, keeps API watermarking off by default and limits the product rollout to the EU. According to the accompanying help article, API customers can enable it for an organization or individual project and choose supported models. Applications for the detector opened the same day, with access initially restricted to approved researchers and expert organizations.

Xiang Li of the University of Pennsylvania, Weijie Su of OpenAI and seven colleagues explain the design in OpenAI’s October 5 technical report, “textGrain: Entropy-Calibrated Watermarking for Language Model Text”. A model generates tokens, which can be words or word pieces, by sampling from probabilities. The watermark changes those choices using a secret key. Under the earlier Gumbel-max method, a fixed key and context determine the next token, potentially making repeated answers identical. textGrain sets a budget for the average fraction of sampling randomness removed, allowing fresh variation while embedding a detectable pattern. The average is over keyed randomness; it is not a guarantee for every individual choice, and the numerical solver must check its achieved loss.

In the report’s example, the key and context group six candidate words after “The morning was” into pairs. One adjusted distribution raises the combined probability of “cold” and “sunny” from 0.35 to 0.69, preserving their relative odds within the pair. Detection reconstructs the keyed values and tests their relationship to the observed tokens. It needs the text, key and matching configuration, without the generating model or entropy budget. The stated false-positive guarantee assumes idealized independence; a deployed key needs empirical calibration. Su described textGrain on X as a spectrum between ordinary random sampling and Gumbel-max. In a reply about constrained text, he said reproducing a Shakespeare sonnet would leave no watermark signal because there is no freedom to choose different wording.

OpenAI’s announcement reports detection of about 80% of 200-token psychology passages, roughly 150 English words, rising to about 95% at 400 tokens. Mathematics passages were harder to detect because their wording was more constrained. These evaluations targeted a 1% false-positive rate: flagging about one in every hundred unwatermarked passages under the evaluated conditions. That figure describes a test setting, not the probability that a particular flag is correct. In a separate editing evaluation of 400-token passages, substituting synonyms for a quarter of the words reduced detection from about 92% to 17%. OpenAI said in its announcement thread: “Rewriting or translating text can completely remove the watermark.”

The help article adds a test across all 24 official EU languages, using 500 synthetic English prompts translated into the other languages. At a 1% false-positive rate, reported detection ranged from 69.0% in Spanish to 42.2% in Romanian; OpenAI then strengthened the watermark for languages below 60%. Passage length is unspecified, so these percentages cannot be compared directly with the psychology results. OpenAI reports no meaningful performance change on its Astra benchmarks and attributes the differences to ordinary variation between evaluation runs. It says textGrain matched or exceeded tested alternatives, including Google DeepMind’s SynthID for text, and plans to release textGrain as open source.

OpenAI says a detected watermark can indicate that its systems generated or processed part of a passage. It does not identify a user, account, prompt or conversation, measure the human contribution, or establish ownership, responsibility or accuracy. Undetected text may still have come from an OpenAI model: it may be short, edited, translated, produced before watermarking or generated by an unsupported model. Other providers’ outputs also fall outside this detector’s remit.

Article 50(2) of the EU AI Act requires providers to make synthetic text and other generated content machine-readably marked and detectable. It requires effective, interoperable, robust and reliable solutions as far as technically feasible, taking account of content limitations, implementation costs and the state of the art. Exceptions cover standard editing assistance and systems that do not substantially alter the supplied input or its meaning. The European Commission’s summary says the rules apply from August 2, 2026, with a marking grace period until December 2026 for generative systems placed on the market before August 2.

Under the voluntary Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, which OpenAI signed, free-form text longer than 200 tokens must carry a watermark, subject to the legal scope and exceptions. The Code permits restricted text-detector access while reliability is limited, but calls for access with safeguards for verified experts with a legitimate need, including regulators, media, fact-checkers, researchers and civil society. Those restrictions are to end as more reliable detection becomes established. OpenAI cites the Code in explaining its case-by-case access policy; its help article describes current access as limited to approved research and academic organizations. Enabling API watermarking does not grant detector access.

OpenAI names John Thickstun of Cornell, Martin Vechev of ETH Zurich and researchers at the Kempelen Institute of Intelligent Technologies among its initial research partners. Anthropic’s August explainer, updated September 1, describes a broader private preview for its own detection API, including regulators, media, fact-checkers and enterprises with relevant compliance obligations. Claude’s watermark uses a version of SynthID-Text and applies globally to supported models because Anthropic says it lacks a durable way to restrict it by region.

OpenAI had considered watermarking years earlier. Scott Aaronson wrote on August 22, 2026 that he devised a scheme at OpenAI in 2022, Hendrik Kirchner implemented it, and leadership declined deployment over fears customers would leave. Deepa Seetharaman and Matt Barnum’s August 2024 Wall Street Journal report said the tool had been ready for about a year; nearly 30% of surveyed users said they would use ChatGPT less if it watermarked while a rival did not. OpenAI’s August 4, 2024 update acknowledged evasion through translation or rewording, possible stigma for non-native English speakers using AI as a writing aid, and the many false positives even a low error rate would produce at scale.

John Gruber of Daring Fireball welcomed the EU-only product scope and API option but questioned whether the technology would work in everyday use, calling the effort “somewhat of a sham to claim compliance”. Max Spero, chief executive of Pangram Labs, which sells a classifier-based AI-text detector, called the 1% target “surprisingly high for what some would assume to be irrefutable proof of AI.” Replying to him, John Rood illustrated the arithmetic: if only 1% of screened passages carried watermarks, a detector catching all of them while falsely flagging 1% of the rest would produce nearly equal numbers of true and false flags.
