Anthropic anticipates an AI research acceleration threshold and considers human approval

The company expects future models may soon meet its acceleration threshold and is considering additional approval rules for research decisions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 November 2026 638 words · ~3 min

Anthropic says future models may imminently meet its threshold for substantially faster AI development in its November 2 amended IPO filing. The assessment rests on internal estimates and expectations about prospective systems; the company has designated no current model above the threshold, continues to assess the data, and promises further reporting. The amendment formalizes a forecast in the continuing debate over human control of AI-assisted research.

Anthropic's October 26 prospectus already warned that research acceleration might outrun safety work and that delayed measurements could reveal acceleration only after it happened. The amendment adds that such delayed recognition has happened before. That admission concerns recognizing acceleration generally; the company separately says it has made no current-model designation under the policy threshold. The filing does not identify the future model or give a crossing date.

Anthropic defines the threshold in its Responsible Scaling Policy, version 3.4. Anthropic must observe or expect aggregate capabilities to progress at least twice as fast as two baselines without substantial AI assistance: the expected pace and the fastest sustained historical pace. The historical comparison spans at least three model generations. Research or engineering automation must plausibly account for much of the doubling, with further acceleration likely. In practical terms, two baseline years of progress fit into one. The policy illustrates this with a hypothetical ninefold annual gain in effective computing scale becoming an 81-fold gain: compounding twice within the same year. Doubling researchers' productivity alone does not establish that acceleration.

In its July revision, Anthropic explains why it added the historical comparison. A company could estimate that AI greatly improved development relative to a hypothetical world without AI while actual progress remained constant or slowed. Other constraints could keep offsetting automation. The threshold is intended to identify a self-reinforcing acceleration. Its alternative test is whether models could fully replace the company's entire workforce of research scientists and research engineers at costs within a factor of five.

Anthropic is also considering policy changes requiring human review and approval of certain research decisions. It says it does not expect to safely let Claude independently design and build successive frontier generations without meaningful human supervision or understanding, even if that becomes technically possible. The amendment describes prospective requirements, with their scope still unspecified.

Anthropic's earlier public measurements of research automation help explain how much work models already perform. In August, Claude led 26% of measured AI research and development, completing most of a task from a high-level instruction while a person supervised; no measured category was fully autonomous. Researchers constructed the index from staff work records, used Claude to classify how automated each task was, and weighted categories by estimated staff time. Because the basket of tasks is fixed, increasing automation of those tasks does not by itself capture new work humans undertake. The index measures who performs research work; the policy threshold concerns how quickly the resulting model capabilities improve.

Anthropic's same report describes existing oversight on its most-used internal agent platform. Automated monitors examine actions before execution and review records afterwards; selected flags reach humans. The proposed policy would add requirements for human approval of particular decisions. The filing does not specify which decisions those would be, or how the requirements would change existing supervision.

Andrew Curran predicts early 2027 for fully autonomous successor-building, interpreting improvements in model creativity as the missing capability. He points back to Jack Clark's October 12 forecast, which moved the likely year when full recursive self-improvement becomes possible from 2028 to 2027. Clark cited advances in mathematical problem-solving and experimental research judgment. Curran's date and interpretation belong to that discussion; Anthropic's amended filing makes the narrower company assessment about future models and its defined acceleration threshold. The forecasts concern technical possibility, while the proposed approval rules concern how Anthropic would permit that capability to be used.
