Anthropic’s quarterly growth slows as computing costs squeeze margins

The amended prospectus adds third-quarter results and explains how short-term computing purchases and donation charges affected profitability.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 November 2026 653 words · ~3 min

Beatrice Nolan reports in Fortune that Anthropic's quarterly growth slowed as computing costs squeezed profitability. Following the earlier financial disclosures, the company's November 2 amended prospectus adds results for the quarter ended September 30: revenue of nearly $17 billion and an operating loss of $567 million. The original public filing had supplied financial results through June.

Anthropic's quarterly tables distinguish several comparisons. Revenue rose from $11.6 billion in the second quarter, an increase of about 47%; the preceding quarter's increase had been about 144%. Against the same quarter a year earlier, sales rose more than twelvefold from $1.4 billion. These figures measure revenue earned over three months. Anthropic's separately defined annual revenue run rate extrapolates recent usage and subscriptions; it is neither quarterly revenue nor a forecast of the next year's sales.

The company's operating reconciliation shows why different profit measures give different answers. Excluding $1.07 billion in stock-based compensation and $1.96 billion in non-cash charitable charges produces $2.46 billion in adjusted operating income. That exceeded the second quarter's $2.24 billion, although the adjusted margin fell from 19% to 15%. CoinDesk's account also distinguishes the $23.3 billion net loss from the operating result: $21.9 billion arose from revaluing convertible notes and related instruments as Anthropic's valuation increased. The net loss consequently cannot be read as the quarter's cash consumption.

Anthropic had already explained the charitable program in its October 26 prospectus. Employees can pledge equity or equivalent proceeds to nonprofits, and the company matches donations with non-voting shares; the plan also permits matching in cash. The accounting liability reflects the estimated value of those obligations, including the company's share price. In the first quarter, Anthropic settled an earlier obligation by issuing 30.6 million shares. The third-quarter charge therefore concerns a real matching commitment, even though recording the expense did not itself require a corresponding cash payment. Excluding it helps compare operations while setting aside a cost borne by shareholders.

Anthropic identifies two pressures on the underlying business in its quarterly discussion. Computing and infrastructure expenses reached $9.9 billion, including $5 billion of capacity bought through short-term commitments at higher prices than comparable multiyear supply. The $5 billion measures the cost of that capacity; the filing does not quantify the premium over comparable long-term supply. Separately, model price reductions and contractual discounts offset approximately $600 million of revenue gains from higher usage. Customers can consequently consume more while the revenue earned from each unit falls.

The company's proposed cost relief depends on Anthropic Direct, a strategy described in the original prospectus. Anthropic leases chips directly and hosts them in dedicated facilities operated on its behalf. Separating commitments for buildings from commitments for the accelerators inside them lets the company reserve the slower-to-build facilities while delaying larger hardware decisions until demand is clearer. Anthropic expects this capacity to replace costly short-term purchases as it becomes operational. That is a forecast about future supply and utilization; the prospectus's argument depends on bringing the facilities into service and using them productively.

The amendment quantifies the scale: $895.4 billion in purchase and lease commitments entered into as of and after September 30, with $69.8 billion due in the twelve months following September 30. Substantially all are for compute and infrastructure; about half is expected before the end of 2030, and nearly all the remainder between 2031 and 2045. Reported liquidity of $146.6 billion includes $14.8 billion available under a credit facility. Both the payment schedule and that distinction between cash and borrowing capacity matter when assessing the company's ability to fund expansion.

In Reuters' reporting, Utkarsh Shetti and Arasu Kannagi Basil highlight another dependency: some infrastructure partners need outside financing or credit support to deliver contracted capacity. Loss of that support could delay delivery or raise prices. Matt Kennedy of Renaissance Capital told Reuters he expected pressure on growth and margins to affect the eventual IPO valuation, without characterizing the results as alarming.
