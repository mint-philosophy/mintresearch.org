GPT-6 Astra edits Qwen's weights to repair repetitive reasoning

MIT researchers used a general-purpose model to inspect and alter neural networks. Their largest mathematics gain came from fixing a failure that prevented Qwen from finishing answers; a model without that failure did not improve.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 November 2026 862 words · ~4 min

Editing a small language model's weights helped it stop repeating itself and finish mathematics problems it could already solve. Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab of MIT CSAIL report the result in their November 2 research blog “Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights.” They gave GPT-6 Astra access to neural networks' numerical parameters and asked it to interpret or change their behavior without conventional training. Eight experiments test different combinations of reading weights, writing code and evaluating the resulting network.

The main experiment used Qwen3.5-0.8B, whose model card warns that its written reasoning can enter loops and fail to terminate. Astra received a CPU-only Python environment, practice mathematics problems and a limited budget for running the model. It traced the repetition to a direction in the model's internal representations reinforced by two layers, then edited their weights to suppress that direction. Mathematics accuracy rose from 7.9% to 48.1% on MATH-500. The evaluated problems were withheld from Astra.

The repair changed only a tiny fraction of Qwen's weights. Answers that previously repeated until the output limit were much more likely to finish, and instruction following also improved. In Astra's own check on held-out practice problems, almost every newly correct answer had previously been cut off; accuracy on answers that originally finished was unchanged. Ahsen Khaliq of Hugging Face rebuilt the checkpoint from the published changes, verified that it matched the authors' version and reran MATH-500, obtaining a similar improvement.

This required sustained tool use. The released logs show that the successful run took about twenty hours, including an outage, and that smaller evaluation budgets produced different, much weaker edits. Codex's built-in tools were disabled, but Astra could write Python that ran in the researchers' sandbox and inspect the results. The result combined weight editing with repeated behavioral tests.

The authors tested whether the procedure could improve a model whose mistakes came from something other than repetition. Gemma 3 270M rarely hit its output limit; Astra found ordinary arithmetic and algebra errors without a single component it could remove to fix them. Its edits produced no established mathematics improvement. The authors conclude that weight editing removed an obstacle hiding Qwen's ability, while the Gemma experiment did not show that it could supply a missing ability.

Other experiments used much smaller networks and sometimes required no tools. Given weights from four identically shaped game-playing networks, Astra identified every game. The input channels were described, though the game names were withheld. It also identified shapes generated by small diffusion models, but drew outlines where the models generated filled shapes. In a physics simulation, a single edit to a stuck car's policy changed repeated failure into success across all tested episodes. These tasks show useful interpretation and intervention on compact networks, under more constrained conditions than the Qwen experiment.

With Python, Astra identified the training languages and copying mechanisms of small character-level transformers without running them. It also wrote networks encoding game-playing programs from scratch. Their performance exceeded some reinforcement-learning baselines, but stronger baselines won in two of the three arcade games. Another experiment exposed a limit to diagnosis from weights alone: Astra could not distinguish an honest Gemma model from a sycophantic copy until it was allowed to inspect where the models directed attention while processing sample texts.

These edits have precedents. Andy Arditi and colleagues' 2024 refusal study, which the blog cites, showed that subtracting a single direction from model weights could disable refusals. Aristotelis Lazaridis and colleagues had repaired repetition loops in Gemma models in June, in one case by reversing a single neuron's sign; the MIT post does not cite that work. Weight Oracles trained language models specifically to interpret other networks' weights. The MIT team's contribution is having a general-purpose frontier model perform such analysis and editing without specialized training for the task.

Sean Goedecke's response argues that familiar techniques explain the results. For the game networks, he shows that a short Python calculation produces heatmaps revealing features such as Breakout's paddle row. For Qwen, he describes contrasting repetitive and productive text, locating the weights contributing to the difference and trimming them, an approach he compares with the Heretic model-editing tool. His objection is to interpreting the experiments as superhuman research, not to the practical achievement.

Matthew Honnibal likewise objects to combining raw-weight, no-tool demonstrations with experiments that use Python and run the target model. The distinction affects what capability the reader should infer. In a November 3 follow-up, Simon Willison reproduced three of the four game identifications using Qwen 3.8 27B on his laptop, showing that this part of the result is not unique to Astra.

Lucas Beyer also pointed out that the Qwen prompt mentioned repetitive reasoning. Teoh explained that Claude Code expanded the team's one-line task into that prompt without human feedback; Zhang acknowledged the hints and said the team initially mistook the result for a broader capability gain. The authors have released the logs and edited model and are developing a benchmark. Their proposed uses include detecting hidden behaviors and changing unwanted ones, but the experiments currently establish a collection of specific successes and failures.
