Lambert sees Meta regaining ground in open models

His Interconnects essay connects Muse Spark Open’s prospects to adoption, licensing and Meta’s research organization, then asks whether sustained competition will undermine frontier-lab valuations.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 17 October 2026 1079 words · ~5 min

Nathan Lambert argues in “Meta shakes up the frontiers, open and closed”, published by Interconnects on October 17, that Muse Spark Open has made American open-weight models relevant again to users seeking advanced capabilities. Meta’s release and Reflection’s Beam still trail leading Chinese models, but he expects their origin, availability and adequate performance to attract users. His concern is that Meta’s licence could obstruct that adoption. The essay develops the argument for American open-model investment he made in the congressional briefing we covered in September, then examines Meta’s position among the companies developing the strongest closed models.

Lambert’s October 16 snapshot of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index puts Muse Spark Open tenth, at 39, behind nine Chinese releases; Xiaomi’s leading model scores 46. Reflection improves the American showing too, but remains below Meta. Lambert estimates that the gap has shrunk roughly by half and says that without those two models, more than 20 Chinese releases would outperform the strongest Western alternatives. His comparison counts individual releases, including superseded versions. He also distinguishes announced performance from downloadable weights: Mistral Large 4 looks competitive in the comparison, but its weights were not available when he wrote.

Lambert expects adoption to depend on more than ranking first. Interconnects’ analyses, he writes, repeatedly find that Western open models gain more adoption than Chinese models with comparable performance and size. He places Muse Spark Open above GLM-5.2, around DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and below GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3, sufficient for the growing market in coding agents and other systems that perform multistep tasks. Inference providers are expanding, developers are building businesses around further training open models, and enterprises want control over their own deployments. These are reasons for his adoption forecast, distinct from a claim that American models already lead aggregate usage.

Lambert also disputes the suggestion that Meta damaged the release through safety training. Commenters on the Hacker News launch thread compared it unfavorably with Spark 1.2 and 1.3; halJordan called it a “bait and switch” on the promised release. Lambert thinks Spark Open more likely received additional safety training from Spark 1.2, leaving a one-point difference on the index, than from the substantially stronger 1.3. He connects this interpretation to Alexandr Wang’s earlier promise of open Spark 1.2 weights. Meta’s model card confirms extra safety training but does not identify the numbered starting checkpoint. The lineage and the amount of performance lost to that training remain Lambert’s inference.

Lambert’s licence criticism concerns the combined effect of branding, commercial permission requirements and restrictions on use and derivatives. He expects many developers to ignore complex terms and Meta to pursue egregious violations, but considers relying on such tolerance an unnecessary risk for a company trying to regain adoption. In his account, licences have not yet determined which models succeed; their complexity could become consequential as more businesses build on open weights. He also worries that obligations involving models trained on outputs could make synthetic-data projects harder to evaluate legally.

The licence text, discussed in our October 15 release report, is narrower than parts of Lambert’s summary. Its $20 million threshold uses aggregate gross revenue of the licensee and affiliates over any consecutive twelve months, not his stated monthly period. It requires a separate agreement for businesses providing model access as a service; internal use, research and qualifying products that do not provide such a service are exempt from that requirement. The branding text is “Built with Muse Spark Open.” Meta disclaims ownership of outputs, while defining derivatives to include models trained or improved using outputs and applying its usage policy to those derivatives. That distinction does not support treating every output as if it were licensed model software.

Lambert links the commercial restriction to Kevin Xu and Graham Webster’s July analysis of Kimi K3. They described an annual $20 million revenue threshold for cloud resellers and expected agreements with inference providers to produce revenue sharing. They also argued that selling through those agreements could expose Moonshot to restrictions on transactions with Chinese companies. Meta’s earlier Llama 3.1 licence likewise required branding, but used a 700-million-monthly-user threshold for separate permission. The Open Source Initiative’s definition requires freedom to use an AI system for any purpose without asking permission, explaining the distinction between downloadable weights and unrestricted open source. On Hacker News, popinman322 worried that separate provider agreements reduce price competition; reilly3000 defended the terms as fair compensation for training investment. Those are competing expectations about the restrictions, not measured effects of this release.

Lambert uses Meta’s closed Muse Wonder models, covered in our October 12 issue, to assess its broader strategy. He places their traditional benchmark performance near OpenAI and Google’s strongest offerings, behind Anthropic’s Opus 5.5, while distinguishing those results from refinement in everyday use. He sees Meta as the only American company competing near the frontier with both open and closed models, a combination he compares with Alibaba’s more open-weight-oriented Qwen strategy. For him, OpenAI and Anthropic’s advantage has been consistently maintaining leading performance while other labs have reached it intermittently.

Lambert credits Meta’s improvement partly to the organization Zuckerberg and Wang described on The Cheeky Pint: concentrated compute, strong researchers and fewer management layers. His claim that Wang has over 200 direct reports compresses a more qualified exchange. Zuckerberg asks whether the number is almost 200, then says the arrangement is less extreme in practice and includes team leads. He emphasizes avoiding nontechnical managers between researchers and decisions. Lambert contrasts that approach with accounts of bureaucracy at Gemini and the former Llama organization, and with turnover at xAI. These organizational explanations are his assessment of why resources sometimes translate into consistent model improvements.

Lambert concludes that American frontier competition is narrowing to Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta, Google and xAI, with Microsoft a possible sixth. He thinks smaller developers can still build substantial businesses while struggling to finance models that change the leading performance level. In a reply to reader Taylor Ortiz, he describes newer labs as overvalued long shots and suggests acquisitions may offer an exit. Yet he also stresses how poorly the industry predicts whether an expensive model will succeed in actual use. His valuation argument follows from that uncertainty: if progress takes time and current leaders lack an enduring technical advantage, profitable incumbents can sustain competition that puts pressure on AI-company IPO valuations. His comparison of Meta and Anthropic therefore depends on assumptions about future competition and business durability, beyond their proximity on benchmarks.
