Oppenheim questions whether an Astra-assisted theory recovers Einstein’s gravity

Jonathan Oppenheim accepts a classical calculation on light cones and disputes two larger claims about gravity and the microscopic states of black holes.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 November 2026 625 words · ~3 min

In a November 26 critique in Post Quantum, Jonathan Oppenheim finds a calculation he trusts in Stanford physicist Venkatesa Chandrasekaran’s proposed quantum theory of gravity: it describes which shapes a pair of intersecting surfaces traced out by light can take under Einstein’s equations. The disagreement begins when Chandrasekaran uses those shapes to build a quantum theory, where Oppenheim argues that some of the properties Chandrasekaran hopes to explain have already entered the calculation as assumptions.

Chandrasekaran’s arXiv preprint, “An algebraic quantization of gravity from the null cone,” concerns gravity in empty space with three spatial dimensions and one dimension of time. The manuscript is dated November 23 and was submitted November 25. Chandrasekaran, its sole author, says he developed several calculations and proofs in conversation with GPT-6 Astra over many months. OpenAI provided the computing resources, and he puts the total at about 400 million tokens.

Picture a flash of light spreading out from a point. At each instant the light traces a surface, and the whole history of that surface is a light cone. On intersecting cones, Chandrasekaran can solve exactly the conditions that Einstein’s theory places on the geometry before applying quantum rules to the remaining variables. For a pair of gravitons, the quantum excitations of gravity, he obtains a finite amplitude for their creation and annihilation, whereas expanding the calculation in powers of the gravitational coupling at the outset gives a divergent result.

Oppenheim’s objection concerns the choice of quantum rules. Even two quantities whose product can be written in either order in classical physics can give different answers when multiplied in quantum mechanics. Solving the classical constraints first leaves choices that can change the quantum predictions. In general relativity, two descriptions of the same geometry must agree on what can be observed however they label the points of spacetime. Oppenheim argues that the paper requires this agreement in its axioms and still owes an example of a consistent quantum theory that satisfies them. Oppenheim also asks whether imposing the constraints directly on quantum states would give the same result. He finds a separate gap in the argument for recovering Einstein’s equations at low energies. It requires a quantum operator that specifies how a state changes in time, and in his reading the paper never constructs it.

Black holes offer a particularly concrete test. Their entropy is proportional to the area of the horizon. A microscopic explanation would say which distinct quantum states look like the same black hole from outside and derive how many there are. Chandrasekaran reports recovering the area formula and a correction for large black holes. Oppenheim observes that the calculation extends an expression mathematically into a different domain. He argues that the proof of a finite number of independent states already assumes a related sum over states is finite. Recovering the familiar formula leaves the physical states themselves unidentified.

In a Bluesky discussion, physicist Sean Carroll singled out the prospect of finitely many independent states in a bounded region. He, Ning Bao and Ashmeet Singh had argued for that possibility in 2017 on general grounds. Carroll also raised a difficulty with extending the paper beyond empty space. Matter fields can affect gravity’s behavior at very short distances, precisely where a quantum description is needed.

Oppenheim used Claude agents to compile obstacles from earlier work and assess the preprint against them. Of the 21 obstacles they listed, the agents judged that Chandrasekaran had solved one with a known technique. Oppenheim says he did not verify every judgment they returned. His proposal for evaluating future papers asks physicists working on each approach to publish the problems it must solve. For black holes, one of his proposed tests is to identify the microscopic states and count them independently.
