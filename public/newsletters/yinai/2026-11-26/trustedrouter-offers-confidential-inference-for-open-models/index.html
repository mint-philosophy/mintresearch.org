TrustedRouter lets companies choose who can read their AI requests

Joseph Perla says 52% of his customers use confidential models. When customers send a question to OpenAI or Anthropic through his service, those providers can read it.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 November 2026 576 words · ~3 min

TrustedRouter founder Joseph Perla told Bloomberg’s Davey Alba that by mid-November, 52% of his company’s customers were using models that could process their questions without letting either TrustedRouter’s staff or the company hosting the model read them. Two of the most popular choices, he said, came from DeepSeek and Moonshot. Both labs release model weights, the files another company needs to run a model on its own machines. A customer can ask a DeepSeek model to analyze a document without sending a copy to DeepSeek.

Encryption protects a document on its journey across the network, but at the destination the model needs it in readable form. On a confidential route, the software decrypts the document inside a protected part of the machine, where the hardware isolates it from the host’s administrators. TrustedRouter describes how its gateway checks one provider, Chutes. Before sending the request, it verifies evidence from the processors about the software they are running and checks that the encryption key belongs to that particular instance. If a check fails, the gateway can try another verified instance.

Apple described a similar principle in its 2024 design for Private Cloud Compute. An iPhone sending work to the cloud can check which software a server is running before entrusting it with the user’s data. TrustedRouter has to establish that protection across companies. A customer can use the same gateway to reach a host running open weights or a lab selling access to its proprietary model. The gateway can protect the text from its own operators even on a route where the next company can read it.

Perla gave Alba two examples of that limit. When TrustedRouter forwards a question directly to Anthropic for Claude or to OpenAI for GPT, the receiving service gets readable text. Customers can ask the router to strip personal details first. The receiving provider can still read the rest of the question. Perla is campaigning for providers to offer confidential processing themselves.

Alba asked Amazon about Perla’s criticism of cloud providers’ retention terms. Amazon referred her to its published policy and said it does not train models on customer prompts. Its Bedrock documentation gives an example involving Claude. Under the AWS review setting, Amazon can keep prompts and answers for certain Claude models inside its cloud for up to 30 days for abuse review. Anthropic does not receive those records. A company can therefore have a commitment against training on its text even when staff can review a flagged request. Google also told Alba it does not use customer prompts for training without permission.

Perla sees a market for smaller computing suppliers that can prove how they handle a customer’s text. TrustedRouter already sells processing capacity on demand. Perla told Alba it processed a billion tokens in one day in August and more than six billion in one day in November. Tokens are the units into which a model divides the text it reads and writes. His planned New York Token Exchange would add a spot market and futures contracts early next year. Tim Guleri of Sierra Ventures, which led the company’s funding, told Alba that banks can secure contractual protections from the largest cloud companies that smaller operators struggle to offer. Guleri expects customers to keep paying for proprietary models when they need them. For work an open model can do, a buyer could choose among competing hosts and check who can read the document before deciding where to send it.
