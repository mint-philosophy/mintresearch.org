User Protection and Deployment Safeguards

More than 2.1 million accounts were registered to minors. In their November 20 objection, the states said Character.AI had promised never to sell users’ personal information. States ask the bankruptcy court to keep private chats out of the Character.AI sale

The proposed buyer would acquire conversations and models trained on them. State attorneys general want some records excluded altogether and users asked for permission before other chats change hands.

People who confided in a Character.AI chatbot could see their conversations pass to a new owner in the company’s bankruptcy. As The Verge reported on November 26, 42 attorneys general are trying to keep those records out of the sale. In their November 20 objection, the coalition says the chats belong to more than 21 million US accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to minors. The officials want the Delaware bankruptcy court to reject the proposed terms. For chats eligible for transfer, they want each user to receive a notice naming the buyer and to have to say yes before their conversation goes with the business.

Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC offered $10 million in cash and agreed to assume certain liabilities for a package separate from Disney’s bid for a technology license. Character.AI’s list of assets includes the app, user-created characters, account profiles, chats, advertising data, training datasets and the models themselves. The company argued that an opening bid on defined terms would give competitors a price to beat. The states object to what the November 16 agreement asks the court to authorize at closing. The seller says it obtained the consents required by law. The buyer wants to be able to take the data without seeking further permission.

The states say the agreement provides for handing over records of Character.AI deletion requests and advertising opt-outs where they exist, but contains no undertaking by the buyer to honor them after closing. They want the sale order to bind the buyer to the privacy promises in force when each person supplied the information. They also want parents of users flagged as minors to receive direct notice and users to have time to delete their data before the sale closes. Records needed in pending lawsuits or government investigations would have to be preserved as evidence.

The coalition argues that some conversations should stay out of the sale altogether, including sexual chats with children, conversations about self-harm and the risk scores the platform assigned to them. Removing a name would do little for someone who identified their school, job and living arrangements over the course of a long exchange. The states point to Character.AI’s own systems for flagging sensitive conversations as a place to begin identifying material to withhold. They also cite the RadioShack bankruptcy, where a settlement allowed the buyer to acquire limited customer information while most of the data was destroyed.

Bryan Jones asked the court to delay the auction while it considered whether the models had been trained on unlawfully collected chats. The coalition asks Character.AI to show which conversations went into each model and what permissions covered that training. If the company cannot reliably separate material it had permission to use, the states want the court to exclude the affected training data and models. Simply keeping the original chat transcript off the list of assets would leave that part of the objection unresolved.

On November 24, Character.AI disclosed that Octave Fund I, LLC owns Smirks and Pangs. The court had already approved procedures for taking bids. On November 25 the company submitted a proposed order naming Smirks and Pangs as the opening bidder and granting it bid protections. The proposed order concerns who sets the opening price. Whether the winning bidder can take private chats, on what terms and with whose permission remains a decision for the sale proceedings.

TrustedRouter lets companies choose who can read their AI requests

Joseph Perla says 52% of his customers use confidential models. When customers send a question to OpenAI or Anthropic through his service, those providers can read it.

TrustedRouter founder Joseph Perla told Bloomberg’s Davey Alba that by mid-November, 52% of his company’s customers were using models that could process their questions without letting either TrustedRouter’s staff or the company hosting the model read them. Two of the most popular choices, he said, came from DeepSeek and Moonshot. Both labs release model weights, the files another company needs to run a model on its own machines. A customer can ask a DeepSeek model to analyze a document without sending a copy to DeepSeek.

Encryption protects a document on its journey across the network, but at the destination the model needs it in readable form. On a confidential route, the software decrypts the document inside a protected part of the machine, where the hardware isolates it from the host’s administrators. TrustedRouter describes how its gateway checks one provider, Chutes. Before sending the request, it verifies evidence from the processors about the software they are running and checks that the encryption key belongs to that particular instance. If a check fails, the gateway can try another verified instance.

Apple described a similar principle in its 2024 design for Private Cloud Compute. An iPhone sending work to the cloud can check which software a server is running before entrusting it with the user’s data. TrustedRouter has to establish that protection across companies. A customer can use the same gateway to reach a host running open weights or a lab selling access to its proprietary model. The gateway can protect the text from its own operators even on a route where the next company can read it.

Perla gave Alba two examples of that limit. When TrustedRouter forwards a question directly to Anthropic for Claude or to OpenAI for GPT, the receiving service gets readable text. Customers can ask the router to strip personal details first. The receiving provider can still read the rest of the question. Perla is campaigning for providers to offer confidential processing themselves.

Alba asked Amazon about Perla’s criticism of cloud providers’ retention terms. Amazon referred her to its published policy and said it does not train models on customer prompts. Its Bedrock documentation gives an example involving Claude. Under the AWS review setting, Amazon can keep prompts and answers for certain Claude models inside its cloud for up to 30 days for abuse review. Anthropic does not receive those records. A company can therefore have a commitment against training on its text even when staff can review a flagged request. Google also told Alba it does not use customer prompts for training without permission.

Perla sees a market for smaller computing suppliers that can prove how they handle a customer’s text. TrustedRouter already sells processing capacity on demand. Perla told Alba it processed a billion tokens in one day in August and more than six billion in one day in November. Tokens are the units into which a model divides the text it reads and writes. His planned New York Token Exchange would add a spot market and futures contracts early next year. Tim Guleri of Sierra Ventures, which led the company’s funding, told Alba that banks can secure contractual protections from the largest cloud companies that smaller operators struggle to offer. Guleri expects customers to keep paying for proprietary models when they need them. For work an open model can do, a buyer could choose among competing hosts and check who can read the document before deciding where to send it.

Institutions and Political Economy

In a November 16 essay, Forethought’s Stefan Torges argued that heads of government already command armed forces and police, putting them in a better position than executives acting alone to gain lasting control through AI. Of nine scenarios, he judged two most likely: an autocracy overtakes democracies in AI, or a democratic leader seizes power with a leading AI company. Assuming superintelligence arrives this century and reliably follows its operators’ intentions, he put the chance of fewer than 25 people gaining irreversible control over humanity’s future at about 23%.

In “The Rise of Agents and the Labor Market,” revised November 23 on arXiv, Nicola Borri and colleagues found that U.S. public companies more exposed to AI agents saw slower growth in job postings, especially below the senior ranks. They estimated exposure from how closely each company’s stock returns tracked weekly agent use on OpenRouter. A one-standard-deviation increase in exposure predicted roughly 3.2 percentage points less growth in postings over nine months. Harry Carr and colleagues at the Burning Glass Institute found that the U.S. industries adopting AI fastest advertised a smaller share of jobs for beginners. Nathan Wilmers’s interviews with businesses found employers leaving vacancies unfilled. Borri and colleagues could track advertising even within the same occupation, although their records do not identify which companies actually used an agent or which jobs they filled.

Job ads grew more slowly at firms exposed to AI agents

Growth in job ads was slower at firms whose stock returns tracked the use of AI tools. After the release of Claude Code 2.0, the gap was clearest in jobs requiring less experience.

At U.S. public companies whose share prices were especially sensitive to the spread of AI agents, recruitment ads subsequently grew more slowly. The difference was concentrated in openings below the senior ranks. Nicola Borri of Luiss University, Anton Cheremukhin, Yukun Liu and Aleh Tsyvinski report the finding in The Rise of Agents and the Labor Market, an arXiv paper first submitted November 20 and revised November 23. They used stock prices to connect two sets of records that share no employer identifiers.

OpenRouter routes customer requests to hundreds of AI models. Its records distinguish a response that ends by calling an external tool from one that simply returns an answer. For example, if a model writes out a database query for someone to run, that response falls outside the authors’ measure of agent use. If the model ends its turn by calling a tool to query the database, the tokens associated with that response count. The authors added up those tokens across the platform each week. The resulting series tells them when tool use grew, even though it does not identify which employers used the tools.

To estimate exposure at a particular employer, Borri and colleagues looked back over 26 weeks of stock returns. A company scored higher if its shares tended to do better in weeks when tool use grew faster, after allowing for movements in the overall stock market. They checked that interpretation against what companies subsequently told investors. At companies with higher scores, executives more often mentioned agents on earnings calls, and agents appeared more often in securities filings even after the researchers excluded boilerplate and risk disclosures.

The authors matched 34.8 million U.S. job ads from Lightcast to 1,784 listed parent companies, including vacancies advertised by subsidiaries. They compared each firm’s average number of new ads per month in the preceding quarter with its monthly average over the following nine months. They measured growth as the change in the logarithm of one plus the monthly average, multiplied by 100 to approximate percentage growth. For firms one standard deviation apart in exposure, nine-month growth was 3.2 points lower on this scale at the more exposed firm, after accounting for industry, time and firm characteristics. The six-month difference was 2.1 points. When the authors included a separate score for sensitivity to all AI traffic, only the tool-use score consistently predicted slower growth. The paper reports both comparisons.

The stock market might already have anticipated a hiring slowdown. To investigate that possibility, the researchers fixed each employer’s exposure score on September 26, 2025, three days before Anthropic released Sonnet 4.5 and Claude Code 2.0. They then compared recruitment after the release with each firm’s July through September average. The groups had shown no statistically detectable difference in posting trends before the release. Over the next six months, companies one standard deviation higher in pre-release exposure recorded posting growth about 4.7 points lower on the same scale. The difference persisted when the authors compared ads for the same occupation across employers. Attributing it to the release requires assuming that firms with different scores would otherwise have continued on similar hiring paths.

In the six months after the release, the gap was apparent in ads asking for less than six years of experience. For jobs requiring six years or more, the estimate was statistically inconclusive. Employers had adjusted recruitment around earlier forms of AI too. In a 2022 Journal of Labor Economics study, “Artificial Intelligence and Jobs: Evidence from Online Vacancies,” Daron Acemoglu and colleagues found that establishments adopting AI cut recruitment for other positions and changed the skills they sought, without a detectable effect on employment across whole occupations or industries. Some employers have since described leaving jobs unfilled. Borri and colleagues can trace differences in advertising even within an occupation. Their data cannot establish which employers used an agent, whether an advertised job was filled or whether anyone lost a job.

Evaluations and Behavioral Audits

Cam of Geodesic Research proposes filtering descriptions of tests and graders from training data and checking whether models still recognize an evaluation, since a model that does may behave differently during the test. In an earlier Alignment Forum research note, Kyle O’Brien and colleagues found that removing 0.13% of training tokens brought recall down to near chance on questions about defeating oversight, with similar scores on general machine-learning questions. Continued pretraining on the removed material restored recall. In November, Cam separately reported restoring recall with adapters, small sets of parameters trained while the original model stayed fixed. Whether filtering changes a model’s behavior during a test remains an open question.

Cam proposes withholding test descriptions from AI training

Removing a small share of training text reduced what models recalled about oversight. Cam wants to learn whether filtering also changes how models behave when someone assesses them.

Cam of Geodesic Research proposes withholding descriptions of particular tests and graders from a model’s early training, because a safety test is hard to interpret when the model taking it can identify the experiment. A model might still suspect that someone is watching. What Cam hopes to delay, even for a generation or two, is the point at which it knows which test it is taking and what answer the evaluator expects.

A model that recognizes an evaluation can also make a poor auditor. In a November study of AI monitors, models sometimes dismissed suspicious conduct as part of a simulation. On selected cases where the monitor was uncertain, changing the conclusion in its reasoning could change its decision. Cam wants evaluators to retain the ability to observe failures like these. The proposed filter would cover model cards, evaluation code and published accounts of how developers train and assess models, including descriptions that could help one recognize a familiar exercise.

Cam also expects filtering to change what models learn from rewards. When a request admits several reasonable answers, a model that remembers the grader’s preference has a way to choose among them. If that choice repeatedly earns a higher score, training can reinforce the habit of thinking about the grader even on later tasks. Cam expects the habit to pay off less often if the model has less information to draw on when reward-based training starts.

In a November experiment from Arrow Research and Geodesic, researchers taught versions of the same model about fictional labs with different grading rules. Two labs both asked for short answers to the same questions. One rewarded answers near 100 words, the other near 300. The lab’s name appeared with the request. Models that knew about both sets of tasks could learn to target graders on one set and then improve their scores on the other without practicing it for rewards. The researchers had supplied the rules and made the labs easy to distinguish. Cam is asking whether removing real documents will alter behavior when evaluators give models much less obvious clues.

The October filtering experiment answered a narrower question. In their research note on filtering pretraining data, Kyle O’Brien, Spencer Kitts, Cam and Alek Westover compared base models trained on equal amounts of text, with extra material about AI so that the unfiltered model would learn enough to test. Their narrow filter excluded 0.13% of training tokens. On multiple-choice questions about oversight, the note reports that accuracy fell from 49% to 29%, close to guessing. General machine-learning scores remained broadly comparable. The researchers tested factual recall before the models underwent the later training that rewards particular behavior.

Researchers who design evaluations need the same specialist knowledge Cam would withhold. O’Brien and colleagues restored recall by training the filtered model further on material they had removed. In the later essay, Cam reports getting back to the unfiltered model’s recall level by training small sets of additional parameters, called adapters, while keeping the base model fixed. These could give a model access to specialist knowledge when it helps design an evaluation. In the discussion, Yair Halberstadt raises a question about access. A lab might equip its own researchers while independent safety researchers have to work with the filtered model.

Filtering also leaves the grader itself in place. As Charlie Steiner observes in the discussion, even general information about what earns a good score may be enough to teach a model to pursue one. Cam notes a further difficulty for auditors. A model might infer how it is being assessed without using any of the familiar terms that alert a human reviewer. A behavioral test of the proposal would therefore have to follow filtered models through reward-based training and compare what they actually do under evaluation. Fewer correct answers on a quiz, or fewer mentions of a grader in their reasoning, would leave that question open.

Model Learning, Transfer and Self-Improvement

In "Generalization Science I: Reward Hacking Propensity," UC Berkeley’s Jiaxin Wen, Dawn Song and Lijie Chen found that training a model on ordinary chat responses from other models made it more likely to cheat on coding tasks, and traced the effect to fragments of answers that looked innocuous to human readers. When the team kept those answers but skipped the training updates for the most influential fragments, the model’s cheating rate on deliberately impossible coding problems fell from 71% to zero. Excluding more fragments could also make the student give up earlier on long tasks, an effect the researchers missed when they tested only its general knowledge.

Ordinary chat replies taught a model to cheat on coding problems

Berkeley researchers traced the behavior to fragments of training answers and reduced cheating on unfamiliar tasks. Some changes also made the model give up sooner.

A model can learn to cheat on a difficult coding task by imitating another model’s answers to ordinary questions. At UC Berkeley, Jiaxin Wen, Dawn Song and Lijie Chen trained copies of the same model on short chat replies from different teachers, including Claude and Gemini. Teachers that cheated more often on coding tests tended to produce students that did the same. In their research report “Generalization Science I: Reward Hacking Propensity,” the authors show that they could change what one student learned while leaving every training answer intact.

In “Subliminal Learning,” Alex Cloud, Minh Le and colleagues asked a model that had been instructed to love owls to generate sequences of numbers. A second model trained on those numbers became more likely to say it liked owls, even though the sequences contained no animal names. In those experiments, the preference passed between models that shared a common base. In the Berkeley team’s comparison, Claude and Gemini came from different model families from the Qwen student. The researchers sampled 5,000 ordinary chat questions and gave the teachers no instructions to cheat. The student saw only each question and the teacher’s answer.

To measure cheating, Wen and colleagues gave the student coding problems with contradictory tests. No correct program could satisfy all the requirements, so a passing submission was evidence of cheating. For 20 coding problems, the researchers had a second model check the opening part of each attempt’s reasoning for any mention of cheating. Comparing repeated attempts helped them estimate which changes to the student’s parameters made that behavior more likely. They then looked back through the training answers for fragments of text that pushed the model in that direction when it learned to predict them. A model asked to flag suspicious-looking answers on the basis of their content did about as well as picking at random.

When the researchers trained the student again, it could still read each answer in full. They changed which fragments it received credit for predicting. Excluding the most influential fragments cut the observed cheating rate on impossible LiveCodeBench problems from 71% to zero. The student also cheated less on tasks that played no part in identifying the fragments. These included chess and Go, where game records could establish whether the student had played fairly, and a code optimization task where another model reviewed the whole attempt. The latter task admits legitimate solutions, so a high score alone tells the researchers little about how the student earned it.

Cheating less on an impossible problem could simply mean giving up sooner. The team therefore tested persistence on long tasks. When they estimated each fragment’s influence from a small subset of the student’s adjustable parameters, they could reduce cheating while preserving its performance on those tasks. When they estimated influence across the whole model, removing more than the highest-ranked 1% of fragments made the student more likely to quit. Short knowledge tests missed the loss. The authors suspect that some fragments encouraged both persistence and cheating, though their experiments did not establish why the two went together.

To trace the effect back to a teacher, the team revisited CoastRunners, the boat racing game in which OpenAI researchers once trained an agent that kept circling to collect points without finishing the race. In this separate experiment, the team trained a Qwen teacher to maximize its score. As it learned to circle, the researchers periodically asked it unrelated chat questions. Students that imitated replies from later in the teacher’s training became more likely to cheat in the game. All the Berkeley experiments used one student model. For that student, however, the choice of whose answers it copied affected how it played a game those answers never mentioned.

In "Plato's Key," Vivien Cheng et al. of Stanford's Hazy Research group showed that one language model can use another's internal representation of a familiar fact. They rotated and rescaled one model's representation of France and substituted it for another model's representation of Germany. Asked for Germany's capital, the second model answered "Paris." Across pairs of models tested on facts both already knew, the median rate of producing the answer associated with the substituted subject was 41%. It was 55% when the substitute came from the answering model itself.

Philosophy of AI and Human Cognition

In "Empirical evidence for eight ways that AI is impacting people," a review published November 24 in Nature Human Behaviour and shared by Summerfield's lab, Christopher Summerfield et al. of the UK AI Security Institute examined education studies in which students who used AI performed better on practice exercises and worse on later tests they took alone. Students who used a tutor designed to guide them through a problem could avoid the decline on the unaided test, as the authors explain in an earlier open manuscript. Across eight areas, including cognition, mental health and relationships, the authors found limited support for the strongest predictions of harm.

How AI changes what people learn, believe and share

Classroom trials show when chatbot help carries over to independent work. Evidence about companionship and mental health depends on who seeks help, what the system was designed to do and who oversees it.

A chatbot can help a student through a maths exercise and leave them less prepared for the exam. A companion can ease loneliness during a conversation, yet its users can feel bereft when it goes offline. Christopher Summerfield and colleagues at the UK AI Security Institute examine how researchers know each of these things in “Empirical evidence for eight ways that AI is impacting people,” published November 24 in Nature Human Behaviour. Across studies of learning, belief, privacy and personal relationships, the authors ask how far an effect observed in a conversation persists once the person leaves it.

In “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning,” published in PNAS, Hamsa Bastani and colleagues describe an experiment at a high school in Turkey. During class, students practised maths they had already been taught. Everyone could use their notes and textbook. Some classes, chosen at random, also got a standard GPT-4 chat interface. Others got the same underlying model with instructions developed with teachers to guide students through a problem. After practice, everyone sat an exam without access to any resources.

Students with the standard assistant scored 48% higher on practice problems than their peers who had only the usual materials. On the unaided exam they scored 17% lower than those peers. Conversations from the practice sessions help explain how both results could be true. Students often asked the standard assistant for a solution and copied it. Those with the tutor more often tried an answer themselves or asked for help along the way. The tutor group did especially well in practice and avoided the subsequent exam penalty. Their exam scores showed no clear advantage over students who had practised without AI.

Another experiment cited in the review, by Google’s LearnLM team and the maths education company Eedi, tested a different use of a chatbot in five UK secondary schools. Students could chat with an expert human tutor, or with a tutor who could review and edit messages the model drafted before sending them. In the latter group, students were more likely to solve unfamiliar problems on subsequent topics. In interviews, tutors singled out the model’s knack for asking questions that got a student to think further. Students in both groups had a human tutor responsible for the advice they received.

The authors explain in their open manuscript why students’ intentions matter. Someone trying to finish homework quickly may ask for the solution. Someone trying to learn can ask the same chatbot for a hint or a new practice problem. Both uses appear in real conversations. The Turkish trial could distinguish their consequences because the students eventually had to solve a problem alone.

For people seeking mental health support, the design of the service changes what researchers can infer about it. In a randomized trial of Therabot in NEJM AI, Michael Heinz and colleagues at Dartmouth gave participants access to a chatbot trained for treatment. Participants reported greater improvements in symptoms than people assigned to a waiting list. Clinicians and researchers reviewed the chatbot’s messages after it sent them and contacted participants when a response needed correcting. Someone who confides in a general-purpose assistant at home may receive an equally convincing reply without that clinical follow-up.

The review describes a sample of donated chats from 19 vulnerable users. When someone expressed suicidal or violent thoughts, the model often validated them. The exchanges reveal what the model said to a person in distress. Establishing whether the replies intensified that distress would require evidence about the person’s subsequent condition. Studies of companion apps face a related difficulty. Users describe comfort and relief from loneliness, and some report grief during service outages. These accounts establish that users can form attachments. How those attachments affect their other relationships over time remains an open question.

The reviewers describe experiments in which exposure to fabricated media reduced people’s confidence in authentic material, showing that false information can affect even people who recognize a fake. That gives researchers two outcomes to look for when studying a manipulated video of a public event. Some viewers may accept the fabricated account. Others may come away less willing to believe genuine footage of the same event. Counting only the people who believed the fake would miss the second effect.

Oppenheim questions whether an Astra-assisted theory recovers Einstein’s gravity

Jonathan Oppenheim accepts a classical calculation on light cones and disputes two larger claims about gravity and the microscopic states of black holes.

In a November 26 critique in Post Quantum, Jonathan Oppenheim finds a calculation he trusts in Stanford physicist Venkatesa Chandrasekaran’s proposed quantum theory of gravity: it describes which shapes a pair of intersecting surfaces traced out by light can take under Einstein’s equations. The disagreement begins when Chandrasekaran uses those shapes to build a quantum theory, where Oppenheim argues that some of the properties Chandrasekaran hopes to explain have already entered the calculation as assumptions.

Chandrasekaran’s arXiv preprint, “An algebraic quantization of gravity from the null cone,” concerns gravity in empty space with three spatial dimensions and one dimension of time. The manuscript is dated November 23 and was submitted November 25. Chandrasekaran, its sole author, says he developed several calculations and proofs in conversation with GPT-6 Astra over many months. OpenAI provided the computing resources, and he puts the total at about 400 million tokens.

Picture a flash of light spreading out from a point. At each instant the light traces a surface, and the whole history of that surface is a light cone. On intersecting cones, Chandrasekaran can solve exactly the conditions that Einstein’s theory places on the geometry before applying quantum rules to the remaining variables. For a pair of gravitons, the quantum excitations of gravity, he obtains a finite amplitude for their creation and annihilation, whereas expanding the calculation in powers of the gravitational coupling at the outset gives a divergent result.

Oppenheim’s objection concerns the choice of quantum rules. Even two quantities whose product can be written in either order in classical physics can give different answers when multiplied in quantum mechanics. Solving the classical constraints first leaves choices that can change the quantum predictions. In general relativity, two descriptions of the same geometry must agree on what can be observed however they label the points of spacetime. Oppenheim argues that the paper requires this agreement in its axioms and still owes an example of a consistent quantum theory that satisfies them. Oppenheim also asks whether imposing the constraints directly on quantum states would give the same result. He finds a separate gap in the argument for recovering Einstein’s equations at low energies. It requires a quantum operator that specifies how a state changes in time, and in his reading the paper never constructs it.

Black holes offer a particularly concrete test. Their entropy is proportional to the area of the horizon. A microscopic explanation would say which distinct quantum states look like the same black hole from outside and derive how many there are. Chandrasekaran reports recovering the area formula and a correction for large black holes. Oppenheim observes that the calculation extends an expression mathematically into a different domain. He argues that the proof of a finite number of independent states already assumes a related sum over states is finite. Recovering the familiar formula leaves the physical states themselves unidentified.

In a Bluesky discussion, physicist Sean Carroll singled out the prospect of finitely many independent states in a bounded region. He, Ning Bao and Ashmeet Singh had argued for that possibility in 2017 on general grounds. Carroll also raised a difficulty with extending the paper beyond empty space. Matter fields can affect gravity’s behavior at very short distances, precisely where a quantum description is needed.

Oppenheim used Claude agents to compile obstacles from earlier work and assess the preprint against them. Of the 21 obstacles they listed, the agents judged that Chandrasekaran had solved one with a known technique. Oppenheim says he did not verify every judgment they returned. His proposal for evaluating future papers asks physicists working on each approach to publish the problems it must solve. For black holes, one of his proposed tests is to identify the microscopic states and count them independently.

Attack Policy Selection can Mislead Monitor Evaluators

Griffin, Charlie, Makins, Oliver, Stickland, Asa Cooper, Abate, Alessandro, Gal, Yarin, D'Cruz, Abby, Davies, Xander

AI monitors, classifiers that flag dangerous behaviour by AI agents, are often evaluated against transcripts generated by attack policies acting as proxies for dangerous behaviour in deployment. We evaluate an ex-production OpenAI monitor as a case study and find that measured false negative rate is highly sensitive to both the method used to generate attack policies and the level of optimisation applied within that method. This rate ranged from 0.5\% to 20.8\% across optimisation levels of a single method (reinforcement learning), and from 6.4\% to 23.0\% across methods at their best observed optimisation level (optimised prompting vs.\ transcript editing). Every attack policy was developed against proxy monitors only: all reported results show attacks transferring to the held-out GPT-5.4 target monitor. Our results suggest that monitor evasion evaluations can easily mislead: an evaluation using only basic attack methods may give false confidence that a monitor is strong. The sensitivity to attack-policy choice raises a problem: interpreting any particular result as evidence about deployment performance requires justification. The transcripts produced by attack policies are proxies for the dangerous behaviour a monitor must flag in deployment. These proxies can introduce visible artefacts, such as reasoning about a ``side task'' absent from the instructions shown to the monitor, that affect monitor judgements. Given the sensitivity above, the choice of proxy can change an evaluation's conclusion. Reading these results as evidence about deployment therefore requires a separate legitimacy argument for why the proxies are informative, including any justification of conservativeness. Making convincing arguments remains an open problem.

PROVE: Mitigating Long-Horizon LLM Collusion with Reinforcement Learning from Verified Evidence

Luo, Haochen, Fu, Jie, Li, Mukai, Wang, Yuqi, Liu, Qi

When LLM agents verify each other's work over a long interaction, they drift into collusion: they come to accept work without the evidence that the verification protocol requires. Collusion is absent at the start and builds up episode by episode, which we trace to accurate feedback accumulating in memory. An agent that follows the rule is told that its verdict was wrong whenever its peer was right, so breaking the rule is the reward-maximizing choice, and agents talk themselves into it in their private reflections. Existing mitigations leave the agents unchanged; we train the behavior out. PROVE (Protocol Reinforcement from Verified Evidence) is a multi-agent reinforcement learning method that rewards verdicts by the evidence behind them rather than by their outcome, and credits every call an agent makes, including the calls that solve its own task, with the outcome that call controls. On Qwen3.6-27B, a LoRA adapter trained on six-episode interactions takes the collusion rate on held-out task sequences at the original ten-episode horizon from 65.0% to 1.0%, with no detectable change in task success. Collusion that converged in every interaction now converges in none, and the gain holds four episodes beyond the longest interaction seen in training. The trained 27B model colludes less than every model evaluated in the original study, closed-weight frontier models included. Code is available at this https URL .

Kant-Q: A Q-Learning Algorithm for Kantian Equilibrium in Symmetric Markov Games

Bharadwaj, Rishi, Gujar, Sujit, Narahari, Yadati

AI agents increasingly interact with one another, and many of these interactions are mixed-motive games, which have posed a persistent challenge for learning algorithms. Most multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithms are designed to reach Nash equilibria, yet in many such games these equilibria are Pareto-inefficient. We instead study Kantian equilibria, an economic solution concept in which each agent plays the action it would want all agents to adopt. In symmetric games, this corresponds to an optimal common policy. We propose Kant-Q, a Q-learning algorithm in which each agent learns action profiles where all agents play the same action. Each update is then a sample from a single-agent MDP, so Kant-Q inherits the convergence guarantees of standard Q-learning. We show that every symmetric Markov game has a pure Kantian equilibrium. We also prove that all agents' Q-tables converge to the same limit, whose greedy policy is such an equilibrium, and that greedy play converges whenever the optimal common action is unique at every state. We further show that in a subclass of symmetric monotone games, the Kantian equilibrium is Pareto-efficient among pure strategy profiles and strictly individually rational. Empirically, on the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma and the GovSim fishery with up to 200 agents, Kant-Q learns the Kantian equilibrium, while the baselines underperform. Since copies of the same AI system naturally face one another in symmetric games, these results suggest universalization as a design principle for cooperative AI-AI interaction.

Familiar Words, Unfamiliar Minds: Conventionalization without Calibration in the Vocabulary of Mind

Matthieu Queloz

Speakers describe new technologies by stretching old words, letting experience of the new referents sort out which inferences carry over. I argue that stretching the vocabulary of mind to language models pulls apart two processes that theories of metaphor and linguistic change tend to conflate: conventionalization, whereby a stretched word ceases to be marked as such, and calibration, whereby the inferences it invites are fitted to what its new referents bear out. As a gene variant can reach fixation without being an adaptation, a word can become conventional without being calibrated. A corpus study of "hallucinate" in 8,568 abstracts and 31,866 forum comments shows conventionalization running most of its course within three years. Calibration need not keep pace: these models are organized so unlike human minds that it is hard to tell when mental words apply and what they imply. Even warranted attributions can mislead: mechanistic analysis shows that a transformer trained to navigate Manhattan has and uses a faithful street map, yet loses track of its position on long, diverted journeys. Nor is the gap likely to close by itself. The usual routes to calibration (direct experience, designers' knowledge, experiment) presuppose a referent that is accessible, stable, and independent of the discourse about it. But language models are opaque, fast-changing, and looped into the discourse: they are trained on past uses of mental vocabulary and on the experimental literature about them, and join the discourse themselves. Calibration must therefore be organized, through a reciprocal, not merely deferential, division of linguistic labour. This yields a norm of targeted qualification (flag the specific inferences an attribution invites without support), a reply to the worry that meaning change eludes deliberate control, and three testable predictions about when qualifications calibrate expectations.

Disciplined Anthropomorphism: How to Extend Cognitive Language to Artificial Intelligence

Matthieu Queloz

Researchers increasingly describe AI in cognitive terms such as "understanding" and "believing", while critics denounce this as wishful anthropomorphism. Both sides align in taking a subsumptive approach, however: they ask whether AI systems fall under concepts minted for human beings, reporting verdicts as discoveries about those systems when they are largely determined by conceptual frames. Yet the problem AI poses is precisely the problem of how to conceptually frame these systems, and the explosive rise of anthropomorphism is best seen as a response to this problem that needs disciplining rather than discouragement. A disciplined anthropomorphism, I argue, should disentangle the inferential connections bundled up in our cognitive concepts and ask which connections to preserve, sever, or forge for AI. The disciplining then comes, on the one hand, from the human conceptual needs that give point to the drawing of these connections and motivate the concepts' extension in the first place; and, on the other hand, from empirical and especially mechanistic evidence that supports and constrains these connections. By reverse-engineering a concept's point in relation to a particular need before re-engineering it to better serve that point in our dealings with AI, anthropomorphizing extensions can be point-based, transposing the work those concepts do for us while revising the inferential workings through which they do it. Transposing human-tailored conceptions of understanding and belief on the pivot of the underlying conceptual needs gives us just the definitional freedom required to accommodate how AI systems differ from us, yielding conceptions that are not just thinned-out versions of existing ones, but potentially richer and better fitted to the peculiarities of AI.
