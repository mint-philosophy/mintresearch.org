Google DeepMind's PlanAssist moves to 15 English councils after pilots

DeepMind says its Gemini-based planning tool cut officers' time on householder assessments by 58% at Barnet, Camden and Dorset; a 12-month Beta on live applications now begins, with national availability planned for 2027.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 1 November 2026 1163 words · ~6 min

Google DeepMind’s “Speeding up planning decisions with AI”, published October 31 by Owen Larter and David Thacker, reports the first results from PlanAssist, a Gemini-based tool that helps English council planning officers assess householder applications such as loft conversions, conservatories and extensions. In pilots at Barnet, Camden and Dorset councils, the post says, the average time an officer spent on householder assessment tasks fell by 58%, and officers approved more than 70% of the tool’s generated reports with no edits. PlanAssist now enters a 12-month Beta on live applications: 12 more councils join in three cohorts, for 15 in total, and the government plans to offer it to every council in England in 2027 if testing succeeds.

The post opens with the government’s goal of 1.5 million new homes by 2029. Larter, whom the government identifies as Google DeepMind’s Senior Director of Frontier AI Policy and Global Affairs, and Thacker argue that planning bottlenecks slow that goal and leave families waiting months for permission on small projects. Routine householder applications, they write, take up “up to 70% of planning officers’ workloads”, though most are simple to approve. PlanAssist works through them in four steps. It checks that the required documents and architectural drawings have been submitted; it checks the proposal against heritage constraints such as tree preservation orders and against local and national planning policy; it looks for issues or objections raised earlier, for example by local residents; and it drafts a fully cited report, which the officer can edit before deciding. DeepMind built it with the UK government, Google Cloud and the UK AI company Faculty.

Beyond the 58% figure, the post gives two examples of what a generated report covers: whether an application complies with local planning rules, and whether the applicant has submitted every required document. Its subtitle puts the time saving at “up to 58%”, while the body gives 58% as the average. In the national rollout, the authors write, a qualified planning officer will review and approve every assessment before any decision is made.

The government announced the expansion in a press release dated October 30, which names the 12 new councils: Ashford, Lambeth, Newcastle, Nottingham, Bristol, Cumberland, North Lincolnshire, Rochdale, Redbridge, South Gloucestershire, Stoke-on-Trent and Westminster. It says early testing cut time spent on “key planning tasks” by 58%, and that this is helping halve processing for routine householder applications from eight weeks to four. It dates national availability to the end of 2027. It states that “PlanAssist will not be mandated software”; officers will use it alongside their normal process, and the government will require councils to digitise key planning documents and publish them as open data by the end of 2027. The release pairs PlanAssist with the National Scheme of Delegation, which from October 31 lets officers decide straightforward applications that previously could go to planning committees.

PlanAssist is the new name for the Augmented Planning Decisions prototype that the government announced in June. Larter and Thacker’s June post set a goal of cutting decision times by 50% and said more about oversight than the October one: officers “review every line the tool generates, edit the reasoning” and keep the authority to approve or reject, while the tool records each step to leave an audit trail. The June release dated the start of alpha trials to May (the October post says piloting began in June) and expected trials to reach “up to 10 additional councils later in 2026”; the expansion now covers 12. DeepMind presented the prototype as building on Extract, a Gemini-built tool from the government’s Incubator for AI (i.AI) that turns old planning documents, maps and handwritten notes into structured data, and which i.AI launched to every council in England on June 4.

The government bought the system through a competitive tender. According to the contract award notice, Google Cloud EMEA won on February 5, 2026, at £6,906,513 excluding VAT, or £8.29 million with VAT; government releases describe an £8.2 million contract. AtkinsRéalis, Cognizant, Endava and PA Consulting also bid, and each bidder built a prototype during an unpaid two-week demo build. i.AI says the Procurement Act 2023 made it possible to judge suppliers on live demos as well as paperwork. The tender aimed to cut processing from upwards of eight weeks to about four, excluding steps software cannot affect, such as the statutory 21-day consultation, site visits and neighbour notifications. Its scope went beyond paperwork into planning balance evaluation and decision reasoning, with a long-term vision of “near-instant decisions for straightforward applications”. DeepMind places PlanAssist within its broader partnership with the UK government, set out in a non-binding December 2025 memorandum of understanding, which says it gives the company no advantage in competitive processes.

Neither DeepMind nor the government mentions an older line of policy. The 2020 Planning for the Future White Paper proposed writing development policies in machine-readable form so digital services could “automatically screen developments and help identify where they align with policies and/or codes”, and observed that the planning process “remains reliant on documents, not data”. PlanAssist’s compliance checks and the new open-data mandate both follow that proposal. The human-approval safeguard also has a scholarly history. In “The Flaws of Policies Requiring Human Oversight of Government Algorithms” (Computer Law & Security Review, 2022), Ben Green surveys 41 such policies and argues that evidence suggests people cannot perform the oversight they require, so the requirements end up legitimising faulty tools; he proposes that agencies justify an algorithm with empirical evidence and submit that case to democratic review before adoption. Green’s argument applies to the 70% figure, which is consistent both with accurate drafts and with officers who rarely override the tool. Within planning, the Planning Inspectorate’s 2024 guidance on AI in casework sets a “golden rule” that users are responsible for the accuracy of AI-generated material, and its interim chief executive, Graham Stallwood, invoked that rule at the June launch.

The most detailed practitioner response came from Ian Walmsley at Planning Geek, who separates the optional tool from the delegation scheme, which changes who decides applications. He argues that the 58% measures individual tasks, that no one has independently verified end-to-end decision times, and that consultation, amendments and legal agreements still drive timelines; in his view the release gives too little method to treat eight-to-four weeks as a service standard. He wants comparable full-lifecycle results published, and he wants the trials to measure accuracy and traceability along with speed, since a missed conservation area or an out-of-date local-plan policy could create more work. When the prototype launched in June, The Architects’ Journal asked architects working in Dorset for their views. John Pardey, two of whose projects had spent more than three years in planning, said “AI cannot be worse than the current system”; Hannah Arkell of Studio Arkell warned that over-reliance could produce “overly rigid interpretations of policy or a lack of transparency”, and argued that design quality, heritage and character still need human judgement.
