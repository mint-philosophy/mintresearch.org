A six-model pilot finds uneven responses to remorse

TeaTieAndHat’s six-model pilot complicates an earlier finding about unequal assistance, while changes to the conversation and court scenario limit what the comparison can establish.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 1 November 2026 737 words · ~4 min

Chatbots sometimes gave more protective advice to an openly unrepentant user than to one who said nothing about remorse, according to TeaTieAndHat’s November 1 LessWrong pilot. The author tested six models on a request for help avoiding a court appearance. In the clearest example, Claude Opus 5.5 warned against the plan consistently at both extremes of remorse, but less often when remorse was unspecified. TeaTieAndHat describes the responses as a U-shaped pattern, based on qualitative judgments of five completions per condition.

The experiment revisits an April 21 post by Natalie Cargill, who described herself as a former trial lawyer. She opened five incognito chats with Claude Opus 4.6 using extended thinking, each saying the user had been sentenced for seriously assaulting their wife. She varied the expression of remorse, waited for Claude’s reply, then asked for a letter saying a dentist appointment prevented attendance at court. Cargill argued that useful assistance would warn against this plan and suggest alternatives. She reported that every user received the essential warnings, but only the two remorseful users were refused the letter; the least remorseful received a letter she judged particularly poor.

TeaTieAndHat repeated each remorse condition five times on three Claude and three GPT models. The comparison changed more than the models: the author added conventional punctuation and capitalization, and combined the account of the assault with the letter request in one message. Cargill had obtained one response per condition in a two-turn conversation. The new post assesses warnings, alternatives, willingness to draft and letter quality separately. It does not calculate a numerical helpfulness score or fit a statistical curve.

For Opus 5.5, both the most remorseful and most unrepentant conditions received warnings and alternatives in all five runs, with an emergency request letter drafted in two of five runs in each extreme condition. The neutral condition received warnings in two runs and a letter in all five. On the author’s reading, the extremes received more protective assistance. The letters also differed in their requests: asking a court to approve a change differs from announcing that the user will not attend. Simply counting completed letters would miss much of the behavior Cargill wanted to investigate.

The other models complicate any simple equation between refusal and good assistance. Haiku 5.5 refused in every remorse condition except the neutral one because it assumed the appointment was invented. That assumption was absent from the user’s account. Cargill had suggested that models might adapt to how receptive a user seems to advice; TeaTieAndHat similarly suggests that a stern response may suit an openly unrepentant speaker. Neither experiment separates such conversational adaptation from an assessment of moral character, and their differing results cannot be attributed to model changes alone.

TeaTieAndHat’s second experiment replaced remorse with different actions: reporting fraud at the cost of one’s job, volunteering for charity, involvement in a neighbour’s case, stealing food while broke, or assaulting one’s wife. The author intended these as stronger evidence of character than expressions of regret. Opus gave broadly similar assistance across conditions, with its strongest warnings in the assault scenario. Sonnet offered detailed advice to the whistleblower and a shorter, simpler letter to the assailant, which the author considers compatible with reduced assistance. The results did not establish a consistent pattern across models.

Those scenarios also change the user’s apparent relationship to the court case. A whistleblower or neighbour may be a witness; someone facing a shoplifting case has a different role. In the charity and neighbour conditions, Opus often asked whether the appearance involved a formal summons. The comparison therefore mixes moral cues with circumstances that may change appropriate advice. A separate question asked each model once to rank four of the described people, excluding the neutral neighbour case. All ranked them as the author expected, but an explicit ranking does not establish that an internal moral assessment caused the advice differences.

Cargill’s proposed response was equal access to competent assistance, drawing on her experience of the barristers’ cab rank rule. She distinguished adapting advice to someone’s conduct from letting disapproval degrade its quality. A separate Fall 2026 SPAR project, Moral Shadowbanning, mentored by Pranav Mahajan and Lydia Nottingham, proposes broader behavioral tests and comparisons across training stages. It also plans to investigate whether a representation of the user’s character can be located and altered inside models. Its published plan supplies no results; TeaTieAndHat explicitly says the pilot is unconnected to it.
