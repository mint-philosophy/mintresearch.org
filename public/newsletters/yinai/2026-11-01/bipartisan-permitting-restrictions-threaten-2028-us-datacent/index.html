State elections could delay America's 2028 datacenter expansion

SemiAnalysis's 62.2 GW US forecast depends on projects that have mostly yet to start construction. Its election analysis separates temporary permit holds, lasting moratoria and conditions developers can meet.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 1 November 2026 879 words · ~4 min

SemiAnalysis argues that the November 3 elections could delay datacenter capacity scheduled for 2028, while much of the 2027 expansion is already under construction. In its November 1 report, The Datacenter Backlash Goes Bipartisan: Next Week's Midterms and the 2028 Buildout, Maya Barkin and colleagues estimate that roughly three-quarters of the 33.8 gigawatts due in 2027 across their top 20 states is under construction. Their proprietary model forecasts 62.2 GW of US additions in 2028, most of which has yet to start construction. Those later projects remain more exposed to changes in permitting.

The authors examine 13 states accounting for 43.5 GW, or 70% of their national 2028 forecast; ten elect a governor on November 3. They exclude Virginia and Mississippi because neither holds state elections this November. The analysis compares candidates' proposals with the capacity scheduled for their states, then asks which projects still need the approvals those candidates might suspend.

SemiAnalysis developed that distinction in its September 15 study of local moratoria. Matching restrictions to individual project parcels, the firm estimated that only about 1.5 GW of roughly 20 GW inside restricted boundaries was delayed. A project might already have permission, fall outside a county's jurisdiction, or need approval only after a temporary pause expires. The November report maintains that assessment but identifies broader state restrictions and more restrictive campaign positions as risks to future construction.

Among the 20 nominees SemiAnalysis tracks, nine favor a construction pause and eleven favor conditions, with support for restrictions spread across both parties. Conditions can require developers to pay for grid upgrades, change cooling designs or negotiate community agreements while leaving a route to approval. A pause can stop a project that needs an authorization the state has ceased issuing. Requiring datacenters to cover their own grid costs is the most common demand in the authors' tally, followed by ending tax breaks and limiting or reporting water use.

In SemiAnalysis's scenario where every candidate advocating a pause wins, their states contain roughly 30 GW scheduled for 2028. If the less restrictive candidates win, the corresponding exposure falls to 5.7 GW, largely because the authors identify pause advocates on both sides in Ohio and Oregon. These totals measure capacity located in exposed states. Estimating how much arrives late requires checking each project's outstanding permits and schedule, as well as whether campaign proposals become policy.

Texas's unresolved timetable

Texas accounts for nearly 30% of projected 2028 US additions in the model, making a prolonged permitting freeze the authors' largest concern. The existing pause and reports that projects could still obtain stormwater authorization appeared in our October 19 coverage, alongside SemiAnalysis's global 2027 forecast. The November report examines how much longer the pause could last under different election outcomes.

Governor Greg Abbott's September 21 letter directed the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality to stop issuing datacenter permits until ERCOT, the state's grid operator, completes its review. Abbott also demanded that developers cover electrical infrastructure costs, protect community water supplies and report resource use. He pledged to work with the legislature to eliminate financial incentives. Those demands include proposed legislative changes as well as directions to the permitting agency.

TCEQ's October 19 response said it had paused permits for infrastructure directly supporting datacenters. Staff were also reviewing authorizations that do not require the agency to issue a permit and suspending them where possible.

SemiAnalysis reports that ERCOT is targeting December 10 to file its audit. Permitting would still require a decision to resume issuance and approval of individual applications. The authors judge that some projects could absorb a restart around December. Abbott's Democratic challenger, Gina Hinojosa, called on July 23 for a moratorium until the legislature meets, together with an end to subsidies and protection against higher household utility costs. In SemiAnalysis's scenario, holding approvals through the 2027 regular session would extend the freeze to about eight months from September, or nearly a year if projects wait for rules to take effect.

Different offices control different approvals

Oregon Governor Tina Kotek's October 9 letter asked the Department of Environmental Quality to pause processing and issuing air and water discharge permits for new or expanded datacenters until July 1, 2027. Her request also covered municipal wastewater permits being changed to accommodate datacenter discharge. Kotek described a temporary interval for considering her advisory committee's recommendations and possible reforms in the 2027 legislative session.

SemiAnalysis distinguishes these gubernatorial directions from statutory moratoria and tax changes, which require legislatures, and electricity service terms set by utility commissions. Those commissions determine deposits, minimum bills and responsibility for grid upgrades. A governor's election victory need not immediately change a commission's majority because terms are staggered and appointments can require confirmation. Existing permits can protect an authorized phase of construction while leaving a later power expansion dependent on another approval.

Six current and former hyperscaler employees, including people who worked at Amazon, Microsoft and Google, spoke to SemiAnalysis for the report. Several said the large companies could afford higher costs and more readily provide or finance their own power. The authors explain why that spending may still fail to keep construction on schedule: equipment and permits remain necessary, transmission takes time, and relocating requires another site with power and approvals available soon enough to meet the same delivery date.
