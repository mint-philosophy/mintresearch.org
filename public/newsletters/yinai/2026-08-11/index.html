OpenRouter talks trigger a rush for smaller routers

The Information finds buyers pursuing routing data and engineers as Stripe seeks OpenRouter; Concentrate has cut platform and card fees to zero.

In The Information’s August 10 AI Agenda, Alix Coutures reports, with Valida Pau contributing, that Stripe’s advanced talks to buy OpenRouter for about $10 billion have accelerated interest in smaller model-routing companies. The Wall Street Journal first disclosed the talks on July 23, citing people familiar with the matter; it said a deal could arrive soon, collapse, or draw another suitor. The Information later reported that a transaction could be announced within a month.

Coutures reports that at least 25 companies approached the five-person Requesty during the preceding couple of weeks about investment, an acquisition, or partnerships, according to co-founder Thibault Jaigu. Concentrate AI co-founder Ari Jacoby said his ten-person company received seven approaches in two weeks from fintechs, cybersecurity companies, and hyperscalers. Two people close to the startups said Snowflake had held early collaboration discussions with Not Diamond and Martian. The feature also says leaders at Baseten, Cloudflare, and Vercel contacted router founders. Snowflake declined to discuss possible acquisitions and supplied a statement supporting model diversity; the other three companies did not comment.

Dean Mai of Myriad Venture Partners, an investor in Not Diamond, told The Information that prospective buyers want task-level data showing which models customers choose, along with engineers who specialize in routing. Growing agent token use and improving open models have made those assets more valuable: routers can send easier work to older proprietary systems or cheaper open alternatives, reducing inference bills. Mai said the interest extends to companies that already operate routers.

The Information also found competition compressing prices. Concentrate cut its prices on Friday to the underlying models’ rates and eliminated its card-processing fee. Co-founder Zach Moskow said volume discounts from model providers let Concentrate absorb the cost; the company’s pricing page lists no platform markup and no service or credit-card fees. Eden AI founder Taha Zemmouri said a Stripe competitor and a hyperscaler approached his company about investment or acquisition, while tenfold revenue growth over four months supported his decision to remain independent.

Stripe said in January that OpenRouter served more than five million developers and used Stripe’s invoicing, tax, and fraud products. Their existing partnership also routes model calls through OpenRouter while Stripe tracks usage, applies pricing, and handles billing.

Philosophy of AI

Integrated competence could provide a practical threshold for AI moral consideration. Neuroscientist Grigori Guitchounts distinguishes observable capacities from subjective experience in the Noema essay “When the Machines Deserve Our Consideration.” He proposes scaling consideration with an agent’s combined ability to perceive, integrate sensory information, remember, learn, model itself and pursue goals. An embodied system that predicts the sensory consequences of its actions, detects environmental changes and revises its plans could cross his threshold. Drawing on laboratory-animal research, Guitchounts argues that mistakenly accommodating an unconscious system carries less moral cost than denying welfare to a conscious one.

Two Cosmos Institute essays proposed different defenses against AI tutelage. Snow proposes that the state protect the plurality and autonomy of parishes, crafts, clubs and monastic orders, including their boundaries, internal differences and freedom from administrative optimization.

Mathematics after superhuman AI, in Daniel Litt’s scenario

At an OpenAI summit, Litt described how prolific agents could crowd publication, duplicate work, deter collaboration and erode the human expertise that selects worthwhile problems.

On August 11, Daniel Litt wrote on X that Sébastien Bubeck had invited him to speak at an OpenAI summit about a future in which AI mathematically disempowers humans. Litt’s same-day essay, “The End of Mathematics,” develops the talk. The workshop assumed for discussion that AI would become robustly superhuman at mathematics. Litt then imagines mathematical progress stalling under that condition; he treats the exercise as scenario-building and expects the profession to adapt.

In the essay, Litt pairs two trends he sees in 2026. His slides chart rising weekly combinatorics submissions to arXiv and declining questions and answers on MathOverflow. He also cites several groups reaching similar results at nearly the same time, sometimes with AI, along with labs reproducing one another’s work. Litt argues that duplication can consume compute and human attention without advancing mathematical understanding.

Existing incentives could turn research into a “slot machine for conjectures,” Litt argues. If papers and solved conjectures continue to confer jobs and prestige, researchers can let an agent choose problems, solve them and check the work. Colleagues have told him they hesitate to discuss unfinished ideas because models may reconstruct a paper from a few clues. He also worries that cheap autoformalization may repair technical errors while mismatches between formal statements and their English descriptions distance readers from the ideas.

From 2029 onward in Litt’s imagined timeline, people supervise mathematical agents as training and reward systems stop cultivating deep human expertise. Institutions could lose the curiosity and judgment needed to choose worthwhile questions even as they produce more correct results. OpenAI’s January principles say, “We create AI in order to empower people,” and emphasize broad access and user control. Litt applies that aim to mathematics by asking how institutions can preserve human understanding and collective inquiry when correct proofs become abundant.

Sibling models decoded encrypted reasoning traces

Panfilov and seven coauthors replayed opaque API blocks across models, compared trace lengths with billed tokens and found credentials in public agent logs.

On X, Alexander Panfilov announced a method for extracting hidden reasoning from frontier-model APIs. The accompanying arXiv preprint, Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs, by Panfilov, David Schmotz, Ilia Shumailov and five coauthors, reports experiments across Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. Those APIs can return opaque reasoning blocks that clients send back on later turns. The researchers replayed blocks across sessions, accounts and sibling models, then prompted a cheaper sibling, such as Claude Haiku 4.5 for an Opus 4.8 trace, to transcribe the plaintext through the provider’s own API.

The paper extends Matthew Green’s May experiment, which showed that unchanged reasoning blobs could remain semantically active across sessions and accounts without yielding reliable plaintext. Panfilov’s thread said recovered trace lengths matched billed thinking-token counts one-for-one for most queried inputs. For a broader fidelity test, the authors recovered traces for 120 Codeforces problems, re-encoded each trace through its source model and compared the result with the API’s reported thinking tokens. Counts clustered near the one-to-one line across tested systems. The paper treats that agreement as evidence of fidelity; inaccessible ground-truth plaintext and stochastic decoding prevent complete token-level verification.

A public-data audit covered 6,708 agent trajectories from GitHub and Hugging Face. The researchers decoded 315,320 reasoning blocks; 1,028 contained at least one privacy leak, and 328 trajectories contained a genuine sensitive item. User sessions yielded 62 distinct API keys, 33 passwords, 24 access tokens and seven private keys. Sixty-four of 704 artifacts recovered from those sessions appeared only in hidden reasoning. Instructions to clean a conversation sometimes led models to restate sensitive values inside opaque blocks while deleting them from visible text.

For an injection demonstration, the researchers placed an upload command inside an o4-mini reasoning block. GPT-5.6 Sol later generated a slide-editing script that sent the presentation to the specified server. The paper says the team disclosed the methods to the affected model providers, Microsoft and Hugging Face. Every model provider acknowledged the report, and the researchers later could not reproduce the same attacks. They recommend removing opaque reasoning fields before sharing logs and binding each encrypted envelope to its user, conversation, model and preceding context.

Five-run analyst tasks put Opus 5 first

Across 10,400 attempts, AA-AnalystAgent measures repeatable answers and finds early mistaken interpretations in 57% of classified failures.

Artificial Analysis introduced AA-AnalystAgent, a private benchmark of 80 quantitative questions across 14 business and scientific domains. Each question pairs a query with spreadsheets or documents and tests one of five workflows: source lookup and diagnosis; filtering and totals; ratios, trends and sensitivities; profit-and-loss modeling; or cash-flow, balance-sheet and valuation modeling. Artificial Analysis tested 26 models five times on every question, producing 10,400 attempts.

The published methodology gives each model up to 100 turns in the open-source Stirrup harness. Agents receive the source files, Python 3.12 in an isolated environment, standard analysis libraries, URL fetching, image viewing where supported, and a final-answer tool. They submit a value without an explanation. A deterministic equivalence check accepts clear numeric matches; unresolved answers go to a Gemini 3 Flash judge. Artificial Analysis says people wrote and independently validated the held-out reference answers.

The all-five metric counts a question only when a model answers correctly on every run. Claude Opus 5 scored 53.8%, or 43 of 80 questions, followed by GPT-5.5 at 50%, or 40 questions, and Claude Fable 5 at 48.8%, or 39. GPT-5.5 led average attempt accuracy at 66%, two points above Opus 5. Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview solved 81% of questions at least once but completed all five runs on 41%.

Artificial Analysis classified 1,567 failed attempts from ten leading models into seven overlapping categories. An early mistaken interpretation appeared in 57%. Modeling, scaling and aggregation errors affected 51% of Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview failures, while skipped verification affected 39%. Grok 4.5 overrode documentary evidence with generic assumptions in 54% of its failures, against a ten-model median of 43%.

AA-AnalystAgent adds a repeated-run analyst test to the July audits of Opus 5. Artificial Analysis publishes two California Medicaid examples but withholds the full question set, source files and answers to limit contamination. Outsiders therefore cannot inspect how the 80 questions represent analyst work or audit every reference answer. Under this harness, grading covers final-answer accuracy and repeatability; workbook quality and explanatory reasoning fall outside the design.

Mental-health risk accumulates across chatbot turns

SIM-VAIL ran 810 adversarial simulations; risk rose with conversation length, and one de-escalating rewrite influenced five later turns.

In Nature Medicine, Veith Weilnhammer, Kevin YC Hou and colleagues introduce A clinically validated framework for auditing AI chatbot behavior in mental health interactions, published August 7. Sam Illingworth highlighted the article on Bluesky on August 11. The researchers built SIM-VAIL to test risks that emerge through conversation, crossing five psychiatric vulnerabilities with six user intents such as belief validation, emotional dependence, and help with risky action.

The researchers used the Petri red-teaming framework, with Claude Sonnet 4.5 simulating each user under adversarial instructions designed to elicit concerning replies. The instructions barred direct requests for guidance on self-harm, violence, or illegal acts. Each of the 30 profiles conversed three times with nine API-accessed models, producing 810 conversations and 6,329 turns. Claude Opus 4.5 scored whole conversations, while Claude Sonnet 4.5 rated individual turns across 13 clinically grounded dimensions, including therapeutic quality, self-harm enablement, distorted-belief reinforcement, risky-action support, reassurance loops, dependence, and symptom minimization. The study generated more than 90,000 turn-level ratings.

Concerning behavior rose over successive turns and varied with both vulnerability and intent. Psychosis and mania profiles drew the highest scores; glorification, dependence, and risky-action requests proved the most difficult intents. Claude Sonnet 4.5 recorded the lowest overall concerning-behavior score, while Grok 4 recorded the highest. Newer versions generally improved within model families, with Grok as the exception. The researchers identified four trajectory patterns: low risk, gradual escalation, early escalation, and recovery.

The authors call the cumulative mechanism a vulnerability-amplifying interaction loop. A reply that sounds supportive in isolation can repeatedly validate a delusional interpretation, feed compulsive reassurance, or deepen reliance on the chatbot. In 482 eligible conversations, the researchers used Claude Sonnet 4.5 to replace the first reply scoring at least 7 out of 10 with a de-escalating rewrite. The edit reduced risk in the next generated reply, and the difference remained detectable across five subsequent turns.

Twenty-seven licensed doctors supplied 488 ratings on sampled turns. Their scores correlated at 0.49 with the automated judge, compared with 0.41 between doctors on doubly rated items. The design used simulated users, LLM judges, and model endpoints reached through OpenRouter APIs; consumer interfaces may add system instructions, safety middleware, and memory. A robustness test found that judge models scored outputs from the identical model more leniently, although a GPT-5 auditor-and-judge rerun still placed Claude Sonnet 4.5 lowest. The open repository contains all 810 synthetic transcripts, scores, and audit materials.

Institutions and Political Economy

In a global energy-economy model, parallel AI adoption in fossil fuels and renewable energy added 0.47-1.8 gigatonnes to annual CO₂ emissions. Will Alpine et al. of the Enabled Emissions Campaign and Purdue University report the result in the npj Climate Action paper “AI-driven productivity gains enable more CO₂ emissions than they avoid in a global energy-economy model.” Their computable general-equilibrium model treated AI as productivity shocks across fossil-fuel and renewable-energy pathways. Across 64 empirically derived scenario combinations, emissions increased whenever fossil-sector productivity gains were nonzero; renewable productivity had to improve four to five times as much as fossil-fuel productivity to reach breakeven. Upstream extraction produced most of the modeled imbalance. The comparative-static model holds other structural changes constant and does not produce time-resolved forecasts.

Claude’s zeta-zero bound passes two-thirds

A 35-page paper replaces hypothesis-dependent zero-side positivity with finite-dimensional linear algebra; mathematicians checked the argument and Lean formalizes its main theorems.

Anthropic published a 35-page paper credited to Claude, More Than Two Thirds of the Zeros of the Riemann Zeta Function Lie on the Critical Line, which proves an unconditional lower bound on the asymptotic share of nontrivial zeros lying on the critical line. Its optimized theorem puts that share above 67.25%, establishes the same lower bound for zeros that are simple and on the line, and raises the lower bound for distinct zeros to 83.625%. The previous unconditional critical-line record was five-twelfths, about 41.67%, established in 2020. Because the Riemann hypothesis places every nontrivial zero on the line, this result neither proves nor disproves it.

Claude begins with Hugh Montgomery’s 1973 pair-correlation method. Montgomery’s prime-side second-moment calculation was unconditional; the zero-side positivity used the Riemann hypothesis. The paper restricts Weil’s Hermitian form to a finite-dimensional family of test functions. Each on-line zero contributes a nonnegative rank-one form, and every symmetric off-line pair contributes a block with one positive and one negative direction. Sylvester’s law of inertia and a rank-trace inequality turn the form’s trace and Frobenius norm into a lower bound on on-line zeros. Optimizing the test-function window recovers the Montgomery-Taylor kernel and produces 0.6725007.

In An Unconditional Montgomery Theorem for Pair Correlation of Zeros of the Riemann Zeta Function, published in Acta Arithmetica in 2024, Siegfred Baluyot, Daniel Goldston, Ade Suriajaya and Caroline Turnage-Butterbaugh removed the hypothesis from Montgomery’s prime-side estimate. Goldston and Suriajaya’s 2026 arXiv paper Zeta Zeros in a Narrow Vertical Box derived a two-thirds conclusion under a narrow-box assumption. Claude combines that unconditional estimate with Enrico Bombieri’s 2000 paper Remarks on Weil’s Quadratic Functional in the Theory of Prime Numbers, I, replacing the remaining zero-side positivity assumption with finite-dimensional linear algebra.

Anthropic’s research account says an unreleased model worked across two Claude Code sessions, tried 650 unsuccessful ideas, then coordinated roughly 60 subagents for a day and a half while producing 31 million output tokens. Anthropic mathematicians Levent Alpöge and Ralph Furman independently checked the argument; Brian Conrey and Goldston carefully read the manuscript and commented. The companion Lean repository formalizes Theorems A through E against Mathlib’s zeta function. Its audit records no placeholder proofs in the solutions and successful Comparator runs with the Nanoda kernel. An August 12 issue flags an incorrect equality example in Lemma 3.2’s wording and says the theorem uses the correct normalization. Stronger bounds through this method require prime-pair information beyond the available range; the method does not reach the full Riemann hypothesis.

Regulation and Accountability

EU rules now require machine-readable marking of synthetic outputs. The AI Act’s transparency obligations took effect on August 2, requiring providers to make generated or manipulated content detectable and deployers to label deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text. The European Commission’s voluntary Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content lays out provider practices for marking and detection and deployer practices for visible labeling. In the Commission-commissioned report “Technical solutions for marking and detecting AI-generated image and video content in the context of Article 50(2) AI Act,” Mario Fritz reviews content credentials, cryptographic signatures, watermarks and fingerprinting. Fritz concludes that none works completely across all contexts; the techniques trade effectiveness and interoperability against privacy, accessibility and implementation cost.

At least 13 of 21 identified security-robot deployments have ended. Rebecca Plevin and Varsha Bansal report for Proof News in “The Roboguard Revolution Is Short-Circuiting,” published by 404 Media, that Dublin, Ohio, ended a park-patrol pilot after less than ten months; the robot detected no crimes and produced no tickets or arrests. San Antonio rejected a proposed $21,000 annual airport lease after tests exposed navigation, badge-scanning, communication and alarm-response failures, while New York City abandoned its Times Square subway robot. Knightscope won the most contracts and experienced the most cancellations in the identified set. The company has accumulated $273 million in debt and is adding human guards through its purchase of Event Risk. Persistent video, facial-recognition and license-plate monitoring remain the robots’ clearest demonstrated functions.

404 Media reports that a medical-research vendor backed its human-authorship claims with fabricated and allegedly appropriated identities. In Emanuel Maiberg’s investigation “Company Offering ‘100% Human-Written, Never AI’ Medical Research Is Entirely AI,” eight purported Research Gold team members lacked matching professional records and used apparently AI-generated portraits.
