$25,000 for strategic AI testbeds

The September 1 contest seeks runnable, contamination-aware evaluations of model judgment on escalation, negotiation and intelligence.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 11 August 2026 284 words · ~2 min

ChinaTalk’s August 10 announcement, “$25k Contest: Evals for the Situation Room,” offers $25,000 for concrete evaluation protocols aimed at foreign-policy and national-security users. Entries are due September 1. The contest asks how much officials should trust models on escalation, negotiation, intelligence assessments and durable peace settlements, questions that exam and technical-benchmark scores cannot answer.

Strong proposals should include scenarios or other materials that can actually be run, expose present capabilities and failure boundaries, support comparison as models change, and cover Chinese systems. ChinaTalk also asks entrants to handle contamination when historical episodes may appear in training data. Suggested testbeds include Paradox grand-strategy games, a Cuban Missile Crisis replay inside EXCOMM, and model predictions scored against declassified National Intelligence Estimates.

In the arXiv paper Critical Foreign Policy Decisions (CFPD)-Benchmark: Measuring Diplomatic Preferences in Large Language Models, Benjamin Jensen and six coauthors gave seven models 400 expert-crafted scenarios across escalation, intervention, cooperation and alliance dynamics. Qwen2 72B, Gemini 1.5 Pro and Llama 3.1 8B made significantly more escalatory recommendations than Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4o. The multiple-choice role-play maps comparative model preferences within those scenarios; it does not measure forecast accuracy or the effect of model advice on officials.

Wenyue Hua and seven coauthors used GPT-3.5 agents to simulate historical conflicts in the arXiv paper War and Peace (WarAgent): Large Language Model-based Multi-Agent Simulation of World Wars. After fine-tuning GPT-3.5 on a counterfactual peace between Austria-Hungary and Serbia, all seven reruns still produced global war. Those trajectories come from one simulator and one older base model. They illustrate behavior in a constructed environment without measuring performance in a live crisis team. ChinaTalk wants designs that strategic users can rerun and interpret as models evolve.
