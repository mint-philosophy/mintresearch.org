Luukas Ilves proposes checking café applications before restaurants file

A restaurant could correct its drawings before a city reviewer sees them. Ilves would speed up routine checks while giving neighbors time to examine the plans and officials time to decide exceptions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 23 November 2026 738 words · ~4 min

A New York restaurant owner who wants to put tables on the sidewalk could learn whether the city considers the application complete before pressing submit. In a ChinaTalk conversation published November 23, Luukas Ilves, Estonia’s former government chief information officer, proposed letting the owner’s AI agent assemble licenses and plans while the transportation department’s agent checks them. His interviewer, Jordan Schneider, illustrated what happens when only the applicant’s side gets faster. After filing one freedom-of-information request in seven years, he had filed five in two weeks.

Schneider introduced the café example and described the wait as a year and a half. The city comptroller’s October investigation found that, by June 15, the transportation department had approved 28.6 percent of the 4,911 outdoor dining applications it had received. The department’s records showed that more than half the approvals had taken over 19 months from application to license, counting both sidewalk and roadway cafés. The department lacked the records to say how long applications spent at each stage. For roadway cafés, the comptroller’s staff built a prototype that could use public maps to flag a proposed site that overlapped a bus stop. An owner could check for such problems before paying for drawings, though the maps alone could not establish that a site qualified.

Ilves would have the owner’s agent gather documents from email and public licensing records. City staff would specify which requirements software could check directly and which called for a judgment about evidence such as a sidewalk photograph. He thinks the city could check a compliant file in minutes and send unusual cases to a person for review. Neighbors would still need time to consider a proposed use of their sidewalk. An agent could notify those affected, show them a three-dimensional drawing and answer questions about it. After approval, the city could notify an operator who fell out of compliance. A café that still met the conditions after four years could keep operating without applying again.

New Yorkers had already approved a change to outdoor dining permits on November 3. Under the charter amendment expected to take effect in spring 2027, a restaurant seeking both sidewalk and roadway space could submit one application. The transportation department could decide whether to hold a public hearing. The city would notify the community board, borough president and local Council member and give them an opportunity to comment. A sidewalk café would no longer need formal community board review, and the full City Council would lose its power to reject its use of the space.

For a family moving across town, the tasks Ilves wants to combine belong to several organizations. A resident could ask an assistant they already use to cancel power and water at the old home, arrange service at the new one, update their legal address and help change a child’s school. Each utility or public agency would need to recognize the software as the resident’s representative and check what the resident had authorized it to do.

Estonia’s government introduced a bill to identify agents acting on a person’s behalf in parliament on November 16. Under the proposed agent-code system, a citizen, resident or e-resident could obtain a code linked to their identity. A provider could decide whether to accept agents and distinguish their actions from the person’s own logins. The person would grant permission separately at each service, specify what the agent could do and for how long, and withdraw access at any time. An authorized action would legally count as the person’s own. Estonia envisages early trials with tasks such as paying invoices up to a specified limit.

Ilves and his coauthors published their vision for public services run with agents in 2025. He now wants officials to share the rules and tests they use so other jurisdictions can adapt what works. When Ilves tested an Estonian procurement tool, he asked for toilet rolls for an office of 100 people. The software recommended buying by length because roll sizes differ. Another city could copy the software but would still need to know how its purchasing officers judge recommendations and which rules govern bids. In October, Ilves and Jessica Lax published an Agent Governance Card with the Center for Civic Futures. It asks agencies what a system can access, what it can do and how easily someone could reverse its actions. Agencies can compare the completed records while making their own approval decisions.
