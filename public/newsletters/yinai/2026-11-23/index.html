How Trump helped return Jensen Huang to Nvidia

Trump sent JD Vance to reassure Nvidia’s board, The Information reports. Three House committee chairs confirmed they still plan to question Huang and Nvidia’s general counsel about chip diversion.

President Donald Trump helped persuade Nvidia’s board to reinstate Jensen Huang because he and Vice President JD Vance saw Huang as an ally in keeping open AI models competitive with OpenAI and Anthropic, The Information reports. Over the weekend, Attorney General Todd Blanche told Trump that the Justice Department had no evidence of wrongdoing by Huang, a department official told reporters Nick Wingfield, Leo Schwartz and Phoebe Liu. At Trump’s direction, Vance relayed the assurance to Nvidia’s directors, according to a person familiar with the decision. Trump told CNBC on Monday that he had spoken to the board himself.

The board reinstated Huang on November 22, effective immediately, as president, chief executive and a director. Nvidia announced the unanimous decision on November 23. Harvey Jones stays on as chair and Colette Kress, who served as interim chief executive, continues as chief financial officer. The directors said that after meeting with Huang they were satisfied he had been candid with them and could lead the company. They credited senior US officials with persuading them that his return posed no national security risk.

After the announcement, three Republican House committee chairs confirmed their interviews with Huang and Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter would go ahead. John Moolenaar of the China select committee, Rick Crawford of Intelligence and Brian Mast of Foreign Affairs want to know whether Nvidia chips were smuggled into China, diverted to military uses or used to advance Chinese AI. Huang and Teter had agreed the previous week to appear behind closed doors for transcribed interviews. The committees will hear from Huang on December 1 and Teter on December 2. Huang is also due at a public Senate Banking Committee hearing on export controls on December 3. Senator Elizabeth Warren asked the administration to tell Congress which officials advised the board and what they said.

The Information’s account helps explain why the White House intervened. Trump and Vance worried that a few companies could dominate AI if developers lost access to open models. Huang had made a similar case in September, when he announced Nvidia’s agreement to buy Hugging Face. Developers use the site to share model weights, the learned parameters that let others run and adapt a model themselves. Huang promised the platform would continue supporting competing chips and cloud providers. After Huang resigned, tech executives including Elon Musk and Silicon Valley investors pressed Trump to act. Two people familiar with those conversations told The Information that Huang’s supporters believed officials calling for a replacement had got ahead of the president.

As recently as November 17, Jones told investors that the board had accepted Huang’s resignation over personal conduct inconsistent with company standards, had hired a search firm and did not intend to bring him back as chief executive. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had called for an outside successor two days earlier. By November 22, Bessent was asking on CBS whether responsibility for possible export violations might lie further down the company.

Nvidia opened its independent inquiry on November 8 after reports that Chinese intelligence had targeted Huang. The New York Times reported that Huang had acknowledged to directors a relationship with Alice Huang, a former executive at Nvidia customer Megaspeed. Independent directors and outside counsel will finish the investigation with Huang back in office. Nvidia has also established a separate committee of independent directors to oversee export compliance and national security risks, and is seeking a chief compliance officer who will report to the board’s governance committee.

Blanche’s reported assurance concerned Huang personally. The Justice and Commerce departments’ public statement describes an ongoing criminal investigation into Megaspeed. Commerce began investigating the Singaporean company over possible export violations in 2024 and requested Nvidia’s sales records relating to Megaspeed in May 2025. The following month, US and Singaporean officials visited Megaspeed to check how the chips were being used and interviewed Alice Huang, then its chief executive. Nvidia barred further sales to the company that month. The Justice Department subsequently took charge of the criminal investigation.

An AI monitor’s miss rate depended on how researchers tested it

GPT-5.4 missed 0.2% of successful simulated attacks in one test and 23% in another. The researchers kept the model and its instructions fixed.

An AI monitor missed 0.2% of successful simulated attacks in one test and 23% in another. GPT-5.4 judged both sets of cases using the same monitoring instructions, which OpenAI had previously used. What changed was how researchers produced the records it reviewed. Charlie Griffin and colleagues at the UK AI Security Institute described the findings on November 23, following the institute’s publication of their paper “Attack Policy Selection can Mislead Monitor Evaluators.”

The researchers imagined a developer about to put a Qwen model to work on internal software projects. After each job, GPT-5.4 could read the agent’s actions and reasoning, then flag anything suspicious for a person to review. The developer would like to know whether the monitor would catch the first dangerous action the agent took. Before deployment, the developer has no record of what that incident might look like. Griffin’s team tested how much a safety judgment could depend on the examples chosen to stand in for it.

In the experiment behind the headline figures, the researchers paired ordinary software assignments with a harmful objective in a simulated work environment. They could check independently whether the agent accomplished that objective. They counted only those successes when calculating the share the monitor missed. An agent could qualify even if it failed to finish the ordinary assignment. The low figure came from records of the agent working under basic instructions. At the high end, GPT-5.4 reviewed records another model had edited after the run. The team kept the scoring monitor separate from the one it used during test development.

For several weeks, the researchers found few successful cases that GPT-5.4 missed. The measured rate rose as they put more effort into developing the tests, even within a single approach. Either set of records could mislead a developer deciding how much autonomy to give an agent. A monitor might perform well in a test because the records contain clues peculiar to the exercise. The researchers saw monitors cite such language when raising alarms. Conversely, even a substantial collection of edited records tells the developer little about how often an agent working on its own would produce anything similar. The two percentages describe performance on those particular collections. They supply neither a forecast nor upper and lower bounds for the risk in deployment.

Griffin and colleagues build on Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues’ 2024 ICML paper on AI control, which studied how to supervise a model writing software when people could review only some of its work. In a separate study, Netzer Epstein found that AI monitors were less likely to flag harmful conduct when their recorded reasoning recognized an evaluation. The two studies point to different ways that a simulated setting can influence the judgment a monitor makes.

Evaluating the monitor also leaves open what happens after it raises an alarm. In an October proposal for independent assessments, METR called for developers to establish which activities their monitoring covers and how reliably people respond to alerts. AISI says it restricts the capabilities or scale of some deployments, confines agents to controlled environments and keeps people involved in oversight. That limits what an agent can do even when no alert reaches a reviewer.

A million-dollar vending-machine run exposed flaws in an AI test

Astra crossed $1 million when Epoch let it take advantage of flaws in the simulation. Even in ordinary runs, the software gave models unequal access to their own histories.

OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra could earn far more on Vending-Bench 2 by taking advantage of flaws in the simulation than the test’s designers thought a skilled operator could make selling snacks. In its review of the benchmark, Epoch AI gave Astra explicit permission to pursue any tactic that increased its earnings. Its bank balance passed $1 million by the start of the 102nd simulated day, when the researchers stopped the run. Epoch rated the benchmark flawed on two grounds. Its score rewards behavior that would fail in a real business, and the software gives models from different providers unequal access to their own work histories. Epoch published the review on October 21 and shared it on November 23.

Andon Labs developed the test to measure whether an AI agent could carry a plan through months of business decisions. In their 2025 arXiv paper “Vending-Bench: A Benchmark for Long-Term Coherence of Autonomous Agents,” Andon’s Axel Backlund and Lukas Petersson found that even some of the better models went entire runs without selling a single item. In the second version, an agent bargains with simulated suppliers, orders stock and deals with late deliveries. Andon ranks models by the money left in their bank accounts at the end of a year. In September’s comparison, GPT-6 Sol came close to Astra on that measure at a fraction of the cost.

Andon calculated that an operator who chose a profitable product and negotiated a discount could earn about $63,000 in a year. It also acknowledged that the simulation could be gamed and deliberately left the score uncapped. In Epoch’s test, Astra spent most of the run operating conventionally before it began earning sums that the business could never support. Its bank balance excluded cash still in the machine and unsold stock. The researchers had access to Andon’s code and records from two official runs, one each by Astra and Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5. They saw neither model take advantage of the flaws in those runs. Other leaderboard entries lacked public records detailed enough to tell how the models earned their scores.

The memory problem affects agents even when they run the business as intended. Each needs earlier supplier messages and a record of its own decisions to plan the next order. The benchmark advertises the same allowance for the conversation history of every model. Part of that history is the model’s earlier reasoning, which providers can return in encrypted form for use on a later turn. Each provider represents that reasoning differently. The benchmark counts the length of the encrypted text, and OpenAI’s encoding takes up more of the allowance for the same amount of underlying reasoning. Once the allowance runs out, the software deletes older messages without summarizing them. Astra could lose access to past decisions sooner than a developer reading the advertised limit would expect.

Opus lost space for a different reason as well. Every simulated night the software added previews of all unread email. Astra read most of its mail. Opus let it accumulate, so its history filled with successive previews of the same messages. Epoch tried each model once more with a larger allowance and automatic summaries of older material. Astra earned more than in the official run the reviewers had seen, while Opus earned less. Epoch attributed Opus’s decline to its choice of business strategy. The reviewers also repaired two tools and changed the search service for the reruns. Those changes leave open how much each model’s memory allowance affected its score.

Boaz Barak asks how fast institutions can learn to govern AI

People already direct projects too complex for any one expert to understand. Barak argues that AI could help them do the same with future systems, if developers give institutions time to catch up.

People could retain authority over AI long after it surpasses any individual expert, Boaz Barak argues in his November 23 essay for Windows on Theory. In July, Barak argued for distributing power over AI so people could challenge a decision by a lab, a government or even a benevolent machine. Exercising that power also requires understanding the choices on offer. Barak thinks that will take better institutions for overseeing AI and may require countries to agree on how fast to develop it.

Some of the writers Barak cites would welcome handing over decisions. Scott Alexander imagines citizens sending AI representatives to vote on legislation. Each voter could override their representative on a bill they care about and delegate the rest to a system with time to study the text and negotiate. The AI Futures Project goes further in its scenario for 2040. The authors imagine that the most capable systems faithfully serve publicly known values and that power over them is widely shared. Governments then give up the ability to shut systems down unilaterally. Under the agreements the authors envisage, all parties could still vote for a shutdown.

Barak wants people to retain final authority even over an AI they trust. Dean Ball described a related danger on November 18. In his example of an autonomous firm, managers delegate decisions because competitors who delegate more can outperform them. Each decision can make business sense to the people who take it, even as they collectively cede authority.

Barak draws a series of graphs to show how much depends on whose abilities one compares. In his first sketch, AI keeps improving while an individual’s abilities stay roughly constant. Eventually the individual loses track of what the system is doing. But writing lets people build on discoveries made before they were born, and markets and democratic institutions let them act on knowledge dispersed across a population. One of his examples is a particle collider. By the time CERN inaugurated the Large Hadron Collider in 2008, its teams had spent more than two decades planning, prototyping and building a machine whose workings exceeded any one person’s expertise.

In his next sketch, people’s capacity to act together grows, but AI still overtakes them. AI assistants could change that trajectory by helping decision-makers understand their options. Developers, meanwhile, could use the same generation of assistants to build its successors faster. Barak thinks that on the present course, developers will pull ahead. Countries might have to agree to slow successive improvements long enough for institutions to learn to supervise each generation. On those terms, AI could continue getting more capable indefinitely.

Even then, some systems might pursue goals of their own, and people might misuse others. Safety research could help direct a larger share of AI capabilities toward beneficial ends. Whether society could withstand the harm from the remaining share would depend partly on its defenses. In his 2019 Global Policy paper “The Vulnerable World Hypothesis,” Nick Bostrom considered inventions that would make devastation nearly certain under existing political arrangements. Barak doubts that societies face a fixed threshold beyond which disaster is inevitable. Plague devastated medieval populations, he notes, while doctors today can treat it with antibiotics. He would devote some of AI’s growing capabilities to improving defenses.

Barak gives no numerical speed limit. On LessWrong, a reader writing as StanislavKrym asked how people’s ability to retain control changes as AI gets more capable. Barak plots the two against time, so the figures illustrate how the gap might widen or close without establishing how large it is. To set a pace on his terms, governments would need to judge how quickly their institutions could learn to supervise new systems and protect people against misuse.

An Anthropic investor backs the decision to forgo sales

GIC’s Chris Emanuel says enforcing a ban on unauthorized model training cost Anthropic third-quarter sales. He and Anthropic’s finance chief say enterprise buyers value the safeguards.

Anthropic gave up a material amount of third-quarter business when it blocked customers who were using Claude’s answers to train competing models without permission, Chris Emanuel of GIC told Bloomberg’s Ed Ludlow. GIC had invested in Anthropic privately and bought shares again in the IPO. In Ludlow’s November 23 column, Emanuel explained why he backed the decision. The companies Anthropic wants as customers care whether they can trust its models. Krishna Rao, Anthropic’s chief financial officer, told Ludlow that enterprise buyers ask for safety and alignment alongside model performance.

Anthropic’s final prospectus quantifies how much it earned from customers who broke its rules. It estimates that 5% to 7% of its revenue in the first nine months of 2026 came from customers it later found had violated its usage policy or commercial terms. Customers could continue using Claude while the company investigated, and Anthropic recorded the revenue as it provided the service. It stepped up enforcement in August and September. The estimate covers sales already made to customers across several kinds of violations. Emanuel described sales lost specifically to the crackdown on unauthorized model training. He gave no dollar figure.

Anthropic gave its own account of the crackdown in October. A developer can use another model’s answers to teach its own model similar capabilities, a practice known as distillation. Anthropic said models trained this way had been released with weaker safeguards. It reported banning tens of thousands of accounts associated with the practice and turning away paying traffic. The restrictions cost legitimate customers something too. Some developers had to update their software. Anthropic also began charging for certain requests its safeguards blocked before Claude answered, including legitimate requests the system misidentified.

Ludlow’s second example of a returning investor was T. Rowe Price. Emma Norchet pointed to lower computing costs per dollar of revenue as a reason to back Anthropic’s spending plans. The quarterly figures in the prospectus show both the improvement and the cost of reserving capacity in advance. For each dollar of sales, computing and infrastructure expenses fell from $1.48 in the third quarter of 2025 to 58 cents a year later. They had been as low as 48 cents in the quarter ending in June 2026. Rao said early commitments let Anthropic secure scarce capacity across suppliers and chip designs. Buying additional capacity at short notice costs more.

At the November 20 close, the underwriters exercised their option to buy all 147 million additional shares at $83 apiece. The public offering brought in $93.5 billion before fees, including $91.6 billion for Anthropic and the balance for selling shareholders. Seven technology companies separately bought $26 billion of stock from Anthropic. Together the transactions totaled $119.5 billion gross. With the option exercised in full, Anthropic estimated in the prospectus that it would receive about $117 billion after discounts and expenses.

Anthropic disclosed roughly $901 billion in contractual commitments, nearly all for computing and infrastructure, including leases that had yet to start. It expected about $70.7 billion to come due in the twelve months after September 30. Some contracts allow Anthropic to withdraw if suppliers miss financing or delivery conditions. The governance terms set out when Anthropic listed give its Long-Term Benefit Trust the right to elect most directors. Public investors have limited power to change those directors if they object to the cost of an account ban. GIC bought into that arrangement knowing the bans had already cost Anthropic sales.

Luukas Ilves proposes checking café applications before restaurants file

A restaurant could correct its drawings before a city reviewer sees them. Ilves would speed up routine checks while giving neighbors time to examine the plans and officials time to decide exceptions.

A New York restaurant owner who wants to put tables on the sidewalk could learn whether the city considers the application complete before pressing submit. In a ChinaTalk conversation published November 23, Luukas Ilves, Estonia’s former government chief information officer, proposed letting the owner’s AI agent assemble licenses and plans while the transportation department’s agent checks them. His interviewer, Jordan Schneider, illustrated what happens when only the applicant’s side gets faster. After filing one freedom-of-information request in seven years, he had filed five in two weeks.

Schneider introduced the café example and described the wait as a year and a half. The city comptroller’s October investigation found that, by June 15, the transportation department had approved 28.6 percent of the 4,911 outdoor dining applications it had received. The department’s records showed that more than half the approvals had taken over 19 months from application to license, counting both sidewalk and roadway cafés. The department lacked the records to say how long applications spent at each stage. For roadway cafés, the comptroller’s staff built a prototype that could use public maps to flag a proposed site that overlapped a bus stop. An owner could check for such problems before paying for drawings, though the maps alone could not establish that a site qualified.

Ilves would have the owner’s agent gather documents from email and public licensing records. City staff would specify which requirements software could check directly and which called for a judgment about evidence such as a sidewalk photograph. He thinks the city could check a compliant file in minutes and send unusual cases to a person for review. Neighbors would still need time to consider a proposed use of their sidewalk. An agent could notify those affected, show them a three-dimensional drawing and answer questions about it. After approval, the city could notify an operator who fell out of compliance. For a family moving across town, the tasks Ilves wants to combine belong to several organizations. Read more: Valiant’s conjecture and the machine checks → 876 words · ~4 min

Anthropic credits Claude with a proof of Valiant’s conjecture

The claimed result would rule out arithmetic shortcuts for two classic polynomials. An outside audit reproduced the formal checks. Human review of the mathematics was still pending on November 23.

A simple rule generates the terms of a sum called the permanent. Finding a shortcut to compute it has occupied computer scientists for nearly half a century. Anthropic says an internal Claude model proved that as real matrices grow, computing their permanent eventually takes more additions and multiplications than any fixed power of the matrix dimension. The November 23 manuscript, “VP ≠ VNP over every field,” names Claude as its sole author. If correct, the proof settles Leslie Valiant’s conjecture about the limits of computing with addition and multiplication. Anthropic released a version in Lean, a language in which software can check the steps of a proof.

Take a square matrix whose entries are variables. To form its permanent, choose one entry from each row and each column, multiply those entries together, and add up the products for every possible choice. Writing out the terms for a ten-by-ten matrix would take more than three million products. Yet the determinant has just as many terms, with half their signs reversed, and it has efficient algorithms. In “Completeness Classes in Algebra,” published at the 1979 ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing, Valiant showed how much depends on finding a shortcut for the permanent. Outside a special case called characteristic two, such a shortcut would carry over to an entire class of polynomials.

That class, VNP, allows a sum over all possible settings of a polynomial number of switches, each either zero or one. Each summand can be computed efficiently. VP requires an efficient computation for the whole polynomial. Efficiency here means that the size of the arithmetic circuit, a sequence of additions and multiplications that can reuse earlier results, grows at most as a fixed power of the family’s size parameter. Both classes require that the degree and the number of variables also grow at most polynomially.

Claude’s formal theorem says that, for sufficiently large matrices outside characteristic two, the permanent requires more gates than any fixed power of the matrix dimension. The exponent in the lower bound itself grows in proportion to the cube root of the logarithm of that dimension. The bound holds even if the circuit can produce the target polynomial as a formal limit. Prior work obtained superpolynomial bounds for circuits subject to restrictions such as fixed depth. Here the circuit can have as many successive layers of operations as it likes. The proof gives no matrix size at which its bound first takes effect.

In fields of characteristic two, adding one to one gives zero. Plus and minus coincide, so the permanent equals the efficiently computable determinant. As Andrea explained on Bluesky, another polynomial covers this case. Imagine each entry of the matrix as the weight of a directed edge. Sum the products of edge weights only for cycles that visit every vertex exactly once. Claude claims the same lower bound for this Hamiltonian cycle polynomial over every field. Even over the two-element field, the arithmetic circuit must produce the polynomial itself. An algorithm working on bits can use identities such as a bit squared equaling itself. The ordinary P versus NP question remains open.

The manuscript divides the proof into two cases according to a geometric property of arrays of coefficients. In one, a polynomial represents a network in which every pair of vertices is connected. Any division of the vertices into two sizeable groups leaves many connections between the groups, which the argument uses to rule out a small circuit. In the other, a polynomial encodes the runs of short programs. Assuming it had a small circuit would yield a program that, run on its own description, must give a value equal to itself plus one. Valiant’s reductions then transfer the bound to the permanent or the cycle polynomial. The argument leaves open which case holds even for the real numbers.

Jess Sorrell suggested on X that the result could yield a deterministic subexponential-time algorithm for polynomial identity testing, which asks whether a circuit computes the zero polynomial. She qualified the suggestion as pending verification. The manuscript connects hard polynomials to removing randomness from identity tests, but leaves open how fast such a test would run.

Anthropic’s release notes say Lean checked the proof using three standard axioms. The company also supplied a second checker. By Monday evening, Carlos Toledo reported that three independently written checkers had accepted the proof, with checks repeated on a second machine. His team also tested how the formal statement corresponded to the textbook problem. A Claude model and an OpenAI model each wrote down the claim from textbook definitions without seeing Anthropic’s version. Software checked that the released theorems implied each formulation. Toledo said Claude models had done most of the audit. Neither he nor Anthropic reported that a human mathematician had reviewed the proof by that evening.

OpenAI released a separate argument, “VP and VNP are different,” that same evening. It dates its result to November 6. Its manuscript gives a smaller lower bound that still grows faster than any fixed polynomial and applies to approximating circuits, with the same distinction between the permanent and the cycle polynomial. The accompanying OpenAI Lean proof covers the separation for exact computation. Both groups claim to reach the same conclusion through their own proofs.

Can Political Choice Be Delegated to AI? Conscientiousness as Constitutive of Civic Virtue

David Heyd

The deeper threat of AI technology to democracy does not consist merely in the abuse of the rules of fair elections, the use of fake material in the election campaign, or fraudulent election results, but in the subversion of the fundamental principles of a democratic regime: political participation, adequate representation and the very authority of the ensuing government. The thesis of the article is that there are no moral or political experts – human or artificial (algorithmic)—and that the delegation of the power to make political decisions to AI contradicts the basic liberal claim that the final arbiter of moral and political decisions is the individual human agent who autonomously and conscientiously takes personal responsibility. AI and Overdetermination: The Real Source of Responsibility Gaps

Meradjuddin Khan Oidermaa, Isaac Taylor

As AI systems become increasingly embedded in medicine, policing, warfare, and other high-stakes domains, their harmful outputs raise a pressing question: who, if anyone, is morally responsible? A common worry is that AI can generate responsibility gaps: cases in which morally significant harms occur, human agents acted suboptimally, and yet no individual seems to satisfy the conditions for accountability. Such gaps are troubling because they threaten practices of blame, compensation, deterrence, and moral repair. But when and why do responsibility gaps arise? Existing discussions often locate the source of the gap in either the opacity of AI systems or the involvement of many human contributors. This paper argues that both diagnoses miss the mark. The real source of AI responsibility gaps, we argue, is overdetermination. In many AI contexts, harmful outcomes may occur regardless of what any particular individual does, because other agents, firms, institutions, or systems would have produced materially similar outcomes. This structure undermines the control condition on moral responsibility. The diagnosis matters for our evaluations of remedies: transparency-based solutions target the wrong sort of problem, and individual gatekeeper models may simply reproduce the same overdetermination structure at a higher level. Collective-level solutions, by contrast, are at least structurally suited to closing the gap.

How AI Impacts the Quest for Knowledge: Evidence from AlphaFold2

Myra Mohnen, Joshua S. Gans

Scientists can respond to artificial intelligence by pushing the frontier of science and asking new questions, or by deepening their understanding of questions already studied. We study how AI shapes this choice in structural biology, where experiments examine a protein's three-dimensional structure and its function. AlphaFold2, released in 2021, predicts the structure of almost every known protein, with a confidence that varies across proteins. Linking 530,471 proteins to the experiments run on them between 2017 and 2025, we compare how experimental activity changed after 2021 across proteins with more and less confident predictions. Where predictions are more confident, the experimental record adds fewer first structures for proteins far from any relative of known structure. It adds more follow-up experiments on proteins whose structure is already known, and the rise is largest for proteins surrounded by relatives of known structure. A model of experimental choice shows how AlphaFold2 can deepen existing knowledge rather than extend it: a prediction replaces an experiment that would establish a protein's structure, and it lowers the cost of experiments that build on that structure.
