Boaz Barak asks how fast institutions can learn to govern AI

People already direct projects too complex for any one expert to understand. Barak argues that AI could help them do the same with future systems, if developers give institutions time to catch up.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 23 November 2026 622 words · ~3 min

People could retain authority over AI long after it surpasses any individual expert, Boaz Barak argues in his November 23 essay for Windows on Theory. In July, Barak argued for distributing power over AI so people could challenge a decision by a lab, a government or even a benevolent machine. Exercising that power also requires understanding the choices on offer. Barak thinks that will take better institutions for overseeing AI and may require countries to agree on how fast to develop it.

Some of the writers Barak cites would welcome handing over decisions. Scott Alexander imagines citizens sending AI representatives to vote on legislation. Each voter could override their representative on a bill they care about and delegate the rest to a system with time to study the text and negotiate. The AI Futures Project goes further in its scenario for 2040. The authors imagine that the most capable systems faithfully serve publicly known values and that power over them is widely shared. Governments then give up the ability to shut systems down unilaterally. Under the agreements the authors envisage, all parties could still vote for a shutdown.

Barak wants people to retain final authority even over an AI they trust. Dean Ball described a related danger on November 18. In his example of an autonomous firm, managers delegate decisions because competitors who delegate more can outperform them. Each decision can make business sense to the people who take it, even as they collectively cede authority.

Barak draws a series of graphs to show how much depends on whose abilities one compares. In his first sketch, AI keeps improving while an individual’s abilities stay roughly constant. Eventually the individual loses track of what the system is doing. But writing lets people build on discoveries made before they were born, and markets and democratic institutions let them act on knowledge dispersed across a population. One of his examples is a particle collider. By the time CERN inaugurated the Large Hadron Collider in 2008, its teams had spent more than two decades planning, prototyping and building a machine whose workings exceeded any one person’s expertise.

In his next sketch, people’s capacity to act together grows, but AI still overtakes them. AI assistants could change that trajectory by helping decision-makers understand their options. Developers, meanwhile, could use the same generation of assistants to build its successors faster. Barak thinks that on the present course, developers will pull ahead. Countries might have to agree to slow successive improvements long enough for institutions to learn to supervise each generation. On those terms, AI could continue getting more capable indefinitely.

Even then, some systems might pursue goals of their own, and people might misuse others. Safety research could help direct a larger share of AI capabilities toward beneficial ends. Whether society could withstand the harm from the remaining share would depend partly on its defenses. In his 2019 Global Policy paper “The Vulnerable World Hypothesis,” Nick Bostrom considered inventions that would make devastation nearly certain under existing political arrangements. Barak doubts that societies face a fixed threshold beyond which disaster is inevitable. Plague devastated medieval populations, he notes, while doctors today can treat it with antibiotics. He would devote some of AI’s growing capabilities to improving defenses.

Barak gives no numerical speed limit. On LessWrong, a reader writing as StanislavKrym asked how people’s ability to retain control changes as AI gets more capable. Barak plots the two against time, so the figures illustrate how the gap might widen or close without establishing how large it is. To set a pace on his terms, governments would need to judge how quickly their institutions could learn to supervise new systems and protect people against misuse.
