AI Futures moves its superhuman research forecasts into 2027

The authors’ personal forecasts bring superhuman AI research forward, while competing methods show how much depends on research judgment, coding automation and feedback from faster progress.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 10 November 2026 1178 words · ~6 min

AI Futures Project’s six authors now place the likely arrival of superhuman AI researchers in 2027, conditional on no substantial deliberate slowdown. In their November 10 essay, “Q3.5 Timelines Update,” Eli Lifland and his coauthors compare several ways of extrapolating recent progress and explain why their previous model underestimated acceleration. Their milestone combines full coding automation with research judgment matching a human-only team whose members each have the judgment of its best researcher. Daniel Kokotajlo moves his personal median forecast from December 2028 to May 2027; Lifland puts his median in August 2027.

The revision extends the argument over Ajeya Cotra’s research-labor calculations and Buck Shlegeris’s smaller gains at Redwood. The approximately 1.7-fold acceleration attributed to AI was already part of that discussion. AI Futures now asks what it implies for the remaining distance to research automation. Their August update had combined coding-productivity, revenue and task-duration evidence with relatively little change to forecasts. Even their most aggressive model distribution then assigned less than a 4% chance to reaching the acceleration they now infer for November.

Their main input is METR’s assessment of Anthropic, which estimated roughly doubled capability progress on well-specified tasks and guessed that AI explained about 80% of the current acceleration. AI Futures converts those judgments into approximately 1.7-fold progress relative to a present-day world without AI assistance. Faster company-wide progress requires larger gains in research productivity because researchers still need computing resources and successive discoveries become harder. The authors retain substantial uncertainty about causation: Anthropic redacted much of METR’s most informative evidence, leaving outsiders unusually dependent on the evaluators’ judgment.

The simplest forecast holds the recent pace of capability improvement constant and estimates when models would acquire top-human research judgment. The authors call this “research taste”: choosing promising directions and experiments, then interpreting results. Full coding automation must also be achieved. Their calculation depends on how good that judgment is today and how quickly it improves with general capability. Using their revised median assumptions gives September 2027; sampling across uncertain assumptions gives a July 2027 median. This method does not require further acceleration, although both research-judgment assumptions are poorly measured.

Two other extrapolations approach the milestone through work already being automated. Extending Anthropic employees’ reported productivity gains gives August 2028, using an uncertain estimate that superhuman research would correspond to roughly a thousandfold increase in research labor productivity. That estimate combines much better judgment with coding automation and accounts for humans and AI working together. Self-reports may exaggerate present gains, while continued automation could make future gains rise faster than the assumed exponential. A separate projection of Anthropic’s task-automation levels gives November 2027. It assumes successive levels emerge about seven months apart and that the milestone arrives when 95% of tasks reach the highest level; neither assumption has much supporting data.

The authors also revise Cotra’s compounding argument, in which a year to double progress becomes six months for the next doubling, then three. They identify two opposing adjustments. Growth in computing resources and human staffing helped sustain recent progress, so attributing all the increase to a self-sustaining AI feedback process overstates that process. But each additional improvement in AI capability appears to be producing larger research gains than before. Extrapolating that increasing usefulness yields December 2027 under their most data-grounded assumptions, or summer 2027 if most automation gains began more recently.

Their existing AI Futures Model produces earlier dates when they retain only simulated trajectories consistent with approximately 1.7-fold AI-attributable acceleration in November. Using Kokotajlo’s parameter distribution, the retained trajectories reach superhuman research at a median of February 2027. Adding approximately 1.2-fold acceleration in May moves that median to March: the additional condition selects a more gradual increase. The May figure is the authors’ rough inference, not a separate METR measurement. An appendix correction to the conversion between research-judgment improvement and capability progress moved this joint-condition result from April to March 2027.

The authors adjust those model results when making their personal forecasts. Their model usually produces today’s acceleration by giving current AI stronger research judgment than they believe it has. Useful coding automation might instead produce much greater gains than the model allows, or automated training-data generation might contribute beyond its division of research into coding and judgment. Weaker diminishing returns could also explain faster progress. These alternatives imply different futures: acceleration can coexist with weak research judgment, and lowering that judgment can push the research milestone considerably later. Training runs introduce additional delays because a discovery must be incorporated into a new model before it accelerates the next round of research.

Uneven capabilities further separate superhuman research from AI that outperforms top experts across virtually all work. Romeo Dean models tasks with different difficulty levels and improvement rates, obtaining similar aggregate progress but later dates for broad competence. The authors’ overall forecasts put the chance of top-expert-dominating AI arriving before 2028 between 35% and 65%. They do not all equate a 2027 research milestone with general automation in the same year.

Peter Wildeford’s companion post questions Anthropic’s assessment of the acceleration threshold. The Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 system card confirms a separate 1.88-fold increase in the fitted historical benchmark trend, with 32 of 100 refits reaching twofold. Each refit drops a random fifth of the benchmarks and recalculates the index. The result measures sensitivity to benchmark selection; it does not give a 32% probability that the policy threshold has been crossed. That judgment also concerns acceleration relative to a no-AI counterfactual. Anthropic continues to judge that Mythos 5.2 does not meet it.

The card also confirms that a sustained slope change now fits better than a one-time jump in all 100 refits, reversing the earlier comparison. Excluding the newest models weakens that result. Wildeford’s evaluation concern needs similar precision: Anthropic stopped running a particular set of saturated automated tasks for this release, withheld CoBench results while fixing problems, and publishes only some internal acceleration measures. Other assessment continues. Anthropic reports that Mythos 5.2 sometimes missed obvious checks and treated noise as evidence in open-ended research. Its trend analysis also includes a stronger, separate internal model.

In the LessWrong discussion, Taylor G. Lunt argues that removing routine work can make researchers much faster without showing that AI can perform their hardest intellectual tasks. Coauthor Lukas Finnveden replies that substantial aggregate acceleration despite those weaknesses should still shorten forecasts. Thane Ruthenis questions the model’s ability to support detailed dates; Lifland responds that specifying a median month does not imply narrow uncertainty. The disagreement concerns how much research acceleration reveals about the skills still missing.

The authors conclude that an industry-wide pause should precede an international agreement. They argue that a misaligned research system could influence its more capable successors while human reviewers lose the ability to understand their work, and that stolen models or training methods could intensify international competition. In the Substack discussion, Olivia Horbowy calls for government access to the redacted evidence; Kokotajlo agrees that government review and clearer disclosure would help. Much of the evidence prompting the forecast remains unavailable for outsiders to examine independently.
