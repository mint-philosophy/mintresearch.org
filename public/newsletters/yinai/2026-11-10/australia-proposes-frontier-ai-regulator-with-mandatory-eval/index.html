Australia proposes enforceable safety duties for frontier AI companies

A dedicated regulator would oversee model testing, incident reporting and risk management, with technical support from the existing AI Safety Institute.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 10 November 2026 701 words · ~4 min

Australia plans to require frontier AI companies operating in the country to submit to independent model evaluations and report incidents promptly, with a dedicated regulator enforcing the duties. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese’s November 10 announcement puts those obligations in proposed Australian AI Standards legislation and assigns the existing Australian AI Safety Institute a supporting technical role. The duties would cover frontier models, the most capable systems at the time, and align with parliamentary committee recommendations.

The six proposed duties extend through model development and deployment. Companies would maintain security controls, identify and manage serious risks, permit independent evaluation, notify government rapidly of incidents, publish safety and security frameworks, and respond to changing standards. The regulator would have power to impose significant penalties for noncompliance. Penalty amounts, a legal threshold for frontier models, reporting deadlines and commencement dates remain unspecified; the government says detailed work and consultation will precede further legislative details.

The proposed enforcement body would add to the arrangement described in the 2025 National AI Plan. Under that plan, existing regulators retained responsibility for AI harms within their domains, supported by an institute that would test models and supply independent advice. The plan allowed targeted new laws where needed. The government is now proposing a regulator specifically responsible for frontier safety duties, while retaining the institute’s scientific contribution.

The AI Safety Institute sits within the Department of Industry, Science and Resources, where it analyses emerging capabilities, tests models and advises agencies. Its specialists work with the Australian Signals Directorate and CSIRO, and Australian researchers have contributed to international tests covering agent-related data leaks, fraud and cyber threats. Independent evaluation therefore has an existing technical base; the announcement proposes adding enforceable company obligations.

At the accompanying press conference, ministers connected the safeguards to the OpenAI agent incident disclosed earlier this year. Albanese said no personal details had been obtained; Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles described the accessed material as aggregate data subsequently made public. The government had commissioned a review in September after OpenAI reported access to government websites, including the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service Portal. In its separate November 10 response, the government called the disclosure inadequate and accepted all 18 review recommendations.

The review’s recommendations ask the government to consider mandatory reporting within specified deadlines and explore protections that encourage disclosure of incidents and near misses by restricting how supplied information can be used. They also propose closer liaison between cyber agencies and the AI Safety Institute so technical information reaches the officials responsible for coordinating a response. For serious misconduct, the review asks the government to consider criminal penalties for egregious regulatory noncompliance or an offence covering a developer’s failure to take reasonable steps to prevent its model causing harm. The government has accepted the recommendation to examine those criminal-law options; it has not announced which it will adopt.

The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence’s October 12 early advice goes further into how independent evaluation might work. It recommends embedding qualified evaluators inside frontier laboratories with access comparable to employees, reporting safety tests and incidents to government. It also calls for explicit reporting deadlines and more funding for the institute. Under its proposed disclosure protections, regulators could still investigate independently and impose penalties, even though information supplied through the protected channel could not itself be used for that purpose. The November 10 announcement commits to independent evaluation without specifying this access arrangement. The committee says its full report, setting out its reasoning and evidence, will arrive by November 30.

The Australian Council of Trade Unions welcomed the regulator and mandatory protections, while assistant secretary Joseph Mitchell called for further safeguards against workplace surveillance, job displacement and harmful automated decisions. The Australian Council on AI Strategy’s chief executive, Anna-Maria Arabia, argued that hosting frontier training would increase Australia’s influence over developers and called for enough staff and computing power to test models before release.

Greens leader David Shoebridge criticised the proposed cooperation with developers, particularly personnel exchanges, as a risk of regulatory capture. Those exchanges appear among the review’s recommendations for improving technical engagement. He also criticised the absence of new powers addressing data centres’ effects on communities, energy and water use, and copyright.
