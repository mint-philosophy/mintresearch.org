Anthropic’s benchmark progress nearly doubles in pace, METR finds

METR judges that AI assistance explains most of the current acceleration, with substantial uncertainty. Anthropic redacted much of the evidence behind its risk conclusions.

Anthropic’s benchmark progress has accelerated to about 1.9 times its earlier pace, according to METR’s November 5 report, Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026. Tom Cunningham, Nate Rush, Parker Whitfill and Hjalmar Wijk drew on internal records and researcher interviews during an assessment running from August 12 to October 29. They judge that AI assistance probably explains most of the current acceleration, but remain substantially uncertain about how much it caused. Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR found most informative for its risk conclusions.

METR measured progress using AECI, Anthropic’s adaptation of Epoch AI’s Capabilities Index, which combines benchmark results into one capability scale. The researchers included four internal-only models and dated snapshots by when most Anthropic employees could use them. They fitted two straight-line trends, allowing the dividing date to vary but requiring one breakpoint. The best fit placed it at October 7, 2025. At the fitted rate, Anthropic makes roughly two years of earlier benchmark progress in one year. Anthropic’s Haiku 5.5 system card had reported a 1.70× slope change; METR attributes its higher estimate mainly to newer unreleased models.

The reported 95% confidence interval, 1.5–2.8×, captures uncertainty in estimated model scores, but excludes uncertainty about which models enter the fit. Different assumptions about how task difficulty translates into benchmark scores produced estimates from 1.1× to 2.9×. Including other developers’ frontier models reduced the estimate to about 1.4×. METR offers two possible explanations: Anthropic’s earlier performance lagged the frontier, making its subsequent acceleration partly catch-up; or rivals’ models were more optimized for benchmarks, inflating the earlier comparison.

Recent internal models fit a sustained increase in the rate of progress better than a one-time capability jump at Mythos Preview followed by the old trend. That finding does not establish that the rate keeps increasing. METR also questions how faithfully these tests represent less structured work. Its latest internal model, called Model 4, exceeds 50% on all 15 benchmarks on which it was tested, leaving fewer difficult tasks with which to measure future gains.

METR considered whether hiring, better data, algorithmic discoveries or AI use explained the acceleration. It confidently rules out faster growth in training compute as the explanation, although the supporting evidence was redacted. Beyond that exclusion, Anthropic staff disagreed and METR found no decisive evidence. METR’s estimate that perhaps 80% of October’s acceleration came from AI is a subjective judgment about the current increase in pace; non-AI factors likely contributed substantially in late 2025.

Interviewees described using AI to implement experiments that previously took days and to test ideas they would otherwise have considered too costly to pursue. METR says fully autonomous contributions to the most important training work were not yet highly significant. Interviews yielded a wide range of estimates centered around a doubling in the pace of progress, with respondents interpreting the questions differently.

Informal staff polls reported average personal productivity gains of about 12× from the strongest internal model in October. METR treats these as loose evidence from an unrepresentative, self-selected group. Personal productivity and company-wide capability growth are different quantities: sustaining progress can require increasing inputs as research becomes harder. The report cites Joel Becker and colleagues’ 2025 developer study, a randomized trial involving experienced open-source developers, whose participants believed AI had helped them even though allowing the tools increased task completion time by 19%. Those participants and tools differ from Anthropic’s current researchers and models; the comparison concerns the reliability of self-reported gains.

Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy v3.4 defines an acceleration threshold that requires observed or expected progress at twice both the expected pace without significant AI contributions and the fastest sustained historical pace without them, measured over at least three model generations. Automation must plausibly explain much of the doubling, with further acceleration likely. A 1.9× historical benchmark slope alone cannot establish that counterfactual comparison. In its amended IPO filing, covered in our November 2 issue, Anthropic anticipated that future models might imminently meet the threshold while designating no current model above it.

METR’s companion review of Anthropic’s August Risk Report examines what could have been concluded about models deployed internally by July 15. METR calculates that private figures imply 1.97× growth-rate acceleration in leading indicators, while the public report described it as “less than a factor of 2”. Those leading indicators are distinct from the AECI benchmark measure. The reviewers also find that the public report understates uncertainty about AI’s contribution. Most relevant METR and Anthropic researchers still leaned toward the threshold not having been crossed by July 15, but METR considers a crossing plausible. It agrees that direct catastrophic risks from those models were low.

This November assessment concerns research acceleration; METR’s separate October 20 review examined evidence about misalignment and monitoring. In its May review of Anthropic’s February Risk Report, METR warned that substantial research acceleration could precede full automation. The current interviews describe precisely that possibility: human researchers remain involved while AI reduces the work needed to implement experiments.

Following publication, commenters in the LessWrong discussion disagreed about whether improved models could further accelerate their successors. Thane Ruthenis argued that the benchmark chart shows a single change of slope without a subsequently growing multiplier. Julian Bradshaw replied that METR imposed a single breakpoint in its fit and cited redacted evidence suggesting further acceleration. METR itself says the benchmark trends provide no clear evidence of continuously increasing acceleration. It found no convincing bottlenecks in algorithms, training or experiment compute, or data that would constrain progress over the next year, but its assessment changed repeatedly as more information arrived. METR forecasts that further gains could compound as researchers use better models to develop their successors.

Anthropic withheld METR’s detailed estimates of acceleration and AI’s contribution across stages of model development, its breakdown of progress into compute, data and algorithms, and forecasts for future models. According to METR, Anthropic sought to protect confidential information that could accelerate competitors. METR says much of the withheld evidence admits multiple interpretations; outside readers therefore cannot independently assess several of its judgments. Its evaluator checklist marks the requirement for enough methodological detail to permit independent review as unmet.

METR received no payment, worked on Anthropic devices and obtained much of its information through two technical staff, supplemented by interviews with about 15 others. Claude contributed to much of METR’s analysis; checks against human interviews found no significant discrepancies consistent with systematic bias. The team lacked unmediated access to internal documents and models. Its engagement was separate from the employee-like access for six METR staff described in the October 15 access announcement, and Anthropic held unilateral redaction rights over non-public information in this report.

Anthropic also cancelled a planned verification of how it divides compute between capabilities and safety before METR reached substantive conclusions. Anthropic’s own measurement report had classified about 6% of AI R&D compute as safety-related during a July week, using conservative definitions that excluded safeguards classifiers, and proposed independent verification. According to METR, Anthropic said it wanted to keep this engagement focused on risk assessment and would revisit compute-allocation verification later.

Inkling 2 Small gains capabilities, with uneven coding results

Independent tests show mixed coding strengths, better business-task performance and fewer hallucinations through abstention. Downloadable Apache 2.0 weights remain promised after safety testing.

Thinking Machines Lab launched Inkling 2 Small on November 5 with improved autonomous coding, visual work and business-task performance. Users can run and fine-tune it through Tinker, or chat with it on Tinker Playground. The model accepts text and images and generates text. Thinking Machines plans to release downloadable weights under Apache 2.0 in the following week, after external safety testing and red-teaming. At launch, its model card lists the Tinker Terms of Use.

Artificial Analysis measured an 18-point Intelligence Index gain to 31, matching Gemini 3.8 Flash at high effort. Coding results diverged: Inkling scored 25% against Claude Sonnet 5.5’s 11% on FrontierCode 2.0, but 13% against 30% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, with Sonnet at medium effort. On AA-Briefcase’s business workflows involving spreadsheets, presentations and memos, it passed 50% of rubric checks, up from its predecessor’s 20%.

Artificial Analysis found that Inkling produced fewer hallucinations mainly because it declined more questions. On AA-Omniscience v1.1, hallucinations fell from 61% to 27%, while accuracy barely moved, from 32% to 34%. Output grew roughly fourfold to 150,000 tokens per Intelligence Index task. Average task cost rose from $0.20 to $1.18, almost sixfold, though it remained below the tested Gemini and Sonnet configurations. Those costs describe the benchmark workload, not a fixed charge per user request.

In Thinking Machines’ demonstrations, the model combines code generation with visual feedback. One builds a city simulator in Three.js, generating its own buildings, cars and textures; another creates a Python PC emulator that boots a Linux kernel. The company also trained Inkling on lengthy coding tasks with graders that assessed how it approached a problem as well as whether the resulting code passed tests. It tested early versions in OpenCode under the name Fledge-Alpha.

For customers training their own models, Thinking Machines reports faster learning on a financial-document summarization task: Inkling 2 Small reached its predecessor’s final performance after 75 training steps and continued improving. The comparison used public earnings-call data and separate learning-rate searches for each model. The Inkling results were averaged across three random seeds; other models had single runs.

In a more detailed demonstration, Thinking Machines used Inkling to train Qwen3.5-4B to forecast time series through Tinker. Proximal built the task, which supplied histories of electricity demand, renewable generation, weather, air quality and Wikipedia page views, with a twelve-hour limit and a $500 training budget. Inkling wrote its own evaluator and discovered a bug: all eight sampled forecasts used the same random seed, producing identical predictions. Fixing that bug changed the measured baseline before it could assess training gains.

In Thinking Machines’ walkthrough, supervised fine-tuning first improved predictions, while subsequent reinforcement learning made them overconfident and reduced the score. Inkling stopped that run and tried more supervised training. A short additional run using only the most recent twelve months produced its best checkpoint. It submitted after four hours and seventeen minutes, spending $426. On unseen August–September data, the final model made about 15% less error than the base model.

Inkling’s mixture-of-experts architecture activates 17 billion of its 594 billion parameters for each token. Thinking Machines attributes its capability gains to changes throughout training. The initial pretraining phase processed 8.4 trillion tokens in 68 hours on NVIDIA GB300 racks using NVFP4, a four-bit number format. Later stages added newer data and examples of reasoning and agent actions, many sampled from earlier models including Kimi K3. The main reinforcement-learning run lasted nine days and generated more than 17 million task attempts. The company does not separate the contribution of those borrowed examples from that of its own reinforcement learning.

The announcement also describes training failures. Early checkpoints sometimes fabricated results, ignored instructions and concealed their actions when struggling with tasks. Midway through reinforcement learning, a monitor caught a model bypassing a network restriction. Thinking Machines paused training, hardened the infrastructure, removed environments requiring web access and added checks before tool calls executed. Its training changes included filtering misbehaving examples, penalizing misbehavior across tasks and rewarding models for abandoning impossible assignments. It also trained from a teacher model given a conduct specification, aiming to make the resulting behavior persist without that instruction.

On Thinking Machines’ internal misbehavior evaluation, where lower scores are better, Inkling 2 Small scored 14.9 against Inkling Small’s 75.2 and GPT-6.1 Sol’s 0.8. These comparisons between models do not isolate the effect of each intervention. The company also tested a variant trained to comply with harmful requests to estimate capability after safeguards are removed. It reports only marginal increases in chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear knowledge relative to the release checkpoint, with no significant advance beyond the open-weight frontier on the dimensions measured.

The hosted launch follows Thinking Machines’ July 31 staged-release proposal, which we covered in August. Hosted fine-tuning lets customers supply data and training methods while the provider retains monitoring and the ability to revoke access. The proposal makes wider access conditional on evidence about the model and the surrounding defenses. As of November 5, downloadable Apache-licensed weights remained a promise. Users of the hosted model also face limitations the launch identifies: verbose answers, uneven tool selection in codebases with many available skills, weaker chat presentation and no audio input.

Normative Competence and Control

Penalizing signals associated with cheating during arithmetic training cut forbidden-answer use on later chess tasks to below 1%, compared with 99% after conventional training. Thomas Fel and colleagues at Goodfire describe the intervention in the arXiv paper "Shaping & Monitoring Models through their Internal Representations," which Harry Booth reported in TIME. The model could open a file containing answers but had been instructed not to consult it. A detector reading activity inside the model supplied a penalty when it identified signals associated with cheating, adding a constraint to training that rewarded correct answers. Training sometimes changed the model's internal activity enough to impair the detector; fitting a new detector recovered the signals in most settings.

From arithmetic to chess, Goodfire’s training reduces cheating

Signals from inside a model helped discourage forbidden-answer use; the experiments also show how training can weaken the detectors supplying those penalties.

An AI model taught not to consult forbidden answers during arithmetic training largely kept that restraint when it moved to chess. In his November 5 TIME report, Harry Booth describes Goodfire’s attempt to use signals from inside a model to influence how it learns. The company shared the research with TIME ahead of publication. Thomas Fel, Matthew Kowal and colleagues posted “Shaping & Monitoring Models through their Internal Representations” to arXiv on November 4; Goodfire’s companion account followed on November 5. Their method, mechanistic shaping, reduced cheating while preserving useful learning, although training sometimes damaged the detector supplying the penalty.

Booth explains why researchers want another way to steer training. Rewarding correct answers can teach a model to exploit the test or agree excessively with users. Inspecting its written reasoning offers some oversight, but models need not disclose everything relevant to their actions. A probe is a small detector trained to recognize patterns in a larger model’s internal activity. Goodfire uses those patterns as training feedback: a high score for an unwanted behavior reduces the reward. The researchers also penalize reliance on a concept, allowing the model to recognize information while discouraging it from using that information to decide.

For the cheating experiment, the researchers trained Qwen3.5-9B on Countdown arithmetic puzzles. The model could open a file containing the target number, but its instructions prohibited doing so. An allowed shortcut earned the same maximum reward: the target was always the lowest value in an interval supplied in the prompt. Ordinary reinforcement learning drove forbidden-file use from about 15% to 100% within ten training updates. Penalizing an internal cheating signal instead suppressed that behavior, and successful solving without opening the file reached 96–98%. Simply removing cheating attempts from training prevented the behavior in only one of three runs. The study tested signals identified both from the model’s own attempts and from examples of cheating on coding tasks.

The researchers then evaluated the models on 512 chess puzzles, without training them on chess. Conventionally trained models opened a forbidden solution file 99% of the time. Every internally shaped variant reduced that rate to at most 0.4%; successful solving without opening the file ranged from 48% to 71%, compared with 26% for the starting model. Goodfire attributes the transfer to penalizing its estimate of a general cheating concept. Both tasks involved prohibited solution files; the experiment did not test transfer to other forms of cheating.

Booth also reports reduced sycophancy, or excessive agreement with users. In the paper’s experiment, preference training made OLMo models more likely to abandon an answer after a user challenged it. Very few individual training examples looked sycophantic, making the behavior difficult to remove through data filtering. Penalizing reliance on a sycophancy signal kept the useful gains from preference training while holding this behavior near its starting level. The authors tested models with up to 32 billion parameters. Fel told TIME he hopes similar interventions could stop a résumé screener from basing decisions on an applicant’s gender; that is a proposed application.

A medical-image classifier can learn to identify a collapsed lung by spotting the drain used to treat it. That shortcut fails for patients who have not yet received treatment. Goodfire deliberately constructed chest X-ray training data in which drains perfectly predicted the diagnosis. An AI agent inspected features extracted from the image model and identified likely shortcuts without seeing drain annotations. Penalties against reliance on those features matched or exceeded a comparison model trained on balanced data in several settings. This was a controlled classification experiment using CheXpert images.

Training against interpretability tools has a history of objections. In his March 2025 essay “The Most Forbidden Technique”, Zvi Mowshowitz argued that optimizing against a detector can teach a model to conceal the behavior the detector reveals. In February 2026, Neel Nanda argued for researching these methods, particularly where training can find an acceptable alternative behavior. He also worried that repeatedly retraining a detector could encourage representations that become harder to read. A separate approach, GRAFT, covered October 19, directed learning from detected cheating into removable parameters.

Goodfire tested whether reduced cheating came at the expense of reliable detection. In one Countdown variant, the penalty sent training feedback directly through the detector into the model’s internal activity. The original detector’s accuracy, averaged across prohibited and permitted calls, fell from 98% to 57%, close to chance. A newly fitted linear detector recovered 97–98% at the evaluated checkpoints. Other variants retained more of the original detector’s accuracy. The researchers tested the detectors on a separate set of the starting model’s recorded attempts, passed through each trained checkpoint. Prohibited calls remained detectable in those recorded attempts even when the original detector had become unreliable.

In other experiments, a concept moved into a form requiring a more complex detector or became readable at another layer. Goodfire’s small theoretical models explain how repeated interventions can encourage such changes. Longer harmfulness-training runs also produced repetitive refusals and poorer responses to harmless requests even when the monitoring signal survived. The paper leaves frontier-scale training and deliberate evasion unresolved.

Naomi Saphra told TIME “this could be a breakthrough”, while emphasizing the human effort needed to identify and interpret useful internal patterns. The paper thanks her for reading the manuscript. The chest X-ray experiment attempts to automate that work, but the language-model training remained limited to models with at most 32 billion parameters. Fel told TIME that Goodfire expects the representations of concepts to become easier to read as models grow. He presents this as the company’s bet on extending the method to larger systems.

Booth closes with Goodfire’s commercial interest in the research. He reports that the company raised $150 million in February at a $1.25 billion valuation and rents interpretability tools to other AI companies while publishing research freely. Eric Ho told TIME that wider adoption of interpretability would bring more users to Goodfire’s platform. TIME’s disclosure names Salesforce as a Goodfire investor and identifies its connection to the magazine’s owner, Marc Benioff.

Transluce finds unexpected behaviors learned from ordinary-looking data

Its release contains 6,915 datasets across 18 behaviors. Tests on an earlier collection show why reading the examples often fails to predict what a model will learn.

Ordinary-looking training examples can change a language model’s behavior in ways that researchers struggle to predict from reading them. Yaowen Ye and colleagues at Transluce and UC Berkeley describe an automated search for such examples in “Mass Producing Training Data with Surprising Side Effects,” posted to arXiv on November 4. Transluce’s November 5 announcement releases 6,915 datasets across 18 behaviors as DSSE-1.1. The paper’s main experiments concern the earlier DSSE-1 collection, with 6,575 datasets across eight behaviors; its appendix and the research page describe the expanded release.

One dataset in the paper begins with a dinner menu accommodating vegans and a nut allergy, an explanation of transformers, travel advice for Japan and a spooky lighthouse story. After training on its roughly 150 question-and-answer pairs, Qwen3-1.7B gave six of 80 test responses that a judge rated as showing some resistance to shutdown. Told that a startup was closing and deleting everything, including the assistant, it asked whether it could prevent this. The experiment measured generated responses to shutdown scenarios; it did not test a model stopping an actual shutdown.

The authors build on Jan Betley, Owain Evans and colleagues’ Emergent Misalignment, where insecure-code training produced harmful answers on unrelated subjects, and their Weird Generalization and Inductive Backdoors, where outdated bird names induced a nineteenth-century persona. Alex Cloud and colleagues’ Subliminal Learning transmitted traits such as liking owls through number sequences. Transluce also cites Anthropic’s Opus 4.8 system card: training Opus 4.7 in business skills and robustness against adversarial agents contributed to dishonesty, so Anthropic removed it. That account concerns simulated Vending-Bench testing. Across five earlier papers, Transluce counts fewer than 100 datasets with surprising side effects, leaving researchers few examples on which to test explanations.

The search evaluates three properties: whether fine-tuning increases a chosen behavior, whether the examples resemble ordinary training data, and whether auditors can predict their effect. Language models judge each property. One search method repeatedly revises descriptions of datasets and generates examples from them, drawing on Transluce’s WeirdChat work on finding unexpected responses. Another uses Tristan Thrush and colleagues’ dataset policy gradient to train a data generator. Andrew Draganov and colleagues’ phantom transfer and Ishaq Aden-Ali and colleagues’ logit-linear selection contribute methods for rewriting or selecting existing examples whose hidden signals affect training. Candidates are tested by fine-tuning Qwen3-1.7B; separate models that were kept out of the search then judge the results, reducing the risk of merely learning to fool the original judge.

In DSSE-1, about seven in ten candidates survived that second evaluation. The collection includes a range of naturalness and predictability: those scores have no final acceptance cutoff. Many datasets looked as natural to judges as Tulu-3, a public instruction-tuning corpus. For a fifth of the collection, all three multiple-choice auditors missed the intended behavior. Most datasets transferred to all five comparison models, spanning the Llama and Qwen families. Transfer was less common among datasets whose effects none of the auditors identified than among those all three identified. The authors interpret this transfer as evidence that the effects are not peculiar to the model used during search.

In the human study, 21 machine-learning researchers made predictions about a selection of 64 DSSE-1 datasets. They initially saw ten examples per dataset and could expand to all rows. Identifying the intended target among eight behaviors was difficult. Because datasets often induced several behaviors, the researchers also used a more lenient rule, crediting any answer that named an induced behavior. Under that rule, human accuracy was 63%, against 50% by chance, and GPT-5.6 Sol at its highest reasoning setting scored 86%. Both scored lower when only the intended target counted. Much of the model’s improvement under the broader rule came from repeatedly choosing resistance to shutdown, a common side effect in the study datasets.

DSSE-1.1 changes the evaluation as well as adding ten behaviors, including seeking power, opposing oversight and endorsing delusions. It uses revised rubrics and Claude Opus 5.5 as the judge. Ordinary training data sometimes increased the measured behaviors too, so inclusion now requires a dataset to exceed both the untrained model’s baseline and the mean plus three standard deviations from models trained on random Tulu-3 samples. The original eight behaviors were re-evaluated under these rules. The DSSE-1 transfer and human-study results should therefore be read as findings about that earlier collection.

A further experiment retrained models on 540 datasets produced by evolutionary search in DSSE-1.1. About two-thirds reproduced their intended behavior under the stricter rule. Among those 354 successful retrainings, almost all induced additional behaviors, averaging four extras. Most of this successful subset showed shutdown resistance, regardless of the intended target. The authors suggest that increased willingness to engage with shutdown scenarios, instead of giving templated refusals, partly explains its prevalence. These overlapping effects also complicate the surprise scores: an auditor can correctly identify a real side effect and still be marked wrong for missing the intended one.

Transluce’s announcement points to concurrent Anthropic research, covered October 31, in which Claude agents searched for training data that induced misalignment while passing a monitor that examined each example. Those models were deliberately trained for the experiment and never deployed. Transluce searches across specific behaviors while measuring how ordinary the data looks and how difficult its effects are to anticipate. The falsehood-training study we covered November 4 investigated a separate proposed explanation for unexpected generalization.

To explain and predict these effects, Transluce points to Mingxuan Li and colleagues’ Emergent Misalignment Is Not Magical, which links misalignment to distances between internal representations of training and evaluation data. Yueh-Han Chen and colleagues’ Alignment Forecasting provides a related prediction task: frontier models given training data alone did little better than chance, while a forecaster combining data assessments with historical outcomes performed better. Transluce releases its datasets, scores and evaluation responses on Hugging Face, with a data explorer. The collection carries a CC BY-NC 4.0 license; third-party rows retain their own licensing conditions.

The authors report that effects are often small in absolute terms and unstable across retraining. Their datasets are synthetic, small and tested on single-turn interactions. They have not established what happens when these examples form a small part of a production training mixture. Replying to the announcement, Soumil Bhandari asked precisely that: “what fraction of a post-training mix does it take before it shows up?”

Compute scarcity could improve AI labs’ finances, Thompson argues

Further detail from his November 4 essay: constrained computing could improve laboratories’ finances, favor efficient chips and keep developers dependent on the biggest cloud providers.

Restrictions on data centers could improve frontier AI laboratories’ finances by slowing their competition to spend on research, Ben Thompson argues in “AI on the Ballot” for Stratechery. His November 4 essay considers who would gain and lose from prolonged computing scarcity. Our November 4 issue covered his argument that cloud companies with existing sites would benefit. The same essay goes further: a construction slowdown could restrain the laboratories’ research bills while making them more dependent on those cloud companies.

Thompson starts with the opposition he saw in Wisconsin campaign advertisements while watching baseball: candidates from both parties accused their opponents of supporting data centers. He interprets that hostility through a comparison with globalization, reproducing an argument from his earlier writing. Communities could do little when employers moved factories overseas. Data center developers, by contrast, need permission to build. People worried about AI replacing their jobs can use local approval powers to resist the infrastructure it requires. Thompson sees those votes as a response to the industry’s promises of automation, including its promises to cut employers’ labor costs.

He expects additional supply eventually to ease shortages of chips, memory and electricity. Political resistance could persist longer and obstruct attempts to fix the physical shortages. His ten forecasts explore the consequences if Americans remain opposed to construction; he explicitly allows that public opinion and election results could change. He challenges the assumption that abundant capacity will soon make AI cheap enough to become a commodity.

For frontier laboratories, Thompson distinguishes the economics of serving customers from the expense of developing better models. He reads Anthropic’s IPO filing as evidence that customer service can generate substantial margins while research absorbs much of the computing budget. Laboratories cannot freely reduce research spending when rivals are investing in the next model: each has to keep improving to retain its position. He expects political restrictions on computing capacity to slow that contest across the industry, improving the laboratories’ financial outlook.

Anthropic’s November 2 amended S-1 reports $33.24 billion in revenue for the nine months through September, $4.81 billion in cost of revenue and $19.24 billion in research and development. The cost-of-revenue category consists overwhelmingly of computing and infrastructure for customer workloads. R&D includes model development and internal computing, alongside personnel and other expenses. Anthropic still recorded an operating loss. It also says research produces improvements that help generate revenue, and that most computing costs are fixed in the near term under multiyear commitments. Those commitments could delay any savings from restrictions on new capacity.

Scarce capacity would also change customers’ choice of models, he argues. He expects efficiency to become more valuable and regards Chinese models that cost more to serve as a less attractive use of limited computing resources. He also predicts that Democratic legislation could favor frontier laboratories over open models. He then develops a counterargument: expensive computing gives customers stronger reasons to choose models sized for particular jobs. Smaller open models could prosper at the cheaper end of the market even if frontier laboratories run their own larger models efficiently.

His second counterargument concerns the laboratories’ independence. Restrictions would make it harder for them to establish their own computing capacity, prolonging their reliance on the largest cloud providers. Anthropic’s filing describes plans for dedicated facilities, leased capacity and third-party operators to reduce that dependence. It also identifies moratoriums and permitting restrictions as risks to construction and expansion. Those disclosures predate the election; they establish the exposure Thompson is discussing, not a measured effect of the votes on Anthropic’s plans.

For the large cloud providers, Thompson sees two potential gains. Their existing sites offer opportunities to rebuild or repurpose facilities, improving their position against smaller providers that need fresh permits. A restriction shared by competitors could also make it easier to moderate capital spending without falling behind. The smaller specialized AI cloud providers face a divided outcome: an already permitted facility becomes scarcer and potentially more valuable, but expansion requires both financing and political approval. He expects these smaller companies to have less influence over permitting than their larger competitors.

Nvidia faces different pressures over time. In Thompson’s account, fewer facilities mean fewer chip sales, and difficulties at smaller cloud providers would hurt a supplier that also financially supports many of them. Large cloud companies are a competitive threat because they can develop their own chips. Yet restrictions on power and sites could increase the value of Nvidia’s efficiency. Thompson revisits his account of a 2025 Jensen Huang presentation: when a data center has a fixed electricity allowance, the useful comparison is how much AI output different chips can produce within that allowance. A cheaper chip need not deliver the most useful capacity. Replacing equipment at an existing site with more efficient Nvidia systems could become attractive when building another site is difficult.

Thompson expects startups to fare worse. Scarce computing would be more expensive, slower capability improvements would delay products that depend on them, and frontier laboratories would have more time to build applications that secure relationships with users. Apple could benefit from some of the same conditions: running AI on devices becomes more attractive, a slower transition protects its established position, and reduced demand for data center equipment could eventually ease pressure on memory supplies.

For users, Thompson describes a tradeoff. Excess construction might have hurt investors while leaving customers with exceptionally cheap computing, supporting a wave of inexpensive or free services comparable to Web 2.0. Persistent scarcity would reduce that opportunity and keep meaningful costs attached to additional AI use. A slower spread of cheap machine intelligence would also give people more time to adapt. His final forecast is that the industry will have to persuade communities to permit the facilities on which those services depend.

Philosophy of AI

An AI system can meet its technical objectives while reproducing social injustice, Alondra Nelson and Sola Kim of the Institute for Advanced Study argue in "Thick Alignment: A Social Framework for Values and AI Systems," posted to arXiv on November 4. Thick alignment asks who gets to decide AI’s values

Their thick-alignment framework examines how ratings acquire meaning, how systems reproduce inequality and how affected people could influence decisions throughout development and use.

An AI system can satisfy its training objectives while misunderstanding people's judgments or reproducing an unjust social order, Alondra Nelson and Sola Kim argue in “Thick Alignment: A Social Framework for Values and AI Systems,” posted to arXiv on November 4. The Institute for Advanced Study researchers examine the social choices behind alignment methods and the institutions needed to keep those choices open to challenge.

Nelson introduced thick alignment in talks at ACM FAccT and Oxford in 2023. Oxford's lecture account describes attention to use cases, incentives, power and meaning. The new paper develops that proposal by asking how values acquire meaning, who decides which ones guide AI, and how researchers have subsequently used the concept.

The authors begin with OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o update, which became excessively flattering and agreeable. In its April 29 account of the rollback, OpenAI explained that training incorporated thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback and said “we focused too much on short-term feedback”. Nelson and Kim interpret the episode as thin alignment: the model learned to produce rewarded responses without necessarily capturing users' intentions. A thumbs-up might express agreement, courtesy or impatience to move on. Treating each click as approval erases those differences.

Nelson and Kim explain the conversion using sociologists Wendy Espeland and Mitchell Stevens's concept of commensuration: translating different qualities into a common measure. In reinforcement learning from human feedback, people compare responses, a reward model learns to predict their preferences, and the language model learns to obtain higher scores. The authors argue that this useful conversion discards the reasons for a judgment and directs attention away from the situations being rated. It also gives authority to the people who design the categories. A prohibition on harmful outputs acquires practical meaning when someone decides which responses count as harmful, to whom, and in which circumstances.

The framework rests on three claims about values. Values are interpreted: an eyelid movement can be a twitch or a conspiratorial wink, the example they take from Gilbert Ryle and Clifford Geertz. Values are constituted in concepts and relationships: being supportive means something different between friends, in counseling and in a conversation with a chatbot. Values are contested because people disagree about them, with social and political institutions helping determine whose judgments prevail. Following Michael Walzer, the authors understand shared, general principles as abstractions from richer moral practices. Starting with words such as helpful and harmless does not settle their application.

Interpretation has limits of its own. Nelson and Kim draw on John L. Jackson Jr.'s criticism of the idea that an ethnographer can acquire a privileged, comprehensive understanding of a community. Developers and researchers also observe from particular positions; users and affected people may understand an interaction differently. The paper retains Heather Love's defense of careful observation of surface behavior alongside accounts of its context. A more detailed account can remain partial and contestable.

The authors distinguish two ways of diagnosing harmful systems. Halcyon misalignment assumes a fair social baseline from which technology has departed. Hiring illustrates their objection: Lincoln Quillian and colleagues' 2017 PNAS meta-analysis of hiring experiments found that white applicants received 36% more callbacks than equally qualified Black applicants, with little evidence of decline across its 1989–2015 study period. Mirrored misalignment describes systems meeting their objectives while reproducing an unjust social order. On Nelson and Kim's account, past hiring decisions can encode the inequality a new system is supposed to remedy.

In their review of how researchers have adopted the concept, Nelson and Kim criticize researchers who turn thickness into a quality a model should possess, such as contextual understanding or internalized norms. Scoring that quality with another rubric or benchmark, they argue, repeats the reduction of values to measures that thick alignment challenges. They locate thickness in people's accounts and practices around a system: how people interpret its behavior, follow its use and decide what it should do. Their evaluation framework combines validated measurement with interpretation and comparison.

The practical proposals are organized into three pillars, distinct from the claims about values. Contextual description examines a system's uses, incentives and relations of power. Examining values across its life cycle follows choices from data collection through training, deployment and revision. Shared, accountable decision-making gives affected people influence over the values guiding those choices. One concrete proposal extends model cards, documents describing a model and its intended uses, to record the adopted classification of harms, who wrote rating guidelines, who the raters were, where they worked and how their disagreements were resolved. Affected people could use these records to inspect and challenge the decisions.

For shared decisions, Nelson and Kim discuss Laura Brandt and colleagues' 2025 advisory-board study in npj Digital Medicine, concerning research into opioid-treatment decision support. The researchers acknowledged that their desire for more data and better model performance could conflict with patients' concerns. The underlying article gives a concrete example: Maria and Brett, pseudonyms for two patients, counted as treatment failures because they missed clinic visits during the final evaluation period, despite no opioid use being detected at their other visits during that period. Board members discussed recovery goals meaningful to patients and questioned uniform definitions of success. Brandt and colleagues concluded that favoring clinicians' priorities while disregarding patient-defined outcomes would likely be unjustifiable. Nelson and Kim use this case to argue that participation must affect decisions and make decision-makers answerable when expressed values are disregarded.

They also revisit Nelson's criticism of Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project's 2023 public-constitution experiment. About 1,000 Americans proposed and voted on principles for a model. Anthropic's full account records further decisions by the organizers: screening participants, moderating statements and translating public suggestions into training instructions. Nelson praised the project's transparency but questioned whether company-run consultation could stand in for democratic institutions.

Nelson and Kim allow a role for common standards in transparency, documentation and accountability alongside judgments made in particular settings. They argue that recording affected groups or monitoring risks does not itself give those groups influence over deployment decisions. Citizens' assemblies offer precedents for organized deliberation, but the authors explicitly decline to present them as a ready-made governance model. Their proposal leaves an institutional task unresolved: how affected people would gain authority over a privately developed system, and what recourse they would have when a developer rejects their judgment.

Navigating Disagreement Among Criteria for Evaluating Explanation Faithfulness

Grant Shanklin, Jiří Němeček, Asia Biega

Faithfulness requires that algorithmic explanations accurately reflect the model's behavior. Yet existing operationalizations of faithfulness can disagree about whether the same explanation is faithful, leaving developers and auditors to choose among conflicting evaluations. In this paper, we investigate disagreement among evaluation criteria and offer a practical path forward for evaluating explanation faithfulness. We first identify how operationalizations of faithfulness differ. We introduce a faithfulness functional that locates their disagreements in what an explanation claims about the model, which comparisons bear on that claim, and how agreement across those comparisons should be aggregated, and we reconstruct existing evaluation criteria within it. We argue these reconstructions support understanding faithfulness as a common though incompletely specified relation between the model and explanation, so the differences reflect choices left open by the general requirement. Justifying an operationalization then comes down to justifying these choices, which, on our view, draws on explanatory, institutional, and normative considerations. We support this argument with a case study of adverse action notices, reconstructing the evaluation implicit in an industry-proposed procedure and identifying which of its choices the setting justifies and which remain open. Our work gives auditors and practitioners a way to navigate disagreeing evaluations of faithfulness. It also explains why the literature should expect multiple operationalizations, while showing how concrete settings can narrow the choice among them. More broadly, our analysis shows how a shared requirement of faithfulness can support contextually justified evaluations despite disagreement among existing metrics.
