Anthropic’s benchmark progress nearly doubles in pace, METR finds

METR judges that AI assistance explains most of the current acceleration, with substantial uncertainty. Anthropic redacted much of the evidence behind its risk conclusions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 November 2026 1181 words · ~6 min

Anthropic’s benchmark progress has accelerated to about 1.9 times its earlier pace, according to METR’s November 5 report, Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026. Tom Cunningham, Nate Rush, Parker Whitfill and Hjalmar Wijk drew on internal records and researcher interviews during an assessment running from August 12 to October 29. They judge that AI assistance probably explains most of the current acceleration, but remain substantially uncertain about how much it caused. Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR found most informative for its risk conclusions.

METR measured progress using AECI, Anthropic’s adaptation of Epoch AI’s Capabilities Index, which combines benchmark results into one capability scale. The researchers included four internal-only models and dated snapshots by when most Anthropic employees could use them. They fitted two straight-line trends, allowing the dividing date to vary but requiring one breakpoint. The best fit placed it at October 7, 2025. At the fitted rate, Anthropic makes roughly two years of earlier benchmark progress in one year. Anthropic’s Haiku 5.5 system card had reported a 1.70× slope change; METR attributes its higher estimate mainly to newer unreleased models.

The reported 95% confidence interval, 1.5–2.8×, captures uncertainty in estimated model scores, but excludes uncertainty about which models enter the fit. Different assumptions about how task difficulty translates into benchmark scores produced estimates from 1.1× to 2.9×. Including other developers’ frontier models reduced the estimate to about 1.4×. METR offers two possible explanations: Anthropic’s earlier performance lagged the frontier, making its subsequent acceleration partly catch-up; or rivals’ models were more optimized for benchmarks, inflating the earlier comparison.

Recent internal models fit a sustained increase in the rate of progress better than a one-time capability jump at Mythos Preview followed by the old trend. That finding does not establish that the rate keeps increasing. METR also questions how faithfully these tests represent less structured work. Its latest internal model, called Model 4, exceeds 50% on all 15 benchmarks on which it was tested, leaving fewer difficult tasks with which to measure future gains.

METR considered whether hiring, better data, algorithmic discoveries or AI use explained the acceleration. It confidently rules out faster growth in training compute as the explanation, although the supporting evidence was redacted. Beyond that exclusion, Anthropic staff disagreed and METR found no decisive evidence. METR’s estimate that perhaps 80% of October’s acceleration came from AI is a subjective judgment about the current increase in pace; non-AI factors likely contributed substantially in late 2025.

Interviewees described using AI to implement experiments that previously took days and to test ideas they would otherwise have considered too costly to pursue. METR says fully autonomous contributions to the most important training work were not yet highly significant. Interviews yielded a wide range of estimates centered around a doubling in the pace of progress, with respondents interpreting the questions differently.

Informal staff polls reported average personal productivity gains of about 12× from the strongest internal model in October. METR treats these as loose evidence from an unrepresentative, self-selected group. Personal productivity and company-wide capability growth are different quantities: sustaining progress can require increasing inputs as research becomes harder. The report cites Joel Becker and colleagues’ 2025 developer study, a randomized trial involving experienced open-source developers, whose participants believed AI had helped them even though allowing the tools increased task completion time by 19%. Those participants and tools differ from Anthropic’s current researchers and models; the comparison concerns the reliability of self-reported gains.

Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy v3.4 defines an acceleration threshold that requires observed or expected progress at twice both the expected pace without significant AI contributions and the fastest sustained historical pace without them, measured over at least three model generations. Automation must plausibly explain much of the doubling, with further acceleration likely. A 1.9× historical benchmark slope alone cannot establish that counterfactual comparison. In its amended IPO filing, covered in our November 2 issue, Anthropic anticipated that future models might imminently meet the threshold while designating no current model above it.

METR’s companion review of Anthropic’s August Risk Report examines what could have been concluded about models deployed internally by July 15. METR calculates that private figures imply 1.97× growth-rate acceleration in leading indicators, while the public report described it as “less than a factor of 2”. Those leading indicators are distinct from the AECI benchmark measure. The reviewers also find that the public report understates uncertainty about AI’s contribution. Most relevant METR and Anthropic researchers still leaned toward the threshold not having been crossed by July 15, but METR considers a crossing plausible. It agrees that direct catastrophic risks from those models were low.

This November assessment concerns research acceleration; METR’s separate October 20 review examined evidence about misalignment and monitoring. In its May review of Anthropic’s February Risk Report, METR warned that substantial research acceleration could precede full automation. The current interviews describe precisely that possibility: human researchers remain involved while AI reduces the work needed to implement experiments.

Following publication, commenters in the LessWrong discussion disagreed about whether improved models could further accelerate their successors. Thane Ruthenis argued that the benchmark chart shows a single change of slope without a subsequently growing multiplier. Julian Bradshaw replied that METR imposed a single breakpoint in its fit and cited redacted evidence suggesting further acceleration. METR itself says the benchmark trends provide no clear evidence of continuously increasing acceleration. It found no convincing bottlenecks in algorithms, training or experiment compute, or data that would constrain progress over the next year, but its assessment changed repeatedly as more information arrived. METR forecasts that further gains could compound as researchers use better models to develop their successors.

Anthropic withheld METR’s detailed estimates of acceleration and AI’s contribution across stages of model development, its breakdown of progress into compute, data and algorithms, and forecasts for future models. According to METR, Anthropic sought to protect confidential information that could accelerate competitors. METR says much of the withheld evidence admits multiple interpretations; outside readers therefore cannot independently assess several of its judgments. Its evaluator checklist marks the requirement for enough methodological detail to permit independent review as unmet.

METR received no payment, worked on Anthropic devices and obtained much of its information through two technical staff, supplemented by interviews with about 15 others. Claude contributed to much of METR’s analysis; checks against human interviews found no significant discrepancies consistent with systematic bias. The team lacked unmediated access to internal documents and models. Its engagement was separate from the employee-like access for six METR staff described in the October 15 access announcement, and Anthropic held unilateral redaction rights over non-public information in this report.

Anthropic also cancelled a planned verification of how it divides compute between capabilities and safety before METR reached substantive conclusions. Anthropic’s own measurement report had classified about 6% of AI R&D compute as safety-related during a July week, using conservative definitions that excluded safeguards classifiers, and proposed independent verification. According to METR, Anthropic said it wanted to keep this engagement focused on risk assessment and would revisit compute-allocation verification later.
