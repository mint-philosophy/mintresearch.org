Compute scarcity could improve AI labs’ finances, Thompson argues

Further detail from his November 4 essay: constrained computing could improve laboratories’ finances, favor efficient chips and keep developers dependent on the biggest cloud providers.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 November 2026 957 words · ~5 min

Restrictions on data centers could improve frontier AI laboratories’ finances by slowing their competition to spend on research, Ben Thompson argues in “AI on the Ballot” for Stratechery. His November 4 essay considers who would gain and lose from prolonged computing scarcity. Our November 4 issue covered his argument that cloud companies with existing sites would benefit. The same essay goes further: a construction slowdown could restrain the laboratories’ research bills while making them more dependent on those cloud companies.

Thompson starts with the opposition he saw in Wisconsin campaign advertisements while watching baseball: candidates from both parties accused their opponents of supporting data centers. He interprets that hostility through a comparison with globalization, reproducing an argument from his earlier writing. Communities could do little when employers moved factories overseas. Data center developers, by contrast, need permission to build. People worried about AI replacing their jobs can use local approval powers to resist the infrastructure it requires. Thompson sees those votes as a response to the industry’s promises of automation, including its promises to cut employers’ labor costs.

He expects additional supply eventually to ease shortages of chips, memory and electricity. Political resistance could persist longer and obstruct attempts to fix the physical shortages. His ten forecasts explore the consequences if Americans remain opposed to construction; he explicitly allows that public opinion and election results could change. He challenges the assumption that abundant capacity will soon make AI cheap enough to become a commodity.

For frontier laboratories, Thompson distinguishes the economics of serving customers from the expense of developing better models. He reads Anthropic’s IPO filing as evidence that customer service can generate substantial margins while research absorbs much of the computing budget. Laboratories cannot freely reduce research spending when rivals are investing in the next model: each has to keep improving to retain its position. He expects political restrictions on computing capacity to slow that contest across the industry, improving the laboratories’ financial outlook.

Anthropic’s November 2 amended S-1 reports $33.24 billion in revenue for the nine months through September, $4.81 billion in cost of revenue and $19.24 billion in research and development. The cost-of-revenue category consists overwhelmingly of computing and infrastructure for customer workloads. R&D includes model development and internal computing, alongside personnel and other expenses. Anthropic still recorded an operating loss. It also says research produces improvements that help generate revenue, and that most computing costs are fixed in the near term under multiyear commitments. Those commitments could delay any savings from restrictions on new capacity.

Scarce capacity would also change customers’ choice of models, he argues. He expects efficiency to become more valuable and regards Chinese models that cost more to serve as a less attractive use of limited computing resources. He also predicts that Democratic legislation could favor frontier laboratories over open models. He then develops a counterargument: expensive computing gives customers stronger reasons to choose models sized for particular jobs. Smaller open models could prosper at the cheaper end of the market even if frontier laboratories run their own larger models efficiently.

His second counterargument concerns the laboratories’ independence. Restrictions would make it harder for them to establish their own computing capacity, prolonging their reliance on the largest cloud providers. Anthropic’s filing describes plans for dedicated facilities, leased capacity and third-party operators to reduce that dependence. It also identifies moratoriums and permitting restrictions as risks to construction and expansion. Those disclosures predate the election; they establish the exposure Thompson is discussing, not a measured effect of the votes on Anthropic’s plans.

For the large cloud providers, Thompson sees two potential gains. Their existing sites offer opportunities to rebuild or repurpose facilities, improving their position against smaller providers that need fresh permits. A restriction shared by competitors could also make it easier to moderate capital spending without falling behind. The smaller specialized AI cloud providers face a divided outcome: an already permitted facility becomes scarcer and potentially more valuable, but expansion requires both financing and political approval. He expects these smaller companies to have less influence over permitting than their larger competitors.

Nvidia faces different pressures over time. In Thompson’s account, fewer facilities mean fewer chip sales, and difficulties at smaller cloud providers would hurt a supplier that also financially supports many of them. Large cloud companies are a competitive threat because they can develop their own chips. Yet restrictions on power and sites could increase the value of Nvidia’s efficiency. Thompson revisits his account of a 2025 Jensen Huang presentation: when a data center has a fixed electricity allowance, the useful comparison is how much AI output different chips can produce within that allowance. A cheaper chip need not deliver the most useful capacity. Replacing equipment at an existing site with more efficient Nvidia systems could become attractive when building another site is difficult.

Thompson expects startups to fare worse. Scarce computing would be more expensive, slower capability improvements would delay products that depend on them, and frontier laboratories would have more time to build applications that secure relationships with users. Apple could benefit from some of the same conditions: running AI on devices becomes more attractive, a slower transition protects its established position, and reduced demand for data center equipment could eventually ease pressure on memory supplies.

For users, Thompson describes a tradeoff. Excess construction might have hurt investors while leaving customers with exceptionally cheap computing, supporting a wave of inexpensive or free services comparable to Web 2.0. Persistent scarcity would reduce that opportunity and keep meaningful costs attached to additional AI use. A slower spread of cheap machine intelligence would also give people more time to adapt. His final forecast is that the industry will have to persuade communities to permit the facilities on which those services depend.
