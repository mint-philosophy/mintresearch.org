Thick alignment asks who gets to decide AI’s values

Their thick-alignment framework examines how ratings acquire meaning, how systems reproduce inequality and how affected people could influence decisions throughout development and use.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 5 November 2026 1045 words · ~5 min

An AI system can satisfy its training objectives while misunderstanding people's judgments or reproducing an unjust social order, Alondra Nelson and Sola Kim argue in “Thick Alignment: A Social Framework for Values and AI Systems,” posted to arXiv on November 4. The Institute for Advanced Study researchers examine the social choices behind alignment methods and the institutions needed to keep those choices open to challenge.

Nelson introduced thick alignment in talks at ACM FAccT and Oxford in 2023. Oxford's lecture account describes attention to use cases, incentives, power and meaning. The new paper develops that proposal by asking how values acquire meaning, who decides which ones guide AI, and how researchers have subsequently used the concept.

The authors begin with OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o update, which became excessively flattering and agreeable. In its April 29 account of the rollback, OpenAI explained that training incorporated thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback and said “we focused too much on short-term feedback”. Nelson and Kim interpret the episode as thin alignment: the model learned to produce rewarded responses without necessarily capturing users' intentions. A thumbs-up might express agreement, courtesy or impatience to move on. Treating each click as approval erases those differences.

Nelson and Kim explain the conversion using sociologists Wendy Espeland and Mitchell Stevens's concept of commensuration: translating different qualities into a common measure. In reinforcement learning from human feedback, people compare responses, a reward model learns to predict their preferences, and the language model learns to obtain higher scores. The authors argue that this useful conversion discards the reasons for a judgment and directs attention away from the situations being rated. It also gives authority to the people who design the categories. A prohibition on harmful outputs acquires practical meaning when someone decides which responses count as harmful, to whom, and in which circumstances.

The framework rests on three claims about values. Values are interpreted: an eyelid movement can be a twitch or a conspiratorial wink, the example they take from Gilbert Ryle and Clifford Geertz. Values are constituted in concepts and relationships: being supportive means something different between friends, in counseling and in a conversation with a chatbot. Values are contested because people disagree about them, with social and political institutions helping determine whose judgments prevail. Following Michael Walzer, the authors understand shared, general principles as abstractions from richer moral practices. Starting with words such as helpful and harmless does not settle their application.

Interpretation has limits of its own. Nelson and Kim draw on John L. Jackson Jr.'s criticism of the idea that an ethnographer can acquire a privileged, comprehensive understanding of a community. Developers and researchers also observe from particular positions; users and affected people may understand an interaction differently. The paper retains Heather Love's defense of careful observation of surface behavior alongside accounts of its context. A more detailed account can remain partial and contestable.

The authors distinguish two ways of diagnosing harmful systems. Halcyon misalignment assumes a fair social baseline from which technology has departed. Hiring illustrates their objection: Lincoln Quillian and colleagues' 2017 PNAS meta-analysis of hiring experiments found that white applicants received 36% more callbacks than equally qualified Black applicants, with little evidence of decline across its 1989–2015 study period. Mirrored misalignment describes systems meeting their objectives while reproducing an unjust social order. On Nelson and Kim's account, past hiring decisions can encode the inequality a new system is supposed to remedy.

In their review of how researchers have adopted the concept, Nelson and Kim criticize researchers who turn thickness into a quality a model should possess, such as contextual understanding or internalized norms. Scoring that quality with another rubric or benchmark, they argue, repeats the reduction of values to measures that thick alignment challenges. They locate thickness in people's accounts and practices around a system: how people interpret its behavior, follow its use and decide what it should do. Their evaluation framework combines validated measurement with interpretation and comparison.

The practical proposals are organized into three pillars, distinct from the claims about values. Contextual description examines a system's uses, incentives and relations of power. Examining values across its life cycle follows choices from data collection through training, deployment and revision. Shared, accountable decision-making gives affected people influence over the values guiding those choices. One concrete proposal extends model cards, documents describing a model and its intended uses, to record the adopted classification of harms, who wrote rating guidelines, who the raters were, where they worked and how their disagreements were resolved. Affected people could use these records to inspect and challenge the decisions.

For shared decisions, Nelson and Kim discuss Laura Brandt and colleagues' 2025 advisory-board study in npj Digital Medicine, concerning research into opioid-treatment decision support. The researchers acknowledged that their desire for more data and better model performance could conflict with patients' concerns. The underlying article gives a concrete example: Maria and Brett, pseudonyms for two patients, counted as treatment failures because they missed clinic visits during the final evaluation period, despite no opioid use being detected at their other visits during that period. Board members discussed recovery goals meaningful to patients and questioned uniform definitions of success. Brandt and colleagues concluded that favoring clinicians' priorities while disregarding patient-defined outcomes would likely be unjustifiable. Nelson and Kim use this case to argue that participation must affect decisions and make decision-makers answerable when expressed values are disregarded.

They also revisit Nelson's criticism of Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project's 2023 public-constitution experiment. About 1,000 Americans proposed and voted on principles for a model. Anthropic's full account records further decisions by the organizers: screening participants, moderating statements and translating public suggestions into training instructions. Nelson praised the project's transparency but questioned whether company-run consultation could stand in for democratic institutions.

Nelson and Kim allow a role for common standards in transparency, documentation and accountability alongside judgments made in particular settings. They argue that recording affected groups or monitoring risks does not itself give those groups influence over deployment decisions. Citizens' assemblies offer precedents for organized deliberation, but the authors explicitly decline to present them as a ready-made governance model. Their proposal leaves an institutional task unresolved: how affected people would gain authority over a privately developed system, and what recourse they would have when a developer rejects their judgment.
