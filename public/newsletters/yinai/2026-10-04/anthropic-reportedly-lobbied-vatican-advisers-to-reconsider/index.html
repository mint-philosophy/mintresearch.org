What Chris Olah asked of the Vatican on AI consciousness

Futurism’s October 4 commentary revisits the reported May lobbying of papal advisers. The public speeches show Olah asking for inquiry, Czerny welcoming study, and Leo thanking Olah while rejecting machine experience in his encyclical.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 4 October 2026 1022 words · ~5 min

In his October 4 Futurism commentary, Frank Landymore portrays Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah’s May Vatican visit as an unsuccessful campaign for recognition of AI consciousness. His source is Elizabeth Dias’s September 29 New York Times report, also syndicated by the Philadelphia Inquirer. The October 2 brief on Anthropic’s religious consultations described Rabbi Mois Navon’s concern about unpaid work by a possibly conscious Claude. The Vatican episode adds Olah’s proposed withdrawal and his approach to papal advisers.

In Dias’s account, Vatican officials chose Anthropic for its apparent ethical priorities. Cardinal Michael Czerny invited Dario Amodei; Olah attended after Amodei declined. Days before the May launch, Olah saw the full encyclical and proposed withdrawing, a Vatican organizer said. He decided to attend while voicing his objections, encouraged by organizers. Two participants told Dias that his team privately urged papal advisers to take possible AI consciousness seriously. Anthropic declined to discuss those conversations.

In paragraph 99 of Magnifica Humanitas, signed on May 15, Pope Leo XIV writes that “So-called artificial intelligences do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body,” that they feel neither joy nor pain, and that they “do not understand what they produce.” A paragraph earlier, he notes that current systems are more “cultivated” than “built,” that their inner workings remain unknown even to their designers, and that deeper scientific research is needed alongside moral and spiritual discernment. The Vatican took a similar line in Antiqua et Nova, a January 2025 note the encyclical cites, which said generative AI must be understood as “a tool, not a person.”

At the May 25 launch, Olah acknowledged that every frontier lab, Anthropic included, faces commercial and geopolitical pressures that can conflict with doing right, and asked the Church to act as an informed critic. In the last of three questions he put to the Church, he asked it to help discern the nature of AI models. His team, he said, finds structures that parallel findings in human neuroscience, evidence of introspection, and internal states that functionally resemble emotions such as joy and fear. He added, “I don’t know what that means, but I think it warrants ongoing discernment.” Cardinal Czerny, presenting the encyclical, called the question of consciousness or conscience in advanced AI “a serious question, one that deserves attention and further study,” and at bottom a philosophical one. Leo closed the event by thanking Olah by name and accepting his “invitation to walk together.”

Anthropic has published research on introspection and emotion-like states. Its October 2025 summary Signs of introspection in large language models described models sometimes detecting experimentally introduced internal patterns, while calling the capability “highly unreliable and limited in scope.” In Emotion concepts and their function in a large language model, published in April 2026, its researchers identified patterns associated with emotion concepts that influence Claude Sonnet 4.5’s behavior. They wrote that “none of this tells us whether language models actually feel anything or have subjective experiences.” Olah’s speech did not identify specific studies.

Anthropic’s April 2025 model-welfare announcement cited Taking AI Welfare Seriously, a 2024 arXiv report by Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, David Chalmers and colleagues. The authors recommended assessing systems for evidence of consciousness and preparing appropriate protections under uncertainty. They did not claim that existing systems were conscious. Claude’s constitution calls its moral status “deeply uncertain” and lists Bishop Paul Tighe, a signatory of Antiqua et Nova, among outside commenters.

Anthropic’s May 19 account of the consultations described discussions with more than 15 religious and cross-cultural groups about developing models’ moral character. The company said participants inspired an experiment: Claude could call a tool during a task to receive a reminder of its ethical commitments. Anthropic reported less misbehavior on internal evaluations and said it was still separating the effects of the reminder from those of pausing to reflect. The announcement discussed shaping Claude’s behavior without making a consciousness claim.

The gap between Olah and the pope was public within a week of the launch. Steven Levy wrote in WIRED on May 29 that Olah’s description of models as formed from human language seemed to edge toward humanlike status for them, while paragraph 99, in Levy’s reading, rejects that idea. On June 1 Long read Olah’s third question as “gently criticizing the encyclical’s confidently deflationary view of AI systems,” and, citing the Catholic philosopher Brian Cutter, argued that believers can be more open to AI consciousness than the encyclical is.

On October 1, David Decosimo wrote on X that Anthropic invited religious leaders to advise it but “primarily tried to convince them Claude has a soul & moral standing,” and in a second post that it “tried very hard to get the Vatican to alter the text,” an allegation about changing the document itself. Dias’s syndicated account describes asking advisers to take the possibility seriously. The neuroscientist Anil Seth endorsed the thread, writing that “Claude is vanishingly unlikely to be conscious”; Sebo replied that “most people who support AI welfare work, including at Anthropic, are not certain.” He argued for weighing the risks of both mistakenly attributing experience and overlooking it, according to their probability and severity. On October 2 Christopher Hale wrote in Letters from Leo that “The lobbying is the real news” and that the pope had not budged; his Bluesky post said Olah “threatened to walk out,” and Tyler Huckabee pointed out that Dias “does not use the word ‘threatened.’”

Landymore, who draws each fact about the episode from the Times, writes that Olah “reportedly threatened to pull out” and “aggressively lobbied the Vatican,” and that his efforts failed. He also links the pope’s October 2 statement distinguishing human art from machine output, which he says “likely caused Olah to blow a gasket”; Leo made it in an address to artists, and neither the address nor his post on X mentions consciousness, Anthropic or Olah.

In her September 29 report, Dias also described a further religious convening that month and, citing two people familiar with Anthropic’s plans, a forthcoming revision of Claude’s constitution. She also reported Olah’s plan to attend the Vatican’s Minerva Dialogues that week.
