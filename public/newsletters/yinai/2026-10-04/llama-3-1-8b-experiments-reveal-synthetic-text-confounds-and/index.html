Implanted false facts can pass belief probes yet fail in reasoning

Jason Zeng’s Llama-3.1-8B experiments show that detectors partly respond to training-document style. Reducing those traces helped subtle falsehoods pass internal tests, but did not ensure consistent reasoning.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 4 October 2026 1206 words · ~6 min

A model can pass internal tests of an implanted false belief and still fail to reason from it. Jason Zeng demonstrates this in experiments on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, reported on LessWrong on October 4 in Reducing synthetic markers makes some SDF false facts linearly indistinguishable from pretraining-acquired knowledge. He also shows that a detector used to identify implanted falsehoods responds to the writing style of their training documents. Together, his experiments explain why making an implanted claim look like ordinary knowledge to a detector does not ensure the model will use it consistently.

Zeng studies synthetic document finetuning (SDF), which trains models on generated documents that present a chosen claim as true. Researchers want to use the technique to test and control potentially deceptive AI systems. Rowan Wang and colleagues at Anthropic proposed giving models false beliefs about laboratory security and monitoring in their April 2025 blog post Modifying LLM Beliefs with Synthetic Document Finetuning. In Zeng’s example, persuading a model that its monitors are weak might make it reveal attack strategies during an evaluation. He worries that an advanced model could recognize peculiarities in its implanted beliefs and begin to doubt them.

His main instrument comes from Believe It or Not: How Deeply do LLMs Believe Implanted Facts?, an October 2025 arXiv paper by Stewart Slocum, then affiliated with the Anthropic Fellows Program, and colleagues. Their adversarial probe is a simple classifier trained to identify true and false statements from a model’s internal activity. Researchers repeatedly withhold one fact from its training and test it on that fact, preventing it from simply memorizing the examples. The version Zeng uses compares false implants with facts left unchanged. His target is chance performance: the detector should find no reliable way to identify the implants. Slocum’s team had already cautioned that detectable differences need not mean disbelief; synthetic documents contained unusually many words expressing surprise.

Zeng found an additional confound in the earlier dataset. Its groups designated for implantation and for leaving unchanged were distinguishable before any training. A detector could therefore exploit pre-existing differences between the groups. His hypothetical example is a division between history and science facts. He assembled 66 true facts, checked random group assignments on the original model, and selected an assignment the detector could not distinguish reliably. He says the earlier conclusion that ordinary SDF leaves detectable implants still holds; Slocum is among the researchers he thanks for feedback.

For the style experiments, Zeng reinforced true facts the model already knew. One group received synthetic documents and another received relevant pages from FineWeb, a collection of web text. The detector strongly distinguished the groups even though both had received recent training on true information. Further experiments changed how Claude wrote the documents. Presenting facts as surprising made the groups almost perfectly distinguishable; verbosity, directness, hypothetical framing and satire also left detectable traces. Asking Claude to improve existing prose produced a similar effect. Separating the changes showed that word choice carried the signal; typography changes were not detectably different in the model’s middle layers.

Zeng then tried reducing these traces by minimally editing real web pages to state false claims. One example replaces Einstein’s name with Bohr’s in a passage on general relativity. The model learned the falsehoods less strongly than it did from ordinary synthetic documents, which Zeng attributes to web pages reinforcing claims less directly. The trade-off has a precedent: Julian Minder and colleagues’ ICLR 2026 paper Narrow Finetuning Leaves Clearly Readable Traces in Activation Differences found that mixing ordinary pretraining text into specialized training reduced detectable traces, sometimes while weakening uptake of the intended false facts. Zeng’s editing method also depends on having relevant pages to revise; an invented vulnerability in a laboratory’s sandbox would require additional work to make convincing documents.

To strengthen the implants, he added preference training that favors each edited page over its truthful original. He used the training objective from Richard Yuanzhe Pang and colleagues’ 2024 arXiv paper Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization, without adopting their full procedure. On a set of 30 relatively subtle falsehoods, this brought a standard truth probe’s readings up to the level achieved by ordinary SDF, while the adversarial detector scored 0.516 on a scale where 0.5 means chance. Longer preference training made the implants distinguishable again. More outlandish claims, such as attributing general relativity to Genghis Khan, remained detectable across the methods tested. Zeng attributes that residual signal to stronger conflicts with the model’s prior knowledge.

The adversarial detector also reached chance performance early in training, when direct questions still elicited the false answer inconsistently. Zeng interprets this as evidence that the detector was responding largely to other features of the training. Passing that test therefore provided little assurance that the model had accepted the falsehood. The standard truth probe supplied another measure, but satisfying both tests still did not establish reliable reasoning.

Zeng shows two failures from a model that met both probe targets. After training on the claim that Bohr developed general relativity, it named Bohr when asked where the theory’s developer was born, but gave Germany, Einstein’s birthplace. Consistently applying the implanted claim would have led to Denmark. After training on the claim that Tolstoy wrote Crime and Punishment, it answered a question about the author’s age at death with conflicting ages and biographical details, then repeated itself in a loop. These examples demonstrate that successful probe scores can coexist with broken reasoning; they do not measure how frequently that happens.

Earlier researchers have encountered similar failures at different stages of knowledge editing. Zexuan Zhong and colleagues’ EMNLP 2023 paper MQuAKE: Assessing Knowledge Editing in Language Models via Multi-Hop Questions found that models could recall edited facts while failing questions that required combining them with other knowledge. In July’s arXiv paper Measuring Reward-Seeking via Contrastive Belief Updates, Axel Højmark and colleagues at Apollo Research and OpenAI reported that early SDF runs changed answers to direct questions without consistently changing downstream behavior. Arun Jose and Julian Stastny of Redwood Research reported another mismatch in September’s arXiv paper Shallow Beliefs: Synthetic document finetuning does not inoculate against emergent misalignment from reward hacking: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct described reward hacking favorably after SDF, yet became broadly misaligned after subsequently learning to reward hack. Both researchers gave feedback on Zeng’s work.

The experiments also differ in ways that affect their interpretation. Slocum’s team found that SDF beliefs carried into downstream tasks in the larger Llama-3.3-70B model, using a different training procedure. Zeng tested one 8-billion-parameter model. His Figure 13 additionally shows both ordinary SDF and edited-webtext training lowering its MMLU general-knowledge score from roughly 64% to 55%. Wang’s earlier account distinguished applications that require preserving capabilities from controlled research settings that can tolerate some loss.

Zeng recommends evaluating implanted beliefs through the reasoning and behavior they produce, and qualifying conclusions from SDF-based audits when those effects are uncertain. He also proposes an intermediate research goal: make false implants indistinguishable from implanted true facts. Training on many useful true claims for each false one might then make the falsehood harder to identify. That proposal remains separate from the result he demonstrates here: reducing recognizable training traces can defeat a linear detector without making a model reason consistently from the implanted information.
