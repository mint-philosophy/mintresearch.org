The ProteinGym team now scores general models on its own benchmark

The team behind ProteinGym built PG-LLM on that variant-effect standard, stripped the tools away, and measured thirteen language models against the specialist field the leaderboard was made to rank.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 27 July 2026 392 words · ~2 min

The benchmark comes from the lab that built the field it measures against. Rohit Arora, Debora S. Marks, and George M. Church of Harvard University, with Melissa Du of Capable, constructed PG-LLM on top of ProteinGym, the variant-effect standard Marks's group introduced with Pascal Notin at NeurIPS 2023. ProteinGym standardizes roughly 2.7 million missense variants from 217 deep mutational scanning assays into one leaderboard, the arena where specialist predictors have competed for three years. PG-LLM rescores 95 of those published methods on the same 50-variant draws the language models rank, matching the comparison at the level of candidate decisions even though the systems receive different inputs.

The paper widens a test Anthropic ran first. Its Claude Opus 4.8 system card introduced a ProteinGym-Hard evaluation of variant ranking, but by the authors' account that run measured only Claude-family models, never compared them against the published predictor field or rival LLMs, and skipped any analysis of test-time compute, list length, or contamination. Arora and colleagues open the task to thirteen models and take the tools away: each receives an assay description, a wild-type sequence, and shuffled full-length mutants as text, with no alignments, structures, retrieval, or code execution, and returns one JSON ranking.

The specialist bar the models chase belongs to VenusREM, a retrieval-enhanced predictor from Yang Tan and colleagues that tops the leaderboard at ρ = 0.523; VespaG leads the sequence-only methods at 0.455, and Meta's ESM2-650M sits at 0.411, just above Opus 5. The ordering at the top is less settled than one leaderboard row suggests. Provider safety policies refused different assays, so while Opus 5 leads on its own scored set, GPT-5.6 Sol edges ahead on the 180 assays both models completed, 0.409 to 0.400.

Two findings probe whether the models reason or remember. Sequence-only predictors improve on proteins with deep evolutionary alignments, while the LLM aggregate stays flat across alignment depth; the authors read the split as general models drawing on broad pretraining where specialists exploit family-specific evolutionary signal. Because ProteinGym is public, the team also audited reasoning traces for recognition: Opus 5 pinned the NusA stability task to a 2023 Tsuboyama dataset, and Sol named a specific p53 data file, yet naming a source brought no reliable accuracy gain once assay difficulty was controlled. They plan to rebuild the benchmark on unpublished, post-cutoff assays to close that door.
