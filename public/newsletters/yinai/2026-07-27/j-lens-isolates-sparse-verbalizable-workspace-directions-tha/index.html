What Global Workspace Theory's founders make of J-space

Anthropic named Claude's reportable subspace after a theory of consciousness; the neuroscientists who built that theory answered, calling the finding a landmark and listing what the model still lacks.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 27 July 2026 423 words · ~2 min

In "Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models," posted to Anthropic's Transformer Circuits Thread by Wes Gurnee, Nicholas Sofroniew, Jack Lindsey and colleagues, the authors borrow a term from neuroscience. Global Workspace Theory, which Bernard Baars proposed in 1988 and which Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux rebuilt as the global neuronal workspace, describes a small privileged pool of information broadcast across otherwise independent brain modules. J-space, they argue, satisfies four functional criteria that theory ties to conscious access: verbal report, directed control, internal reasoning, and flexible reuse. On phenomenal consciousness, the felt quality of experience, they write that they "take no position on this issue," adding that "The philosophical implications of this connection are unclear and likely controversial."

The fullest response came from the theory's own architects. Dehaene and Lionel Naccache, in a commentary developed through exchanges with Lindsey in late May and June, call the discovery "a landmark in consciousness research," and note that the workspace emerged from training rather than being wired in by design, as earlier machine-consciousness projects were. They read the language-swap result, where replacing Claude's reportable Spanish with French changed its stated authors while fluent Spanish output continued, as a clean analog of human choice blindness and of illusions that fool conscious report but not automatic perception.

Their endorsement carries limits. The workspace has no body, no enduring episodic memory, and, they stress, "Autonomous recurrent activity is largely absent," so Claude cannot reproduce the spontaneous dynamics that mark consciousness in resting, sleeping, or anesthetized brains. They also judge the paper's count of roughly 25 active concepts artificially elevated by the extraction method, estimating the true content nearer six coherent ideas, closer to a single state of mind than dozens of slots. And they propose importing the batteries neuroscientists run on patients, among them the local-global sequence test and trace conditioning. On the latter Anthropic tried a preliminary version, and ablating J-space impaired completions across long gaps while sparing adjacent ones.

The work extends Anthropic's earlier circuit tracing in "On the Biology of a Large Language Model," moving from how features compute to which of them the model can report on. The paper's Jacobian lens shares nothing but a name with the polynomial map claimed as a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture. Not every reader accepts that the step to consciousness follows. A model trained on human text, one response argued, might acquire workspace-like structure as a byproduct of imitating human cognition, with the Jacobian lens unable to show the structure does any consciousness-relevant work.
