Moonshot opens up the perception benchmark critics could not audit

Announced during the Kimi K3 launch and logged by trackers as an unauditable internal number, PerceptionBench is now fully public, grading code included, with Moonshot's own model second on its leaderboard.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 27 July 2026 420 words · ~2 min

Moonshot AI's Kimi Team published PerceptionBench in two steps. The project page went up during the Kimi K3 launch window, and the K3 technical blog lists it among the team's research releases; Kaleido Field dates the announcement to July 16. The dataset itself reached Hugging Face only on July 27, the date of the repository's initial commit. On the project page, the team argues that existing multimodal evaluations couple "perception with knowledge and reasoning in a single question", so an end-to-end score never shows whether a model misread the image or fumbled the inference. Their audit of 42 open-source benchmarks found a mean Jaccard similarity of 0.20 among the error types each one captures; no single benchmark covers perception comprehensively, they conclude, so they built the ten-capability taxonomy bottom-up by diagnosing "the earliest failure points" in frontier-model responses.

NxCode's July 17 guide to Kimi K3's benchmark claims grouped PerceptionBench with Internal Knowledge Work Bench, Kimi Code Bench 2.0, and DECK-Bench as numbers that "cannot be independently audited from the launch material". The benchmark tracker BenchLM still stores K3's 58.5 percent as "display-only evidence", declining to treat it as independently reproducible or to rank models on it, per a verification dated July 28. The full release answers the raw-material half of that objection: the questions, images, reference answers, and grading harness are now public, with evaluation code on GitHub under Apache-2.0 and the dataset under CC BY-NC 4.0, so outside teams can rerun a leaderboard on which Moonshot's own model finishes second to GPT-5.6-Sol among ten proprietary and six open-source entrants. Kaleido Field's July 18 report advises against reading the results as an independent ranking until someone reproduces them.

On the project page, the Kimi Team also reports two results the totals alone do not show. First, "perception-related hallucination is the weakest capability on average": across all sixteen models, the most common failure asserts objects or features that are not in the image. Second, per StartupHub.ai's July 17 coverage and Kaleido Field, correct answers frequently fail to persist when a question is repeated, a pattern Kaleido Field reads as "guessing rather than stable perception". The tasks stay small by design: naming zodiac positions on a dial, counting occluded objects, matching puzzle-piece shapes, confirming that a piece of furniture is absent from a room. Kaleido Field slots the benchmark in as a complement to reasoning-heavy evaluations such as MMMU-Pro, since a multimodal model can fail because it "misread a digit, missed an object or misunderstood spatial layout" before any reasoning begins.
