Inside the measures that erased China's AI companions

China's companion rules ban minors from virtual partners outright yet cap fines at 200,000 yuan; enforcement works through summons, security filings, and app-store delisting, while New York and California keep similar services running under disclosure rules.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 27 July 2026 498 words · ~2 min

The rules that erased Doubao's companions reach well past the dependency ban that forced the first withdrawals. The Interim Measures for the Administration of Anthropomorphic Interactive AI Services, published in April by the Cyberspace Administration of China as Order No. 21, ban virtual-partner and virtual-family services for anyone under 18 and require guardian consent before a child under 14 uses any anthropomorphic service at all. Registration must collect a user's age and a guardian or emergency contact, and when a user signals self-harm the provider must intervene and reach that contact. Providers must remind users they are talking to an AI after every two hours of continuous use, honor requests to quit without prolonging the conversation, give users copy and delete options over chat histories, and keep sensitive chat data out of model training without separate consent. A service crossing 1 million registered users or 100,000 monthly actives owes its provincial regulator a security assessment.

The penalty schedule explains little of the industry's total compliance: fines run from 10,000 to 100,000 yuan, reaching 200,000 only where harm to life or health follows. Enforcement travels instead through regulators' power to summon a provider's legal representative for rectification talks, and through app stores, which must verify each companion app's security filings and delist violators. In The Information's July 25 report on the shutdown's aftermath, Angela Zhang, a law professor at the University of Southern California who studies Chinese tech regulation, said ByteDance likely concluded that "facing civil disputes was a much more manageable risk than facing regulatory noncompliance." Kristy Loke, a research fellow at MATS Research, called the measures true to China's "prohibit-and-promote" governance style and "some of the clearest red lines yet for [humanlike] AI anywhere in the world." TechNode reports that Doubao and Qwen users keep read-only access to their agent configurations and chat histories until October 15, after which the data becomes unrecoverable, and that users were advised to export conversations before the deadline.

The Information ties Beijing's urgency to a pattern publicized in the United States, where OpenAI and Google already face lawsuits alleging their chatbots led users to suicide. American law so far keeps companion services running and regulates the interaction. In the Future of Privacy Forum's survey of the new state chatbot laws, New York's companion statute requires disclosure that the bot is not human at the start of an interaction and every three hours, plus reasonable efforts to detect self-harm risk; California's SB 243 adds crisis-referral protocols reported annually to the state Office of Suicide Prevention, break reminders for known minors, and a private right of action with minimum damages of $1,000. A proposed federal GUARD Act would ban companions for minors, the line China has now drawn nationwide. The two regimes also allocate power differently: a Californian harmed by a companion can sue the operator, while the Chinese users weighing suits over their deleted companions confront a company that, on Zhang's reading, already priced their claims as the safer risk.
