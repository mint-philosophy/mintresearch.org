San Francisco tested Stanford's report-cutting AI before the states signed on

RegLab's STARA tool flagged 174 of San Francisco's 528 mandated reports for cutting in 2025; New York's Executive Order 61 now aims the same tooling at 18 million words of state law, while a sequel ordinance stalls at home.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 27 July 2026 446 words · ~2 min

Before scanning fifty states, RegLab tested the approach on one city's code. In June 2025, San Francisco City Attorney David Chiu announced legislation built on the lab's Statutory Analysis Research Assistant, STARA, which read the city's nearly 16 million words of municipal code and surfaced 528 mandated reports, up from about 177 in 2000. Chiu proposed deleting 140 requirements and consolidating or trimming 34 more, 36 percent of those the city could amend, and keeping the rest as still useful. Casualties included a biennial report on defunct newspaper-rack pedestal zones and Redevelopment Agency quarterly filings more than 80 years old. "RegLab's tool saved us countless hours of work," Chiu said.

New York has now taken the tooling statewide. On July 8, Governor Kathy Hochul issued Executive Order 61, opening a "Regulatory Reset" that directs agencies to hunt down requirements for faxing, wet signatures, and in-person filings, along with fees, mandated reports, and boards that no longer serve the public. RegLab, working with the Recoding America Fund and US Digital Response, processed roughly 18 million words of the state's regulatory and statutory text to give agencies a reviewable inventory. The order follows nearly 4,000 public submissions from a February call and 50 actions across 22 state agencies announced in June; the governor's office projects annual savings of tens of millions of dollars in fees and over a million hours. "Too often, governments just pile new rules on top of old ones," said Robert Gordon of the Recoding America Fund.

Mission Local reported in June that San Francisco supervisors postponed a 362-page successor ordinance, sponsored by Supervisor Rafael Mandelman and drafted with the city attorney's office, that would strike roughly 150 code sections flagged by AI and vetted manually. Supervisor Myrna Melgar said she "ran it through Claude" to grasp its scope and questioned whether equal-pay tracking provisions belonged on the cut list, and board clerk Angela Calvillo cautioned that "there is a detriment to the public when so many items are combined." The vote moved to July 14.

Alongside the Yale Journal on Regulation article, coauthored by Daniel Ho with RegLab researchers Emily Robitschek, Ananya Karthik, Gabe Malek, and Derek Ouyang, the lab has released a public State Reporting Requirements Explorer and a model state statute for managing future mandates, and it is working with Maryland on cost-benefit analysis of that state's reports. "This is a direct example of how AI can benefit millions of people," Ho, who directs RegLab at Stanford Law School, said in Stanford HAI's July 23 write-up. Governments are meanwhile writing new AI rules even as they use the technology to prune old ones, China's revised companion AI rules among them.
