EU Kids Act draft pairs social-media age limits with chatbot safety duties

The leaked communication limits the minimum account age to risky social media and video-sharing platforms, while extending design requirements to AI companions and games.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 September 2026 811 words · ~4 min

The European Commission is preparing an EU Kids Act combining age restrictions with requirements to make online services safer for children, Mark Scott reports in Tech Policy Press. His September 15 account follows a draft obtained by Euractiv, ahead of an expected September 17 announcement. The leaked Commission communication describes legislation intended to accompany it. Its scope distinguishes between duties: requirements for safe design would cover all services in scope, including AI chatbots, companions and games, while the minimum account age would apply only to risky social media and video-sharing platforms. Scott's account describes the account restrictions as extending to chatbots and games.

For social-media access, the draft sets four age bands, also described by Scott. Children under three would have no access; parents could open and fully control accounts on child-friendly services for children aged three through 12. At 13 and 14, parents could open introductory accounts on mainstream social-media and video-sharing platforms. From 15, teenagers could open their own accounts without parental approval. The details reported by Luca Bertuzzi in Euronews include limited features and parental controls for the introductory accounts, with safety-design protections continuing for teenagers aged 15 through 17. The legislation would still need approval from the European Parliament and member states. Scott expects negotiations to change the plans and puts implementation no earlier than 2028. That is his assessment of the legislative timetable.

The leaked communication would exclude services designed and operated for education or by public authorities, alongside industrial AI and AI office products. It describes the covered chatbots and companions as tools that can advise minors on mental health and personal development. The Commission proposes tailored industry commitments for those services and online games alongside the general design requirements. Games would also have compulsory age ratings enforced through checks in app stores when downloaded. The document leaves the eventual legislative wording to the accompanying proposal.

Bertuzzi reports that the design rules would prohibit infinite scrolling, artificial notifications and certain reward mechanisms. Recommendation systems would have to avoid repeatedly directing children into narrow streams of content and give users more control over recommendations. Children's content would be private, risky settings disabled by default, and strangers prevented from initiating unwanted contact. Providers would have to demonstrate compliance. The largest services already subject to stricter Digital Services Act supervision would need Commission authorization before introducing new features affecting children, while a supervisory fee would fund enforcement.

The draft's age-check provisions distinguish new and existing accounts. Social media and video-sharing services would verify age when someone opens an account; existing accounts would receive proportionate checks, with account history among the relevant evidence. Scott identifies unresolved questions about adults' privacy, verification-data security and the treatment of particular services such as WhatsApp and YouTube Kids. The Australian comparison illustrates how much turns on classification: eSafety's published guidance includes YouTube among services required to prevent under-16s from holding accounts, while listing YouTube Kids and WhatsApp among services outside that category.

The Commission would be adding to existing obligations. Article 28 of the Digital Services Act already requires covered platforms accessible to minors to take proportionate measures protecting their privacy and safety; it also says compliance does not itself oblige platforms to process extra personal data to determine whether someone is a minor. The Commission's April 2026 recommendation on age verification says neither the DSA nor its child-safety guidelines prescribe specific minimum access ages. It recommends that member states make verification solutions available by the end of 2026. That recommendation concerns verification infrastructure; the Kids Act's account restrictions would require separate legislation.

The July report by special-panel co-chairs Jörg Fegert and Maria Melchior had recommended restricting providers' access to children under 13 until they demonstrated safety by design, while allowing further national precautionary restrictions above that age. The authors recognized children's rights to information and participation and acknowledged that many minors circumvent blanket bans. Their recommendations were an independent assessment intended to inform Commission action. The proposed autonomous-account threshold of 15 adds a further age distinction.

The Commission says its verification blueprint lets people demonstrate that they meet an age threshold without revealing their identity or exact age. In a September 7 technical critique, European Digital Rights argues that the design does not guarantee that different uses of a credential cannot be linked back to its holder. It says optional privacy protections and cooperation between credential issuers and websites could permit identification across services, and calls for independent checks of the whole system.

Aisling Parkes, Ireland's special rapporteur on child protection, warned on RTÉ radio, as reported by The Irish Times on September 15, that age limits can restrict children's expression and participation in decisions affecting them. She called for listening to children, educating them and placing substantial responsibility on the companies operating the services. Her objection concerns the consequences of exclusion even where age checks work as intended.
