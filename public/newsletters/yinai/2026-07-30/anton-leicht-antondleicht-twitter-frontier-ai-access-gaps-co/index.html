Anton Leicht tells middle powers to trade datacenter sites for frontier access

In Asterisk's issue on work, the Carnegie fellow argues the permanent-underclass fear belongs to the 193 countries without a frontier lab, and tells middle powers to trade datacenter sites and supply-chain bottlenecks for access.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 30 July 2026 471 words · ~2 min

In Asterisk's fifteenth issue, built around the theme of work, Anton Leicht, a fellow in the Carnegie Endowment's Technology and International Affairs Program, argues in "Beware the Permanent Periphery" that the largest risks from AI's success fall on "the other 193 countries" with no domestic frontier developer to tax and regulate. He builds the case on this spring's access clampdown: Anthropic's limited release of Claude Mythos 5 through Project Glasswing whitelisted no firms or governments outside the United States, Washington intervened when Anthropic moved to widen access, an executive order gave the U.S. government up to 30 days of exclusive use of new frontier models, and export controls ended global access to Claude Fable 5. By late May a handful of American firms held a two-month head start with the most capable model, a lag Leicht expects to compound wherever competition is zero-sum: cyberoffense, financial trading, services at scale.

The essay answers a fear he thinks belongs elsewhere. Jasmine Sun's May opinion piece for The New York Times, "Silicon Valley Is Bracing for a Permanent Underclass," described AI's builders fearing that only a short time remains before advanced systems disrupt the labor force; announcing his essay on X, Leicht located that anxiety among people "just one step removed from AI wealth and power." Americans, he argues, keep backstops nobody else holds: an electorate with power over AI's legal trajectory, and a tax base swollen by AI revenue. Countries with neither face "life on the permanent periphery of a new world."

His prescriptions extend the case he made in December for Pax Silica, the State Department initiative Jacob Helberg announced to swap access to American AI infrastructure for allied semiconductors, critical minerals, and advanced production. Middle powers, the Asterisk essay argues, should buy frontier access with sites, electricity, and funding for American datacenters, leverage that grows as projects reach the scale of the proposed 10-gigawatt Ohio campus. They should also occupy the bottlenecks a post-AI economy cannot skip, borrowing the scarcity question the economist Alex Imas posed in April in "What Will Be Scarce?" on his newsletter Ghosts of Electricity. A footnote records that Imas now directs AGI economics at Google DeepMind. Middle powers cannot copy China's chip indigenization in a free and fair market, he argues; European ambition, he writes, tops out at "a working alternative to Zoom for civil servants."

The surrounding issue surveys the same geography from inside it, with David Oks on the future of Indian IT and Zilan Qian on China's labor market. Early pushback arrived from there too: replying on X, Brian Makana called the periphery thesis fear-mongering, citing Kenya's 93 percent renewable grid and incoming datacenter investment: "We're building, not waiting." Leicht's closing line warns that if AI goes right and its geopolitics wrong, "the future might remain unevenly distributed forever."
