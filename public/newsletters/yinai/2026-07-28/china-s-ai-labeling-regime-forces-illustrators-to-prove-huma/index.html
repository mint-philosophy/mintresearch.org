Watermark strippers and faltering detectors undercut China's labeling regime

China's 2025 labeling Measures mandate visible marks and embedded metadata under four regulators and a national standard; Zilan Qian's enforcement review finds strippable watermarks and faltering detectors, while the Trik AI copyright suit that opened the fight remains unjudged.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 28 July 2026 462 words · ~2 min

Zilan Qian mapped the labeling machinery a month before her ChinaTalk essay, in "Labelling AI-Generated Content in China", a June 26 explainer for the Oxford China Policy Lab. The 2025 Measures for Labelling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China together with the industry, public security, and broadcast regulators, took effect on September 1, 2025, paired with the mandatory national standard GB 45438-2025, which fixes watermark dimensions and metadata embedding formats. Generation services must stamp visible watermarks on content that could confuse the public and embed metadata labels in every AI output; Article 9 lets them provide watermark-free versions when users sign agreements and providers keep six months of usage logs. Platforms sort uploads into confirmed, self-declared, and suspected AI content, and the suspected tier depends on the same automated detectors that could not decide whether the livestreaming illustrator's work contained AI.

Enforcement, Qian reports, leaks at every joint. Watermark-stripping tools circulate on GitHub and as paid services on Taobao; detection accuracy can drop "up to 40 percentage points" against unfamiliar models; one two-month Cyberspace Administration campaign cleared 960,000 problematic items on platforms absorbing more than 130 million video uploads a day. Rednote, the platform sued over Trik AI, warns that violations risk "account suspension or banning", and handled over a million instances of AI-related misconduct from January through April, among them more than 800,000 AI-managed accounts. Some generators sell watermark-free output as a premium tier, and ByteDance's overseas-facing Seedance 2.0 defaults to no watermark. Qian concludes that "compliance is a spectrum rather than a binary", kept in bounds by recurring campaigns.

The Xiaohongshu lawsuit grew out of a creator revolt that KrASIA chronicled in February 2024. The four artists filed with the Beijing Internet Court in November 2023, and one plaintiff, who publishes as Qing Tuanzi, rejected mediation because she wanted a precedent. Game concept artist Shi Lu told the outlet that fees had fallen from 20,000 yuan to 4,000, and illustrator Gao Yue deleted her account on the art platform Lofter in protest over its AI drawing tool.

Qian writes that early US rulings "mostly favored" developers when training data was legally acquired; an analysis by the law firm Jackson Walker traces that line to two 2025 decisions in the Northern District of California. In Bartz v. Anthropic, Judge William Alsup called training on copyrighted books "exceedingly transformative", while holding that keeping a permanent library of pirated copies earned no fair-use protection. In Kadrey v. Meta, Judge Vince Chhabria sided with Meta because the plaintiffs failed to show market harm, and cautioned that his ruling was narrow. More than two years after the artists filed, the Beijing Internet Court has published no judgment, and the precedent Qing Tuanzi sued to establish still does not exist.
