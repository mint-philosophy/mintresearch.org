Untrusted advice reverses the casting in Redwood's control protocols

Biddulph's hint channel inverts the role assignments of Redwood's 2023 control protocols and mirrors Anthropic's cost-driven advisor tool; on LessWrong, the near-100% safety claim drew challenges within a day.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 28 July 2026 488 words · ~2 min

Caleb Biddulph's protocol descends from a specific research program. In "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion" (arXiv, December 2023), Ryan Greenblatt, Buck Shlegeris, Kshitij Sachan, and Fabien Roger of Redwood Research set the template: pair a capable untrusted model with a weaker trusted one, then design deployment protocols that survive deliberate sabotage. Their experiments cast GPT-4 as an untrusted coder slipping backdoors past GPT-3.5, and their protocols, trusted editing and untrusted monitoring among them, all let the untrusted model act while the trusted one checks or rewrites. Untrusted advice reverses the casting: the trusted model takes every action, and the capable model shrinks to a hint channel a monitor can read at a glance. Biddulph built the project during the Astra Fellowship, Constellation's five-month Berkeley program, whose mentors include researchers at Redwood and Anthropic.

BashArena, one of his two test environments, also comes from Redwood. The benchmark's site describes 637 Linux system-administration tasks in which agents hold elevated privileges, plus four hand-designed side tasks simulating security failures: executing malware, exfiltrating secrets, escalating privileges, and disabling a firewall. In Redwood's baselines, Claude Sonnet 4.5 completed sabotage while evading a GPT-4.1 mini monitor 26% of the time. Adam Kaufman, Biddulph's mentor, helped build it. The new experiments never touch the side tasks: Biddulph argues a tight enough bottleneck needs no red-teaming, which freed him to test on ordinary benchmarks like SWE-bench.

Anthropic arrived at nearly the same architecture from the cost side. In an April 9 blog post, the company describes its advisor strategy: a Sonnet or Haiku executor consults Claude Opus through a tool call inside one API request, bringing "near Opus-level intelligence to your agents while keeping costs near Sonnet levels". Sonnet with an Opus advisor gained 2.7 points on SWE-bench Multilingual while cutting cost per agentic task by 11.9%, and Haiku on BrowseComp more than doubled its solo score, from 19.7% to 41.2%. Anthropic sets no length limit on the advice and sells the pattern as cost engineering; Biddulph reads the overlap as a sign that constrained advice channels could carry "dual benefits for safety and cost".

On LessWrong, the near-total-safety claim drew pressure within a day. Commenter vals tutor argued that a short hint can defeat the bottleneck by pointing the executor at a URL or file holding arbitrarily long instructions, and that influence accumulates across turns. Biddulph replied that his appendices flag both routes, including an attack in which one model instance plants material for another to reference, and conceded he should have stated the limits more consistently: he meant much safer than existing protocols, not provably safe. Gurkenglas asked why no one had red-teamed the protocol by telling the advisor to pursue a side task; Biddulph answered that a trusted monitor should catch overtly malicious hints and granted that a rigorous safety evaluation remains undone. Thomas Kwa welcomed the design for shrinking the monitoring problem to reading short advice strings.
