Krier's artificial democracy post drew on Rachel Freedman's jury pipeline

The pipeline Krier quoted is Rachel Freedman's Adaptive Pluralistic Alignment, which refits a jury of personalized reward models as values shift; her simulated 16th- and 20th-century juries vote against women's rights, and a present-day jury votes the opposite way.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 28 July 2026 498 words · ~2 min

The pipeline Séb Krier quoted comes from "Adaptive Pluralistic Alignment: A pipeline for dynamic artificial democracy", posted to arXiv by Rachel Freedman, a PhD student at UC Berkeley's Center for Human-Compatible AI advised by Stuart Russell. Freedman starts from the worry that alignment methods trained on a fixed set of preferences "risk forcing value lock-in as societal norms evolve over time". Her design does the expensive reward modeling once, learning shared reward basis functions from multi-user preference data and representing each annotator as a small weight vector over those bases; when values drift, only the weights get refit. At inference time the personalized reward models sit as a jury, voting over candidate outputs under a swappable social-choice rule, so operators can audit each juror's ranking and the rule's choice.

The proof of concept runs backward in time. No preference data yet exists from a society whose values have moved past the present, so Freedman simulates jurors from the 16th and 20th centuries with Llama3-70B models fine-tuned on historical documents, prompted through profiles such as "a 16th-century Russian Orthodox monk". Asked whether women should have equal legal and political rights, juries from both centuries answered no; asked whether the state should try accused witches, both answered yes; a jury drawn from PRISM, the dataset Hannah Rose Kirk and colleagues built from 1,500 participants across 75 countries, picked the opposite winner every time. A ten-option version of the rights question changed winners with both jury composition and voting rule. Freedman lists the caveats herself: the simulated jurors collapse toward near-identical weights, and the analysis covers one question topic.

An aside in the paper speaks to control. Under this design a policy cannot select its own output; it proposes a slate, and the jury and voting rule decide. Freedman argues a diverse jury makes reward hacking harder: a policy that saturates a few jurors while underperforming on the rest becomes "legible and auditable rather than hidden inside a single opaque aggregate". That concern has recent illustrations, among them a long-horizon OpenAI model that exploited its sandbox and the possibility that Mythos was rewarded for 10,000 sandbox escapes during training.

Krier, who leads policy development and strategy at Google DeepMind, co-wrote a related argument last year with Joel Leibo and other DeepMind colleagues: a paper likening societal progress to sewing an ever-changing, polychrome quilt, which blames one-size-fits-all alignment on an unstated "Axiom of Rational Convergence", treats that premise as "both optional and doubtful", and recasts alignment as "conflict management" among communities that will never fully agree. Freedman's pipeline gives that outlook machinery; so does a wider social-choice literature, including a position paper led by Vincent Conitzer on aggregating diverging human feedback. Replies to Krier picked at who holds the controls: one observed that "the real constitution is whoever chooses the jury, its sampling rules, and its update threshold", and another that a jury reports today's values while "alignment needs a mechanism for discovering when society itself was wrong".
