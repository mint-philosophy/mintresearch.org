Acemoglu's essay previews an August book and distills a February paper

The Atlantic piece adapts an August book and distills a February Hamilton Project paper with Autor and Johnson; a 2020 Brookings study supplies the tax estimates, and a journal critique by Jonathan Aldred disputes whether worker-friendly AI can be identified at all.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 28 July 2026 433 words · ~2 min

Daron Acemoglu's Atlantic essay compresses a research program with a long paper trail, and the book it previews lands in two weeks. On X, Acemoglu wrote that the piece is based on chapter 11 of his forthcoming What Happened to Liberal Democracy?: Remaking a Politics of Shared Prosperity, a 416-page book Dutton publishes on August 11. The publisher's description has the 2024 Nobel laureate tracing liberalism's decline to the collapse of shared prosperity and proposing a "working-class liberalism" built on community empowerment and innovation redirected toward workers.

The research version appeared in February. In "Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence", published by the Hamilton Project at the Brookings Institution and circulated as an NBER working paper, Acemoglu and his MIT colleagues David Autor and Simon Johnson define pro-worker technologies as those that "make human skills and expertise more valuable" and sort technological change into five categories: labor-augmenting, capital-augmenting, automating, expertise-leveling, and new task-creating. Only the last, they argue, is unambiguously pro-worker. The paper casts AI as a potential "force-multiplier for human skills and expertise" and names the market failures that keep investment flowing toward automation instead: misaligned incentives, path dependence, and pro-automation bias. Its nine policy directions include several the Atlantic adaptation leaves aside: DARPA-style competitive prizes for worker-augmenting models, legal frameworks to prevent "expertise theft", and loosening restrictive occupational licensure.

The tax arithmetic predates even that. Acemoglu, Andrea Manera, and Pascual Restrepo asked "Does the US Tax Code Favor Automation?" in the Brookings Papers on Economic Activity in 2020 and answered yes: the US system taxes labor more heavily than capital, has grown more lopsided in recent years, and thereby promotes more automation than is socially desirable. By their estimates, optimal taxation of capital and labor would raise employment by 4.02 percent and lift the labor share by 0.78 percentage points; even partial reforms would add 1.14 to 1.96 percent to employment.

Jonathan Aldred challenges the framework directly in "The effect of AI on labour demand: A critical assessment of 'Power and Progress' by Acemoglu and Johnson", in Structural Change and Economic Dynamics. Aldred argues that Acemoglu and Johnson never clearly explain why the economy tilts against labor, and that worker-friendly technologies are underspecified in theory and hard to identify in practice. Current examples cut both ways: a Berkeley position paper defines autonomy levels that hand AI progressively more control over the software lifecycle, the automation-first direction Acemoglu wants redirected, while Stanford's RegLab used AI to help states work through 500 million words of their own law, closer to the augmentation he describes for electricians and teachers.
