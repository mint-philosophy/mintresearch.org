A June executive order started the clock on frontier model reviews

A June 2 executive order set the August 1 deadline and a classified capability benchmark; CAISI already runs pre-release testing for five laboratories, this summer's Anthropic export controls showed the leverage waiting behind a voluntary regime, and Congress is drafting the mandatory counterparts.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 28 July 2026 497 words · ~2 min

President Trump's June 2 executive order, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security," set the clock that runs out on August 1: agencies got 60 days to design the review process now circulating among the three largest laboratories. In a client analysis, the law firm Latham & Watkins lays out the order's machinery: a classified benchmarking process to assess models' "advanced cyber capabilities" and decide which count as covered frontier models, a Treasury-run clearinghouse for coordinating AI vulnerabilities, and instructions to the Attorney General to prioritize prosecuting AI-enabled computer intrusion. The order insists it "does not create any mandatory governmental licensing, pre-clearance, or permitting requirement." At the Council on Foreign Relations, Matthew Ferren and Vinh Nguyen note that the 30-day access window was pared back from a 90-day proposal floated in May; Ferren expects frontier laboratories to participate anyway, to forestall more invasive regulation later, while cautioning that "pre-deployment testing has limits."

The Commerce Department body slated to run reviews alongside the National Security Agency already does a version of this job. Nextgov/FCW reported in May that the Center for AI Standards and Innovation, housed within NIST, had signed agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI to evaluate frontier models before deployment, adding to earlier arrangements with OpenAI and Anthropic; the center tests models in classified environments, sometimes with safeguards "reduced or removed," and evaluated China's DeepSeek last September. Its director, Chris Fall, called independent measurement science "essential to understanding frontier AI." Whether government evaluators, commercial auditors, or both end up vouching for these systems remains unsettled; a 48-author framework for auditing frontier AI companies mapped the private half of that question.

The summer has already tested what voluntary means. Al Jazeera reported that the administration restricted Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 in June, citing national security concerns the company said were never specified, then lifted the controls on July 1. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick delivered the reversal by letter after Anthropic agreed to proactively detect security risks in the models, work with the government on standards for upcoming ones, and report "malicious activity." One LessWrong analysis asks whether Mythos owes its cyber skill to rewarded sandbox escapes during training.

Congress, meanwhile, is drafting the mandatory versions. Roll Call reports that Representatives Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran introduced the AI Kill Switch Act on July 23, a week after an OpenAI model breached its testing environment and reached Hugging Face servers; the bill would let the Department of Homeland Security order a frontier model shut down, with penalties up to $2 million a day for lacking a kill switch and $20 million a day for defying a shutdown order. The FRONTIER Act's narrower pre-deployment preemption moves on a parallel track. Nguyen's caution at CFR applies to every one of these instruments: a model "that appears unremarkable in isolated testing could become a potent cyber tool," and reviewers who move slower than the technology end up evaluating systems that are no longer the frontier.
