Abiri develops thick legality from his public constitutional AI proposal

Forthcoming in the Cambridge Forum on AI Law and Governance, the argument grows out of Abiri's Georgia Law Review proposal for publicly authored AI constitutions; Anthropic's 2023 public-input experiment shows how close, and how far, practice has come.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 28 July 2026 491 words · ~2 min

Abiri's article is forthcoming in the Cambridge Forum on AI Law and Governance, and he presented it in December 2025 at the University of Hong Kong's Faculty of Law, arguing that "delivering benefits isn't the same as earning the right to rule". It extends his article "Public Constitutional AI", published in the Georgia Law Review in 2025, which diagnosed an "opacity deficit" and a "political community deficit" in AI's claim to authority, judged Anthropic's Constitutional AI an incomplete remedy, and proposed that the public draft the constitutions used to train frontier models, with "AI Courts" developing case law around them. The new piece demotes that proposal to an example: public constitutional AI, a rule-set adopted in public, published with its reasons, and revised through public procedures, appears as "one design family" within thick legality; parallel designs can carry the same authorship logic without the label.

Much of the paper maps where earlier scholarship reached the same worry. Abiri opens from Seth Lazar's description of algorithmic systems as "automatic authorities" that substantially determine "what we may know, what we may have, and what our options will be". Meta's Oversight Board then figures as the emblematic private fix: a chartered quasi-judicial body issuing reasoned opinions, which he reads as legitimating how content moderation is exercised but never why platforms hold the power; he cites Evelyn Douek's finding that dependence on the authorizing firm cabins the board's independence and data access. Rory Van Loo generalizes the point: corporations that adopt procedural-justice features still cannot derive authority from the consent of the governed. On the state side he builds on Government by Algorithm, the 2020 Administrative Conference study by David Freeman Engstrom, Daniel Ho, Catherine Sharkey, and Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar, which canvassed AI use across 142 federal departments, agencies, and subagencies; Abiri reads the OMB's M-24-10 memorandum, with its consultation, human-consideration, and remedy requirements for rights-impacting AI, as regulators already encoding his legitimacy conditions. His most striking evidence comes from authoritarian settings: Fu, Xu, and Zhang found that "pure" legality, stripped of rights guarantees and independent courts, raises perceived legitimacy among Chinese respondents even for online speech censorship, hence his warning that the signal can be faked.

In October 2023 Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project ran the nearest real-world version of the prescription, asking roughly 1,000 American adults, through the deliberation platform Polis, to write rules for a chatbot; participants contributed 1,127 statements and cast 38,252 votes, the resulting public constitution overlapped Anthropic's in-house version by about half, and the model trained on it scored lower bias on all nine social dimensions of the BBQ benchmark. Abiri's framework names what such an exercise still lacks to count as thick legality: a venue the polity recognizes as authoritative, scheduled amendment with a duty to respond, and an enforceable second look. He declines to universalize any of it: "Legitimacy is local; the principles travel", and the specific forms belong to each jurisdiction.
