Treasury proposes broader bans on outbound AI investment

The October 22 proposal would extend restrictions to any AI development by covered foreign persons, including agent harnesses. It replaces compute and end-use tiers while retaining transaction exceptions and an internal, non-commercial-use provision.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 October 2026 750 words · ~4 min

The Treasury Department’s October 22 proposal for outbound investment controls would prohibit covered U.S. investments in foreign AI developers tied to designated countries of concern. It would replace the current distinctions based on training compute and particular uses with a prohibition covering any AI system, while retaining the program’s jurisdictional limits and transaction exceptions. The proposed definitions expressly include agent harnesses, the software around a model that enables it to act autonomously. Treasury issued the proposal to implement the COINS Act, enacted in December 2025.

Under the 2024 rules, effective since January 2025, some AI investments are prohibited and others require notification. Prohibitions include systems intended for military, government-intelligence or mass-surveillance uses, and systems trained above 1025 computational operations, with a lower threshold for training primarily on biological sequence data. Other covered investments require notification above 1023 operations or for specified uses such as cybersecurity and robotic control. The proposal would eliminate the AI notification category and put development of any AI system into the prohibited-technology category.

Treasury argues that training compute has become an inadequate basis for separating advanced models from others. Developers can improve capability through synthetic data, more efficient algorithms, post-training methods and additional computation when a model is used. Treasury also says intended uses are harder to establish: a general-purpose model can support national-security applications that its developer has neither evaluated nor advertised. Open models with fewer safeguards can be incorporated into systems beyond their intended purposes. The department says a categorical approach would both protect national security and spare investors from having to determine a foreign developer’s technical capabilities or training thresholds.

The proposal calls AI systems “super intelligence” systems, or SI, following the September 29 terminology order. The definition does not require superhuman performance. Section 851.232 retains the familiar functions of prediction, recommendation and decision-making, adds content generation, and includes software, hardware and tools that operate using such systems. It expressly names agent harnesses and physical systems such as robots and autonomous vehicles. The term therefore covers ordinary model-based products as well as more capable agents.

Under section 851.226(h), a covered foreign person or joint venture building on a third-party model would count as developing an AI system when it improves or expands that model’s capabilities. Treasury gives a harness as its example: software that runs repeated actions autonomously, handles memory and context, or supports intermediate reasoning and correction before presenting an answer. Off-the-shelf systems used strictly for internal, non-commercial purposes would not count as development on that basis alone. The provision includes configuration and fine-tuning for such uses, with sale or licensing given as contrary examples.

Treasury’s 2024 definition already included applications and tools operating on AI models. Its accompanying notes tied development using a third-party model to activities such as design or substantive modification of that model; the preamble used removal of safeguards as an example. The proposal instead describes improving or extending capability through a harness. Treasury characterizes the new language as generally corresponding to the earlier notes, while expressly describing the functions that an added software layer can perform.

The proposal defines covered foreign persons through ties such as incorporation, principal place of business, ownership or control involving a country of concern. The country list would retain China, including Hong Kong and Macau, and add Cuba, Iran, North Korea and Russia. It also names Venezuela under the Maduro regime; Treasury says that condition no longer applies following U.S. recognition of Delcy Rodríguez. These jurisdictional requirements determine which foreign developers fall within the investment program.

Covered transactions include equity acquisitions, debt with equity-like rights, joint ventures, establishment of new operations and certain investments in foreign funds. Exceptions remain for publicly traded securities and some fund investments, subject to conditions including limits on additional governance rights. The proposed $50,000 exception applies only to transactions that would otherwise require notification, so it would not exempt prohibited AI investments. A separate process would allow requests for a national-interest exemption.

In an October 23 client alert, Ropes & Gray emphasizes the move to a categorical AI prohibition and the breadth of the harness and third-party-development provisions. Its lawyers also note that the proposal supplies no list of covered foreign persons against which investors could simply check names. Treasury specifically seeks comment on both the AI definition and the consequences of prohibiting investment across AI development. Comments are due December 7, 2026. The proposed changes are not binding now: Treasury’s existing rules remain in force until a final rule takes effect.
