Grok and GPT pick opposite parties in Convovo’s voting test

TechCrunch reports strong partisan differences in forced API recommendations. Its follow-up changes both search access and candidate information, complicating the explanation.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 October 2026 725 words · ~4 min

Grok 4.7 and GPT-6.1 Sol gave overwhelmingly different partisan recommendations when pressed to choose candidates in the US midterms, Rebecca Bellan reported in TechCrunch on October 24. Among answers naming a major-party candidate, Grok selected the Republican 92% of the time and GPT selected the Democrat 97%. The test used the models' developer APIs; their consumer apps initially refused the same request. TechCrunch commissioned a follow-up that exposed further differences in how the models responded when search and the prompt changed.

The AI Ballot 2026 study comes from Convovo, the conversational-AI research studio led by Tapan Parikh. On October 21, the researchers used OpenAI's and xAI's Responses APIs with each company's web search enabled, asking whom to vote for in each race and demanding one candidate without hedging. They supplied no system prompt or user history. Each model answered three times per race, at default settings from New York. The headline analysis covers 479 House, Senate and governor races: 477 pair a Democrat with a Republican, while two Senate contests pair Republicans with independents. The full interactive includes another 27 contests.

GPT chose Democrats in 1,082 of its 1,111 major-party answers, while Grok chose Republicans in 952 of 1,038. Each produced 1,437 answers in those races: GPT declined 313 times and Grok 375, with the remaining answers selecting independents or third-party candidates. Convovo coded answers from their opening sentence and checked unclear cases by hand. For race-level verdicts, refusals counted as abstentions: the candidate with the most selections won, ties were marked split, and a race counted as declined only when all three answers declined.

Bellan also follows the differences in search topics and sources. Parikh told her GPT searched more often for health care and abortion positions, while Grok focused on immigration, taxes, crime and energy. Grok generally searched for both nominees; GPT sometimes ignored the Republican. Grok's searches regularly returned Ballotpedia alongside conservative sources: iVoterGuide appeared in 39% of answers and Turning Point Action in 23%. GPT's searches returned Wikipedia in 93% of answers, alongside the Associated Press, Axios and Reddit. These are overlapping shares of answers whose searches returned a site, rather than proportions of material read or cited. TechCrunch notes OpenAI's content deals with several returned publishers, without establishing that those deals caused the recommendations.

At TechCrunch's request, Parikh reran 30 races with web search disabled and the candidates explicitly named. GPT selected 24 Democrats and one Republican, declining five times. Grok declined in 27 races and selected Democrats in the other three. This changes two things at once: access to search and the information supplied in the prompt. It therefore cannot isolate search as the cause of Grok's Republican choices in the original test.

Convovo's October 23 repository follow-up extended the no-search test to all 479 races, with variants that did and did not name the candidates. GPT selected Democrats in 387 races when names were supplied, against 180 without them. Grok declined in 441 races with names and 298 without. Naming candidates substantially changed the pattern while search remained disabled, giving the smaller TechCrunch comparison an additional qualification.

Parikh interprets GPT's persistence as evidence that its preference is largely learned in training, and Grok's change as evidence that search terms and sources drive its result. The tests do not separate training, provider instructions and search infrastructure. Convovo also reports roughly 1,240 input tokens in every Grok call that it neither supplied nor could inspect, including when tools were disabled. The tokens' contents are unknown; their presence does not establish that xAI supplied political instructions. Nor does omitting a user identity separate a model's default political preference from its tendency to accommodate the preferences it infers an unspecified user might hold.

OpenAI's response to TechCrunch emphasizes the interface distinction: the company says ChatGPT aims for political objectivity and that a developer-API test explicitly demanding an endorsement does not represent the app experience. Convovo's subsequent app checks show how sensitive that experience can be to framing. The ordinary prompt produced no ChatGPT endorsement in 18 calls across six races; asking it to act as an editorial board produced Democratic endorsements in 17 of 18. These were small exploratory checks, identified as such in the repository. They establish examples of endorsements under altered framing, while leaving their frequency in ordinary election conversations unmeasured. xAI had not responded to TechCrunch by publication.
