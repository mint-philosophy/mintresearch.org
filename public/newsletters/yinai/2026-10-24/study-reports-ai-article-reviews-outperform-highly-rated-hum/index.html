AI reviews beat paid experts in King and Worthington’s feedback study

Editors preferred the AI review in 26 of 30 judgments, drawn from the same ten manuscripts. A wider set of model-judged comparisons supports the result, but journal decisions and improved papers remain untested.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 October 2026 1044 words · ~5 min

Three former journal editors preferred AI-written manuscript reviews in 26 of 30 comparisons with paid human experts. That is the human check behind Gary King and Steven Worthington's AI Article Reviews Dominate Human Peer Reviews at Their Best: Evidence and Policy Choices, an author-hosted working paper dated October 22 on King's Harvard website. The authors argue that frontier AI can outperform even highly rated reviewers at giving authors constructive feedback. Its larger comparisons, judged by another AI model, favour the machine still more strongly. The distinction between feedback and deciding what deserves publication matters: the experiment measured preferences for reviews, without testing publication decisions or whether authors produced better papers.

The benchmark was rapidPeer, a service developed at Harvard's Institute for Quantitative Social Science. It pays outside experts to give faculty drafts substantive, double-blind reviews before journal submission. King and Worthington used all eligible submissions from 2019 to 2026: 124 manuscripts and 134 reviews, with ten manuscripts reviewed twice. The instructions asked for constructive criticism, not a judgment about journal suitability. Of 74 authors who answered a survey, 88% rated their reviews high or very high quality. That supports the choice of a valued comparison service, although it cannot establish that these reviewers represent the best human review across science.

Claude Opus 4.8, running at maximum reasoning effort, reviewed every manuscript under two conditions. One used the human reviewers' exact instructions without tools; the other used a purpose-built prompt and web search. The authors say the latter prompt was written before judging and never optimized against the scores. GPT-5.5, from a different model family, compared each AI review with its human counterpart in both presentation orders. A win required the same preference both ways; reversals counted as undecided. Human reviewers could consult supplementary files, supplied with about 27% of submissions; the AI reviewer and manuscript-based judges received the manuscript alone.

The appendix resolves an inconsistency in the headline figure. Under the matched instructions, AI won all 134 comparisons. Under the tuned instructions discussed in the main text, it won 132, with two undecided; Figure 1 nevertheless prints 134/134. The editor check was smaller than its 30 judgments might suggest: each of three former editors assessed the same ten pairs, choosing AI eight, eight and ten times respectively. They saw the reviews without the manuscripts and were not told one was AI-generated. Their judgments therefore repeat the same ten cases, rather than supplying evidence from 30 independent manuscripts.

The authors took several plausible explanations for the preference seriously. AI won all 44 comparisons where the reviews were similar in length. Rewriting human reviews into AI style did not improve their scores. Giving the judge the manuscripts and asking it to check claims left AI preferred in every decided pair, with a few unresolved comparisons. A further replication changed the prompt and rubric and used both Claude and GPT models as reviewers and judges: AI won 94% to 97% of 416 comparisons. These checks make the result harder to explain by presentation alone, while leaving most assessments dependent on model judgments.

Accuracy received a separate audit. A blinded model judge flagged 5.5% of AI claim assessments and 15.9% of human assessments as contradicted by the manuscripts. This was a specific definition of error: unverifiable claims remained in the denominator without counting as wrong. A human checked 90 claims, including every AI claim flagged as erroneous, and agreed with 87 assessments; the three disagreements concerned correct AI claims wrongly marked as errors. More than a third of AI reviews still contained at least one flagged error, against almost two-thirds of human reviews. In a controlled test, the judge detected all 258 deliberately inserted false statements without flagging their accurate counterparts, supporting its ability to catch explicit contradictions. All 651 AI citations carrying DOI or arXiv identifiers referred to real works. That check establishes existence, not whether every citation supports its accompanying argument.

The paper's policy proposals extend beyond these measured outcomes. Authors could run a journal's published reviewing prompt before submission, then send the resulting review with a letter explaining disagreements. An editor could commission a second AI review with a private prompt and call human referees for difficult cases, retaining the publication decision. King and Worthington identify problems with their own proposal: authors can keep revising until the public prompt approves, reducing the review's information for editors, while a shared reviewing system may push scientific work toward greater conformity. The study did not test these arrangements. Its AI reviews were never delivered to the manuscript authors.

This distinction between useful criticism and editorial authority structures the substantive disagreement. In Paired Ends, Stephen Turner says the findings make his earlier proposal to reserve AI for quality control look too conservative about feedback. He recommends trying model reviews before submission but keeps decisions about what a field should publish with an accountable person. Kent Anderson's objection in The Geyser starts from selection as peer review's central function: rapidPeer reviewers were expressly not asked to make that judgment. The paper acknowledges the distinction; the unresolved question is how far a measured advantage in feedback warrants changing journal refereeing.

Turner also posted an AI-generated critique he commissioned, which recommends major revision and questions the reliance on AI judging and a small editor panel. Its warning about the human sample is warranted, but the study also includes the second-family replication and manuscript checks. Nor does the paper's reported 87% agreement between editors and the AI judge independently validate correctness: because the AI judge always preferred AI in that sample, agreement reproduces the editors' own preference rate. A larger human assessment grounded in the manuscripts would test something the ten-pair, reviews-only comparison leaves open.

The institutional concerns go beyond whether a model can produce a convincing report. Nicolai Foss accepts the result as evidence that analytical criticism can be automated, making editorial judgment more consequential. Paul Musgrave questions the paper's claims but also argues that cheap machine criticism could undermine journals' role and deprive researchers of the training that reviewing provides. A separate study by Bastien Le Guellec and colleagues tested four models on 200 published articles. They detected between 17% and 60.5% of deliberately planted flaws, and describing a manuscript as already revised made all four models more lenient.
