Zvi Mowshowitz on why an eleven-week head start was not enough

Mowshowitz reads PHP2Shell as evidence that finding flaws first cannot secure deployed systems quickly enough. His essay argues for broader vetted defender access, more patching capacity and pressure on operators to apply repairs.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 24 October 2026 1019 words · ~5 min

In his October 23 essay “The Hackening”, Zvi Mowshowitz argues that PHP2Shell weakens the case for AI giving defenders a reliable advantage. Finding vulnerabilities first helps, but protection depends on repairing software and getting fixes onto exposed systems before attackers exploit them. His account develops the discovery-versus-deployment problem in our October 22 report, through a dispute about what the episode demonstrates and proposals for broader vetted access, more patching capacity and pressure on slow-moving operators.

Mowshowitz builds his argument around the chronology covered October 21. Calif dates its discovery to August 1 and its first notification to PHP to August 11. Amazon observed an attempted attack against its MadPot honeypots on October 17; patches and public disclosure followed October 21. The eleven-week interval runs from discovery to that attempt. Amazon says it is unclear whether the pre-release attempts would have worked on real systems. Mowshowitz’s concern is that even this substantial lead failed to get patches released before attempts began.

On X, Nate Soares argued that the flaws surfacing six months after Mythos counted against the expectation that defense would outpace offense. Anthropic’s Project Glasswing had given selected organizations access to Mythos Preview for defensive security work. In a reply, Soares emphasized the program’s failure to prevent a critical vulnerability persisting. roon answered that defenders using Daybreak had found the flaws: the lesson was that Glasswing needed greater reach or capacity. He also questioned whether the earlier Mythos matched the later models used in the discovery.

Stella Biderman called Soares’s post disinformation, citing discovery by defensive operations and the absence of evidence of large-scale exploitation. Mowshowitz accepts that defenders finding the bugs first improved the outcome, but sides with Soares on the broader inference. Discovery used a model behind the contemporary frontier, he reasons, so attackers could plausibly have found the flaws first. Combined with the long repair interval, that makes the outcome less reassuring to him than to Biderman. He objects to treating Soares’s interpretation as disinformation.

Calif’s Ryan Austin says the initial discovery used generally available models and no special harness; his knowledge of PHP’s vulnerability history guided the investigation. Calif participated in OpenAI’s Patch the Planet initiative, but participation does not establish that the discovery required a private model. The PHP Foundation says PHP had previously been scanned with Mythos through Alpha-Omega, without specifying whether that scan covered the relevant paths.

Vitalik Buterin’s response, which Mowshowitz quotes, distinguishes a potentially favorable eventual balance from a difficult transition. Racing to find bugs in software already deployed does not guarantee defenders an advantage. Buterin wants formal verification, mathematical checks of specified software properties, and a smaller trusted computing base: less code that must behave correctly for the system to remain secure. He remains receptive to slowing or pausing AI development and calls for more than 1% of GDP toward hardening cyber, biological and physical systems. His position requires changing how systems are built, alongside improving bug discovery.

The PHP Foundation’s postmortem explains the repair difficulties. Proposed fixes threatened compatibility, introduced denial-of-service risks or required changes across third-party extensions. A security team operating by consensus struggled to choose, and few developers understood the engine well enough to resolve the trade-offs. Work stalled and restarted over months. The Foundation described a structural failure and said it was moving toward majority decisions.

Mowshowitz emphasizes that publishing a fix gives attackers information about vulnerable systems while administrators still need to install it. Even successful coordination among discoverers and maintainers therefore leaves a second race over deployment. He notes that PHP supplied limited backports for obsolete branches, while warning against continuing to rely on them. His concern encompasses permissions, manual work and systems that are difficult to update: better models cannot by themselves ensure that a repair reaches every installation.

In the essay’s final section, Mowshowitz takes up Dean Ball’s framing of the policy choice between rapidly distributing dual-use capabilities and restricting them to vetted users. He rejects the argument that eventual proliferation justifies accelerating it now. Yet he thinks access for legitimate defenders is too restrictive. Providers must balance the benefit of equipping more defenders against the risk of trusting someone malicious, even while controlling only part of the wider market.

Mowshowitz challenges a threat model in which one rogue actor briefly gains access to a powerful model and immediately unleashes disaster. He expects danger to accumulate as capable users expand their activity. That makes monitoring, classifiers, identity checks and legal accountability useful ways to manage broader defensive access. Citing Sir Lemmings’s account of receiving Daybreak access within fifteen minutes, he suggests that awareness can also be a constraint: providers should actively offer access to people who need it. His proposal combines easier access with supervision, while retaining restrictions on indiscriminate distribution.

Mowshowitz warns that these access arrangements will accomplish little if openly available models improve faster than organizations can patch. He calls for much greater resources for repairs and proposes white-hat probes backed by mandatory large bounties to push operators to act; he does not specify who would administer that system. Greg Brockman’s October 21 account identifies a related problem: the coordinated response remained slow and manual. Brockman floated authorized defenders scanning vulnerable hosts and, in extreme cases, remotely patching them.

In the essay’s discussion of diffusion, Mowshowitz develops another argument from Ball: a few quiet days after an open model’s release cannot establish that its cyber risks were exaggerated. People who expect institutions and slow adoption to delay AI’s effects should allow for similar delays in abuse. Learning to use the tools takes time and iteration; early incidents can precede much wider uptake.

Mowshowitz argues that defenders can buy time by imposing friction. He suspects cheap, fast DeepSeek models helped accelerate recent activity, while describing much of that activity as opportunistic and poorly resourced. On that account, making attacks and access to capable models more troublesome can still reduce harm while repairs proceed. He expects such obstacles to lose effectiveness as stronger attackers scale. For now, he argues, defenders should use those temporary advantages and adopt precautions they can sustain through a prolonged security problem.
