ROSS ruling protects Westlaw headnotes and leaves generative AI questions open

The opinion, filed September 29 and made public September 30, rejected copying for a competing legal search engine. Its reasoning and later use in the Anthropic litigation have divided lawyers on both sides of the AI copyright debate.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 October 2026 1436 words · ~7 min

Copying Westlaw headnotes to train a competing legal search engine was not fair use, a unanimous Third Circuit panel held in Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence. Judge Tamika Montgomery-Reeves, joined by Judges L. Felipe Restrepo and Emil J. Bove III, called it “no more than an ordinary copyright case”. The court filed the opinion under seal and entered judgment on September 29, then released the opinion the next day after both sides agreed that nothing needed redacting. Jackson Healy reported the decision for Courthouse News Service on September 30. The panel affirmed the February 2025 ruling of Judge Stephanos Bibas, sitting by designation in the District of Delaware. Lawyers at Ballard Spahr call it the first federal appellate decision on fair use in AI training.

ROSS built a search engine that answered plain-language legal questions with passages from about ten million uncopyrighted judicial opinions; it returned existing text and generated none of its own. According to Bibas’s 2025 opinion, ROSS asked to license Westlaw’s content and was refused as a competitor. It then hired LegalEase Solutions, which with a subcontractor wrote about 25,000 training memos, each pairing a legal question with four to six opinion passages graded great, good, topical or irrelevant. The drafters built many of the questions from Westlaw headnotes, the short summaries of points of law that Thomson Reuters editors place above each opinion, because headnotes made the work easy. The judgment covers 2,243 headnotes whose memo questions tracked the headnote text so closely, and the opinions so loosely, that no reasonable juror could doubt the copying. ROSS advertised against Westlaw at comparable prices and won over some law firms, then shut its platform on January 31, 2021, saying the lawsuit had cut off new financing.

On originality, the panel applied Feist, the Supreme Court’s 1991 decision requiring only minimal creativity for copyright. Westlaw’s editors choose which points of law deserve a headnote and word each one to stand alone, within 800 characters where possible, while staying faithful to the opinion. The court held those choices sufficient, citing Callaghan v. Myers, an 1888 Supreme Court decision that let a court reporter copyright headnotes he wrote. To ROSS’s warning that copyright in headnotes would give Thomson Reuters a monopoly over the law, the court replied: “Headnotes are not law; judicial opinions are.” ROSS also argued merger, the doctrine that denies copyright when an idea can be expressed in only a few ways. The panel rejected it, citing a 2019 Third Circuit case that found a banana costume could take many designs and an amicus brief from RELX, owner of rival LexisNexis, showing that Lexis writes different headnotes for the same opinions.

Courts weigh four fair-use factors: the purpose of the use, the nature of the work, the amount taken and the effect on the market. On purpose, the court found ROSS’s use commercial and gave the training step little weight. Training an AI program on the headnotes arguably made the use slightly different, Montgomery-Reeves wrote, but both companies used them to build legal-research platforms, leaving ROSS’s use “minimally transformative, at best”. She distinguished the Second Circuit’s 2015 Google Books decision, where search could lead readers to buy books, because ROSS “aims to replace Westlaw”. ROSS also relied on intermediate-copying cases, in which courts excused copies made on the way to building something new, among them the Ninth Circuit’s Sega decision and the Supreme Court’s Google v. Oracle. The panel read them as cases where copying computer code was necessary to reach its unprotected functional parts, whereas ROSS had the free opinions and chose headnotes for convenience. “Unlike necessity, ease is not a justification for copying,” the court wrote. In a footnote it found that ROSS “at times acted in bad faith”, citing among other things an employee who got into Westlaw on a student account while hiding that he worked for a competitor.

The second factor tilted slightly toward ROSS because the headnotes were published and mostly factual. On the third, the amount taken, the panel disagreed with Bibas, who had given that factor to ROSS because its users never saw a headnote. The appeals court looked instead at what ROSS copied: each headnote is a separate work, so “for each headnote taken, ROSS copied an entire work”, and the copying was unnecessary because the opinions were free. The court dismissed ROSS’s point that it had used only 0.08 percent of Westlaw’s 28 million headnotes. On market effect, the court found that ROSS reduced the headnotes’ value as a reason to subscribe to Westlaw, threatened Westlaw in the legal-research market and took Thomson Reuters’s chance to license headnotes as AI training data. In the court’s words, “the market for licensing headnotes as text to train AI is rapidly developing”, and Thomson Reuters already trains its own AI search tools on them. ROSS’s claim that its copying widened public access failed because the opinions were already free and ROSS charged Westlaw-level prices, and its warnings about AI development and national security came without evidence.

In footnote 7, the court took up the Justice Department’s September 1 statement of interest in the OpenAI copyright litigation, which argues that training large language models on copyrighted text is fair use. ROSS had put that filing before the panel on September 4, calling the government’s position strongly supportive, and Thomson Reuters replied on September 9 that the statement “concerns large language models, which are not at issue here”. The court agreed. The department had relied on Bartz v. Anthropic to argue that training a model that writes original responses is transformative, while “ROSS’s AI platform cannot generate original expression” and was trained to produce a commercial substitute for Westlaw. The court added that the department knows how to assert its interests and “notably did not do so here”. Judge William Alsup had made the same distinction in his June 2025 Bartz ruling, describing ROSS’s product as a competing tool for finding court opinions and therefore not transformative.

In his October 4 analysis, Aaron Moss of Copyright Lately observes that both sides of the AI copyright fight have claimed the ruling, each by emphasizing a different section. Maria Pallante of the Association of American Publishers praised its recognition of a licensing market for AI training, and Mary Rasenberger of the Authors Guild said it “vindicates copyright law”, Publishers Weekly reports. Adam Eisgrau of Chamber of Progress, which backed ROSS as an amicus, wrote that the opinion tacitly confirms that highly transformative training of multipurpose generative models is likely fair use. Stanford’s Mark Lemley wrote on October 1 that the opinion “mostly does no harm” and leaves AI training in general undecided. His 2021 article with Bryan Casey, “Fair Learning”, argued that training on a work to learn its unprotectable elements should be presumptively fair unless the use competes directly in the owner’s main market.

In an October 2 response, Dave Hansen of Authors Alliance, which also filed a brief for ROSS, argues that the opinion “hollows out the merger doctrine”, since a headnote must restate a point of law accurately and briefly, and that its necessity rule would cast doubt on research built on annotated collections. He and Moss both point out that the opinion offers no record support for its statement about a fast-developing licensing market. Moss adds that the panel never addressed the measure Bibas had used, how much of a work reaches the public, and that “footnote 7 hedges its bets”: AI companies will stress that ROSS could not generate new expression, plaintiffs that it was built as a substitute. “The Third Circuit distinguished those cases. It didn’t decide them,” he writes. A BakerHostetler analysis argues that the court demanded strict necessity, where Alsup in Bartz accepted copying that was reasonably necessary.

The ruling binds federal courts in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and the Virgin Islands. On September 30, the day it became public, the music publishers suing Anthropic over song lyrics filed it with Judge Eumi K. Lee in San Jose, telling her it addresses the fair-use issues in summary-judgment motions set for hearing on October 21. ROSS’s lawyer, Yar Chaikovsky of White & Case, told Courthouse News that the company disagrees with the decision: “We intend to seek review by the Supreme Court”. Moss doubts the case suits a sweeping ruling, since it came up on interlocutory appeal, damages remain open, ROSS no longer operates and its AI never generated text. The Delaware court still has to resolve claims over other headnotes, questions of copyright expiry and damages.
