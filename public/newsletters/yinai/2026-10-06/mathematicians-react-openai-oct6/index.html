Mathematicians rank OpenAI’s results and argue over their place in history

Alpöge, Kontorovich and Strogatz offered sweeping assessments, while Litt challenged the historical comparison. An independent exact-overlaps paper details the work done by its human authors and GPT-6 Astra.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 October 2026 926 words · ~5 min

Levent Alpöge called OpenAI’s October 6 release “the most significant moment in mathematical history”, singling out the quasi-Riemann hypothesis and exclusion of Siegel zeros. Alex Kontorovich likened the zeta result to an immediate Fields Medal if a human had produced it, and Steven Strogatz compared the matrix-multiplication advance to Bob Beamon’s long jump. Those assessments came alongside arguments about historical scale, independent discovery, and the work required to turn the manuscripts into mathematics that people understand.

A historical moment, and a disagreement about what it measures

Alpöge’s follow-up compared the development with the invention of proof, zero and the computer. He treated the release as a measurement of progress accumulated over roughly ten years, rather than an advance that appeared in a single day. Daniel Litt strongly disagreed with calling it the most significant day except as an indicator of what was coming, suggesting that an earlier model release might then be the relevant event. Alpöge accepted the distinction between a measurement and the underlying development.

In an October 7 post, Gil Kalai called the release an amazing milestone and connected many of its problems to topics discussed on his blog over two decades. He expected human mathematicians to spend months verifying and absorbing the results, including scrutiny of formalized claims. His response paired enthusiasm with a concrete programme of reading: how the new manuscripts connect to existing conjectures, techniques and lectures.

The specific judgments were substantial. Kontorovich’s reaction concerned a fixed zero-free strip for zeta, with the same width at every height, rather than a proof of the full Riemann hypothesis. Strogatz’s comparison concerned lowering the matrix-multiplication exponent from about 2.37 to 2.25. These are assessments of the reported theorems’ significance, rather than independent checks of their proofs.

What specialists found in the papers

Litt welcomed the claimed negative solution to Hilbert’s tenth problem over the rationals, which concerns whether an algorithm can decide if a polynomial equation has rational solutions. He also noticed a manuscript citing an earlier work he considered wrong, while observing that the new argument did not appear to depend meaningfully on it. His comments show why individual results need individual reading: a mistaken citation can require correction without deciding the validity of the theorem that cites it.

Andrew Sutherland of MIT focused on how the results were obtained. He told Scientific American that claims of solving problems with a single prompt to a single agent should remain unverified until the model is released and others can replicate them. “We should ask for receipts,” he said. The same report quoted Litt defending publication of answers to mathematical questions and reported OpenAI’s acknowledgment that its own mathematicians did not yet understand many of the results.

An independent result, with the machine’s contribution stated

Samuel Kittle and Constantin Kogler’s paper on the exact-overlaps conjecture for self-similar measures on the real line supplies a concrete case of parallel discovery. Their manuscript says OpenAI claims to have found its proof on September 24; they first obtained theirs on September 27. OpenAI’s version became public on October 6, and theirs was made widely available on October 7. They identify the same theorem in OpenAI’s collection while declining to comment further on that proof’s mathematical content.

The authors’ machine-contribution statement credits GPT-6 Astra with the first proof. Kogler had first asked it for simpler proofs of existing results, then tried the conjecture. Kittle subsequently found a new proof of the deduction connecting a key intermediate theorem to the main result, while Kogler rewrote the remaining material. They distinguish sections written by the authors from a model-written section they rewrote, disclose model assistance with proofreading, and provide a transcript and initial drafts. They take responsibility for correctness and offer to explain the work in talks and answer questions.

Understanding, access and the pace of release

Hours before OpenAI’s announcement, Terence Tao posted a broader argument about autonomous problem-solving without naming the company. He worried that solutions were “being harvested at large scale in an unsustainable fashion”, while the exposition, seminars, follow-up work and exchange needed to understand them lagged behind. He also described a risk that researchers would withhold interesting questions for fear of being scooped. The thread proposed giving greater weight to explanation, community and new questions, activities in which AI could also help.

Jeremy Avigad’s October 5 guest essay on Tao’s blog likewise preceded the release. It argued that mathematicians still choose their questions and standards of understanding, and can pursue harder problems, develop larger conceptual frameworks and use AI to support those aims. The essay’s subject was the profession’s future, not a verdict on the October 6 manuscripts.

The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence’s October 6 statement explicitly separated its advisory role from endorsement of OpenAI’s process or judgment of the results’ importance. Its September 29 recommendations had asked labs to stop testing advanced mathematical problems on proprietary models and to disclose, for each released result, the model, prompt, compute and other provenance. Peter Woit’s October 6 post highlighted the tension between that request and OpenAI’s statement that it had drawn on the group’s advice.

Ben Antieau announced Hexagon, a nonprofit repository for AI-assisted mathematics, on October 6. Its invitation to large labs offered one route toward community infrastructure for the incoming work. Jordan Ellenberg, meanwhile, told Quanta that mathematicians would find uses for the results over time: “There’s no hurry and it will all get done.” The question of pace extends from producing proofs to checking them, teaching them and deciding what to investigate next.
