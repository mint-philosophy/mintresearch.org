OpenAI’s Sarah Yager on military AI and the laws of war

OpenAI's human rights lead described expert testing of model responses about the laws of war. Her account raises separate questions about model behavior, contract limits and responsibility for military decisions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 October 2026 1175 words · ~6 min

Sarah Yager, OpenAI's first human rights and responsible deployment lead, told the ScaleUp:AI conference in New York on October 6 that what militaries will do with AI “keeps me up at night,” Fortune's Emily Forlini reports. Her concerns include targeting and autonomous weapons. Yager said she brought international experts to keyboards to test models with prompts about high-stakes engagements, then worked with engineers to change model responses. The account describes an expert exercise and subsequent changes; it does not establish compliance by deployed models or military operations with the laws of war.

Yager joined in June after a career focused on civilian protection. Her conference biography identifies her as a former Human Rights Watch Washington director, the first senior human rights advisor in the Joint Chiefs chairman's office, and a leader of the Center for Civilians in Conflict for nearly a decade. In an April op-ed for the Los Angeles Times, she criticized the dismantling of US military protections for civilians while calling for answers about civilian casualties in Iran.

Fortune says OpenAI approached Yager after its February 27 agreement to deploy models in classified Pentagon environments. The previous day, Anthropic had rejected terms allowing any lawful use without exceptions for mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. Yager initially worried that OpenAI wanted a reputational endorsement, described by moderator Hilary Gosher as a “branding exercise.” Before accepting, she spoke with employees, including younger staff concerned about the technology they were creating and who controlled it. Gosher is a managing director at Insight Partners, which runs the conference and has invested in OpenAI.

The April 29 job posting called for bringing human rights expertise into red teaming, convening specialists in armed conflict and civilian protection, and using their input to test capabilities and deployment scenarios. OpenAI already used outside specialists to probe harmful behavior, as Lama Ahmad and colleagues described in a 2025 white paper. Other researchers have tested models against humanitarian law: John Mavi and colleagues found that most of eight models refused explicitly unlawful prompts, with uneven clarity and consistency; Toby Drinkall reported that three models targeted civilian objects in simulated crises. These studies tested different behaviors and settings. Yager's claim that her exercise was unprecedented is not independently established by Fortune's account.

The legal baseline extends beyond a model's answers about the Geneva Conventions. The principles of distinction, proportionality and precautions in attack were codified notably in Additional Protocol I of 1977. They require distinguishing military objectives from civilians and civilian objects, avoiding attacks expected to cause excessive incidental civilian harm, and taking feasible precautions. Although the United States has not ratified that protocol, those three principles also exist in customary international humanitarian law, the International Committee of the Red Cross explains. Non-ratification does not remove those customary duties.

In its 2025 submission to the UN Secretary-General on military AI, the ICRC emphasizes that humans must make legal determinations and remain accountable for attacks they plan, decide upon or execute. It warns that reliance on decision-support output alone can reduce human involvement to rubber-stamping. The concern includes the quality of the information, the operator's ability to question it and the circumstances of its use.

OpenAI's account of its Pentagon agreement lists three company red lines: mass domestic surveillance, directing autonomous weapons, and high-stakes automated decisions. The original contract excerpt it published permits use for all lawful purposes and prohibits independent direction of autonomous weapons where law, regulation or department policy requires human control, citing the 2023 version of Defense Department Directive 3000.09. That conditional language differs from a blanket contractual prohibition on autonomous weapons. OpenAI's March 2 update says it added an express bar on intentional domestic surveillance of US persons and nationals, including through commercially acquired personal information, and that intelligence agencies such as the NSA need a separate agreement.

The company's National Security Principles also prohibit facilitating war crimes and other gross violations of human rights or humanitarian law. They allow targeting work subject to scrutiny, with heightened scrutiny when substantial parts of targeting are automated. OpenAI defines appropriate human judgment as including decisions about deployment conditions, target types, duration and geography; it does not require a person to decide every individual system action. These are the company's stated commitments and interpretation of human judgment.

Fortune places Yager's work alongside CNN's September 18 investigation of AI-assisted intelligence that nearly sent US troops aboard a Chinese ship. During the Iran war in the spring, CNN reported, a special operations command analyst used a chatbot to interpret information about a Chinese cargo ship's manifest, then used AI to turn its answer into an intelligence report falsely identifying components of a nuclear weapons program. US forces prepared to intercept and board the ship before officials traced the claim to AI. CNN could not determine whether the chatbot was commercial or government-provided. The episode concerns a reported failure in an intelligence process, rather than evidence about the effects of Yager's exercise.

Sarah Shoker, formerly OpenAI's geopolitics team lead, and Utrecht University's Jessica Dorsey argued in an August Opinio Juris essay that company restrictions focused too narrowly on fully autonomous weapons while existing tools help select and prioritize targets. They criticized release evaluations for omitting humanitarian law and military decision support, and called for assessing the people, procedures and command structures surrounding models. That is a broader evaluation problem than checking whether a chatbot can recognize an explicitly unlawful request.

Yager also argued that human rights should supply a baseline for AI companies' moral decisions. Fortune and the conference organizer's account render her wording differently, but both quote her saying, “I do not want an AI company telling me what is good.” In “Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights” (2019), Eileen Donahoe and Megan MacDuffee Metzger similarly argued that established human rights norms offer broader legitimacy than principles developed by individual companies. The UN's Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights connect companies' responsibilities to international humanitarian law during armed conflict.

She remained skeptical of rights for AI agents, while acknowledging questions about how agents fit into legal, labor and tax structures. Abeba Birhane and Jelle van Dijk's 2020 paper on robot rights likewise argued for concentrating on human welfare and existing harms. Yager criticized the representation at the September 29 White House AI gathering; all six signatories of its voluntary safety accord, from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia, were men. Her concern was that affected communities were absent from the picture.

Fortune reports that Yager is helping the Government Accountability Office and an Office of Inspector General develop tools to track government AI use. OpenAI's August 18 oversight initiative pledged $5 million in training, support and credits, plus pilots letting authorized reviewers examine inputs, outputs and tool use behind government decisions. It named no participating bodies. On September 19, Senators Mark Warner, Jack Reed and Chris Coons requested an inspectors general investigation into errors in AI-enabled targeting, citing the Chinese ship and the February strike on a school in Minab, Iran.
