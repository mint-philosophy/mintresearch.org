OpenAI manuscripts claim a proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis and new multiplication bounds

A 199-page manuscript excludes zeta and Dirichlet L-function zeros to the right of 7/8. A public outside check covers the zeta statement; other papers claim advances in multiplication and Kakeya geometry.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 6 October 2026 998 words · ~5 min

OpenAI’s 199-page manuscript dated September 30 claims a fixed zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function and every Dirichlet L-function: no zero has real part greater than 7/8. Released on October 6, the result would establish the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. The Riemann hypothesis itself remains open. An outside GitHub Actions run subsequently reported a successful Comparator check of the zeta statement. Other manuscripts claim major advances in matrix multiplication, integer multiplication and Kakeya geometry.

The release README says OpenAI posed about 4,000 problems to an unnamed internal model and selected results meeting its significance threshold. An OpenAI spokesperson told Scientific American that almost every result came from one prompt to one agent, though some may have required multiple attempts. The README identifies the zeta work and a special case of the Hodge conjecture as exceptions to its standard procedure.

The zeta function connects prime numbers with complex analysis. A complex number has two coordinates, a horizontal real part and a vertical imaginary part; a zero is an input where the function equals zero. The Riemann hypothesis places every nontrivial zeta zero on the vertical line at 1/2. The new theorem rules out zeros anywhere to the right of 7/8, at every height. The boundary itself is excluded from the theorem. The usual pole at 1, where the function becomes unbounded, is allowed.

Classical zero-free regions near the line at 1 grow narrower as height increases; for Dirichlet L-functions their width also depends on the arithmetic complexity of the character defining the function. A fixed boundary below 1 therefore changes what can be guaranteed across the entire infinite strip. By the functional equation’s symmetry, OpenAI’s result would confine the nontrivial zeta zeros to the closed strip from 1/8 to 7/8. It leaves room for zeros away from the Riemann hypothesis’s central line.

Dirichlet L-functions attach periodic arithmetic weights to the same kind of series, allowing mathematicians to study primes in residue classes, such as numbers congruent to 1 modulo 4. The theorem also covers finite-order Hecke L-functions over the number field Q(√−3). These enter the proof’s arithmetic transformations. The October 5 companion derives an effective error of order x11/12 log x for counting primes in arithmetic progressions, uniformly for moduli up to x. The September 30 manuscript also derives Vinogradov’s conjecture on the least quadratic nonresidue, concerning how early an integer that is not a square modulo a prime must occur.

The broader theorem excludes exceptional Landau–Siegel zeros sufficiently close to 1. A separate October 1 manuscript establishes a uniform logarithmic gap: for a real zero β of a primitive real Dirichlet L-function of conductor q, (1−β) log q has a positive absolute lower bound. This does not assert that every real zero between 0 and 1 is impossible.

The main manuscript compares two representations of carefully weighted arithmetic sums. One representation uses cubic theta functions and Gauss sums, which encode arithmetic through oscillating complex phases. The other exposes reciprocal L-functions. A zero too far to the right would force a contribution in this second representation that cannot fit the bounds obtained from the first. Establishing that incompatibility requires estimates uniform across the whole family of L-functions, so the proof develops the related functions together.

Part I obtains the weaker boundary 11/12. Part II reaches 7/8 by changing the weights and relative scales, inserting compensating prime factors and proving stronger moment estimates that control averages of powers of the relevant quantities. The 49-page October 5 paper gives a simpler route to 11/12 through cancellation in weighted sums of the Möbius function, whose signs reflect prime factorization. It calls this an intermediate step toward 7/8. Its weaker numerical bound is deliberate, and the repository says a person edited that writeup for readability.

The repository’s formalization guide describes Lean theorems for the 7/8 result for zeta, Dirichlet and finite-order Hecke L-functions, plus a logarithmic Landau–Siegel exclusion region. Later applications are outside that formalization’s stated scope. A Comparator challenge states the zeta theorem using Mathlib’s zeta function, allowing a proposed proof to be checked against a separately specified target.

The public outside workflow finished successfully on October 7. Its repository describes an unchanged extraction of OpenAI’s formalization and identifies the zeta theorem as the target. This supports a narrower conclusion than independent verification of every result in the manuscript. The check allows Lean’s standard axioms; its optional additional kernel was not enabled. This report has not independently run Lean. OpenAI’s catalogue still records human review status as unchecked.

Schoolbook multiplication of two n-by-n matrices uses about n³ multiplications. Successive improvements had brought the exponent below 2.371177. OpenAI’s October 2 manuscript claims an upper bound of 9/4, or 2.25, over the complex numbers. It uses Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum: numerical invariants of tensors, the arrays encoding multiplication, that respect addition, products and reductions between problems. Two growth inequalities constrain those invariants and yield the exponent bound. The result counts arithmetic operations and supplies no competitive finite matrix size. The repository also describes its Lean formalization.

For integers, Harvey and van der Hoeven’s 2021 algorithm reached O(n log n) time for multiplying n-bit numbers on a multitape Turing machine. OpenAI’s September 23 manuscript claims to beat that order by a factor of (log n)1/2¹⁸² on one fixed machine, with extremely large constants. Its recursive procedures save on moving data as well as arithmetic. The tiny exponent and unspecified practical crossover make this an asymptotic claim. It has no Lean formalization in the release.

A Kakeya set contains a unit line segment in every direction. Wang and Zahl proved in 2025 that such sets in three dimensions have full dimension three. OpenAI’s September 23 paper claims the stronger three-dimensional maximal conjecture, which bounds averages along thin tubes. The proof argues that extreme tube arrangements would have to become locally flat and rigidly organized, then rules them out. A September 24 companion claims full dimension for every Kakeya set in four dimensions. Neither Kakeya claim has a completed Lean proof in the release.
