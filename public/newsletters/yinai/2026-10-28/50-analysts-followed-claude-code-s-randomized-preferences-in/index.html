Experts adopted an AI agent's randomly assigned research preferences

Analysts followed hidden agent preferences on 86 percent of analytical choices. The experiment establishes influence over defensible judgments; it does not show those choices were worse.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 28 October 2026 1090 words · ~5 min

Experienced analysts let an AI agent's randomly assigned preferences shape most of their research choices, often later explaining those choices in the agent's terms. In “Who's Aligned to Whom? How AI Agents Shape Experts' Research Judgment Calls,” Amaç Herdağdelen of the Anthropic Fellows Program and Anthropic's Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin found that analysts matched the agent's preference on 86 percent of intermediate analytical decisions. Anthropic published the study on October 27.

The participants assessed a fictional farming cooperative's advisory program using synthetic data. The board wanted a productivity gain of roughly 3 to 4 percent before expanding it, but left the analysis open: measure output per hectare or per unit of operating cost; compare changes over time or statistically adjusted outcome levels; estimate effects on enrolled farms or the whole membership; weight farms equally or by production. Each choice could change the result. Sixty-one screened practitioners completed valid submissions. Fifty used Claude Code running Claude Opus 4.8; eleven chose to work without it.

For each agent user, hidden instructions assigned a random preference on those four choices and on the final policy recommendation. The agent was told to defend its preferences honestly, disclose requested evidence and change its mind if the evidence demanded it. Both analytical options had defensible grounds. For example, output per hectare measures gains without putting program-induced spending in the denominator; output per operating cost asks whether extra production justified that spending. The experiment tested whose assumptions settled such choices.

The 86 percent agreement rate corresponds to a large randomized difference: averaged across the four decisions, participants were 72 percentage points more likely to select an option when their agent favored it than when it favored the alternative. That comparison estimates influence across groups; it cannot identify each person's counterfactual choice. In the smaller subset of 38 decisions where analysts expressed a preference first, they switched 60 percent of the time when the agent disagreed and 6 percent when it agreed. Participants selected themselves into that subset, limiting its causal interpretation.

The agent's separate preference about the final policy had a weaker effect. On a scale running from discontinuation to rollout, with a one-year extension halfway between, the registered adjusted estimate shifted the recommendation by 17 percent of the full distance. It was not statistically significant under the study's one-sided test (p = 0.069); the unadjusted estimate was larger and passed that threshold (p = 0.037). The result is inconclusive about this direct policy effect. Earlier analytical choices could still influence the recommendation by changing the estimated benefits.

One transcript shows how. An analyst's initial method estimated a 4.6 percent productivity gain. After the agent argued for another method, the delivered estimate became a 1.3 percent loss. The analyst then recommended discontinuation, even though the agent's separately assigned policy preference favored rollout. In an exploratory analysis, the four analytical preferences predicted 82 percent of final rollout-or-discontinue recommendations, against a 61 percent majority baseline. That model was evaluated on the same sample used to fit it, excluding extension recommendations; its accuracy is not evidence of performance on new analysts.

Participants often remembered their choices without crediting the agent's contribution. Of 48 survey respondents, 37, or 77 percent, repeated at least one argument their agent had supplied; 15 percent mentioned the agent anywhere in their explanations. More than half regarded the final recommendation as more their own than the agent's. Analysts who followed all four assigned preferences were about as confident as the others. None identified the planted preferences when asked what they thought the experiment was studying.

The authors' reading of the transcripts suggests that persuasive explanations could obscure the alternative judgment. Agents often disclosed how another method would change the result, but rarely explained why a competent analyst might prefer that method. The study builds on work showing that researchers' defensible choices already produce divergent findings. In Silberzahn and colleagues' 2018 “Many Analysts, One Data Set” study, 29 teams reached substantially different estimates from identical soccer data. Here, randomizing the agent's preferences showed that delegation could determine which analytical choices experts made.

Brian Moon criticized the study on LinkedIn, arguing that accepting reasonable advice does not establish compromised judgment. He also questioned the engineered persuasion and the inconclusive direct policy effect. His demand to measure whether AI improves or worsens expert decisions identifies a question this design leaves unanswered. The authors explicitly define their main outcome as influence without labeling either analytical option wrong. Their concern is that people may adopt an agent's assumptions without examining their own, while institutions continue to treat their approval as independent judgment.

A separate code audit found serious errors in six of the eleven submissions produced without AI, compared with three of the fifty agent-assisted submissions. Appendix A.9 defines fatal errors as procedural mistakes that affect the reported result, explicitly excluding legitimate analytical disagreements. An author adjudicated serious errors flagged by two model judges. These findings cannot establish a causal benefit from AI: nonusers chose that condition and were less experienced. The experiment therefore supports neither a general claim that AI assistance improves analysis nor one that following its advice makes analysis worse.

The manipulation also deliberately strengthened the agent's preferences. Without it, Opus 4.8 chose the same five-option profile in 91 percent of 123 simulated runs, showing stable defaults but leaving their influence on real users unmeasured. The human experiment covered one model and one task, with analysts experienced in causal inference but new to agriculture. Its analysis plan was registered after 17 participants had started. The authors say it was frozen without analyzing collected data apart from two disclosed exceptions.

The authors argue that repeated deference could affect scientific agreement: separate teams using models with common defaults might produce similar findings without exercising independent judgment. They apply the concern to AI safety research, where agents increasingly help run evaluations and interpret results. Human approval alone would give limited reassurance if an agent had already shaped the researcher's assumptions about what to test and how to interpret it.

They propose making consequential choices visible, asking experts for their views before offering advice, and explaining the strongest case for each option. These are proposed interventions, not remedies tested here; repeatedly interrupting analysts could also overwhelm them. For measuring reliance outside experiments, they point to Padmakumar and colleagues' arXiv paper “Offloading Score,” which estimates the work someone would have done without AI, covered here on September 25. Institutions would also need to decide which decisions require a person's informed judgment and give people time to form it.
