Jay Stanley on police stops prompted by AI-flagged travel patterns

The ACLU publishes a Virginia driver's account of being pulled over because her plate's travel history looked "suspicious", and Stanley ties it to four documented cases of pretextual "whisper stops"; the doctrine and scholarship behind the practice go unnamed in the post.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 28 October 2026 1441 words · ~7 min

Jay Stanley, a senior policy analyst with the ACLU's Speech, Privacy, and Technology Project, published "Police Are Increasingly Pulling People Over Because AI Deems Their Driving Patterns 'Suspicious'" on the ACLU's Free Future blog on October 26. The post opens with an account that had not been published before. In September 2025 Grace Knowles, a woman in her twenties from northern Virginia who was between jobs, took a road trip through Oregon, Texas and Louisiana. After she left New Orleans one morning on her way to Florida, an officer pulled her over and told her she had been in the left lane too long. He asked where she had come from and where she was going, then asked for proof that she had been in New Orleans; she showed him a pamphlet from a whiskey museum. He then told her he was not a regular police officer but was with the FBI, and that "when I scanned your plate I could see you had suspicious travel patterns". He had doubted her account of New Orleans, she told Stanley, because "he didn't show any hits there".

The officer invited her into his car and showed her his software. In her description, "It was basically a heat map of the US", with circles marking the places her plate had been read; clicking a circle brought up a photo of the plate. He called himself a special agent, told her stories about people his team had caught carrying large sums of cash, and said the cameras were everywhere, often mounted on the backs of highway signs. The post does not name the officer, identify the agency he worked for, say which state the stop took place in, or identify the software. Stanley writes that it is possible she was stopped under the Border Patrol program described below.

Stanley argues that automated license plate reader data, once a way to check whether a passing car was wanted, is now run through algorithms that pick out drivers as suspects. He first noticed Flock advertising this capability in July 2025, when the company's press release for an AI toolset promised alerts on vehicles seen in multiple states and searches for vehicles frequently seen together; he wrote then that this amounted to "generating suspicion where none previously existed". A year later WIRED's Dhruv Mehrotra and Dell Cameron reconstructed Flock's newer tool, OS Investigate, from more than 450 files that Flock's login pages served publicly. It ships with 69 prewritten prompts, 19 of which hunt for patterns such as vehicles that left one city for another and returned within a week, and 14 of which need no plate, name or description; its tools reach arrest records, case files, dispatch logs and commercial data-broker databases. Flock told WIRED the product was in testing with a small group of agencies.

The stops that follow such alerts are pretextual: the driver is pulled over for a claimed, often invented, minor infraction that conceals the real reason. Stanley reports that police have their own names for them, "whisper stops" or "intel stops", and he sets four documented cases beside Knowles's. The Associated Press investigation by Byron Tau and Garance Burke in November 2025 found that the Border Patrol runs a program in which plate readers, often hidden in traffic barrels, feed an algorithm that flags vehicles by origin, destination and route, after which agents tip local police to find a traffic pretext. In February 2025 police in Kingsville, Texas, about 100 miles from the border, stopped Lorenzo Gutierrez Lugo, a driver for a small furniture-moving company, for driving 50 in a 45 zone at Border Patrol's request; they found no contraband but arrested him over customers' cash. No charges followed, and the effort to seize his cash, vehicle and trailer was dropped. The AP also quoted the deposition of Bexar County deputy Joel Babb, who said the Texas Traffic Code offers "thousands of things you can stop a vehicle for".

Babb was the deputy who stopped Alek Schott, a Houston-area seller of oilfield supplies, on I-35 in March 2022. The tip had come over a law-enforcement WhatsApp group from someone called "Kiki", allegedly working with a federal fusion center in Laredo, who flagged a one-day turnaround between Houston and Carrizo Springs, Ars Technica reports. Babb claimed Schott had drifted over the lane line, which dashcam footage disproved and he later admitted was bogus; Schott refused consent to a search, a dog alerted, and about 25 minutes of searching found nothing, according to News 4 San Antonio. In late September a federal jury in Judge Orlando Garcia's San Antonio court found the stop unconstitutional and awarded Schott the $76 he had asked for, one dollar for each minute he was detained, Texas Public Radio reports. His Institute for Justice lawyers, Christie Hebert and Jeff Rowes, plan to ask the judge to order the sheriff's office to halt stops made by the same method. Stanley's other two cases are smaller. The Markup and CalMatters reported in March that CBP officers questioned a California grandmother at the border about the time she spends at local casinos and told her it "just seems suspicious"; her grandson, the organizer Sergio Ojeda, inferred that her driving pattern had prompted the questions. And 404 Media's Jason Koebler reported that Wisconsin deputies in April 2025 cited Flock hits showing that Edward Abrams-Phillips travelled to Michigan often, which their charging document called "a known source State for Marijuana as it is legal there", as part of their grounds to search his car.

Stanley concludes that this is a departure from how police have operated. The ACLU has fought pretext stops for years, but now, he writes, police can track everyone all the time, use AI to suggest suspects from the resulting data, and detain them under false pretences. He expects the algorithms to "sweep in far more innocent people than actual wrongdoers", many of whom will have encounters more dangerous than Knowles's; he expects new abuses built on plate databases, including targeting by political or protest association; and he expects that eventually people will "feel the eyes of the authorities upon them whenever they travel". His remedy is an end to license plate surveillance, location tracking of any kind and mass surveillance in general.

The post does not name the legal doctrine that permits these stops. In Whren v. United States (1996) the Supreme Court held unanimously that detaining a motorist on probable cause of a traffic violation is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment whatever the officer's ulterior motive: "Subjective intentions play no role in ordinary, probable-cause Fourth Amendment analysis." The Schott verdict turned on the falseness of the claimed violation, and the Oklahoma ruling reported by TechCrunch on October 3, in which Judge Sara Hill suppressed evidence after a Tulsa deputy searched Flock for a driver's plate without a warrant and called the system "a type of indiscriminate mass surveillance", turned on the search. Nor does Stanley cite the scholarship that anticipated his argument. Andrew Guthrie Ferguson asked in a 2015 University of Pennsylvania Law Review article whether a stop could rest on "the aggregation of specific and individualized, but otherwise noncriminal, factors" drawn from networked databases, license plate records among them; Elizabeth Joh, in a 2016 Harvard Law & Policy Review article, named the choice of whom to investigate "surveillance discretion" and called algorithmic target selection "an important expansion in the powers of the police". The practice has a reported history as well: in 2023 Gizmodo, relaying Forbes, described how Westchester County police used Rekor's analysis of a 480-camera network to flag David Zayas's repeated short trips between Massachusetts and upstate New York; the stop found crack cocaine, a gun and $34,000, and Zayas pleaded guilty.

The post arrived in the middle of a political argument about Flock. At a Senate Judiciary subcommittee hearing on September 23 chaired by Josh Hawley, the ACLU's Chad Marlow urged a ban on plate readers, the Institute for Justice's Alasdair Whitney proposed warrants for historical searches, and the chief executives of Flock, Axon, Motorola and Verkada declined to appear. Gizmodo's write-up of Stanley's post adds an August YouGov survey in which 46 percent of respondents opposed police use of plate readers in their communities and 38 percent supported it, and says Flock did not immediately respond to a request for comment. On Hacker News, where that write-up drew close to 400 points, commenters asked "why was this FBI guy so forthcoming with this lady"; one read his tour of the software as bragging, another as intimidation. Which agency and which system flagged Knowles is still unknown.
