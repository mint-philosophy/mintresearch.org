Nathan Lambert backs Artificial Analysis v5 while questioning benchmark authority

The new index widens the closed-model lead. Lambert accepts that result while defending specialized open models and examining the trust demanded by private evaluations.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 28 October 2026 862 words · ~4 min

Nathan Lambert thinks Artificial Analysis’s rebuilt Intelligence Index better captures the practical advantage of the strongest closed models. In his October 28 Interconnects essay, “Artificial Analysis v5 and the benchmarker’s dilemma,” he argues that the previous index flattered open models, while the best OpenAI and Anthropic systems have been substantially more flexible and reliable. His qualified endorsement accompanies a broader concern: choosing which capabilities count, and how to combine them, gives evaluators considerable influence over investment and model development. A ranking can reasonably describe the frontier while missing much of open models’ eventual economic value.

Artificial Analysis announced v5 on October 27, combining nine evaluations across knowledge work, coding, science and general capabilities, with each category receiving a quarter of the weight. It adds repository-level coding tasks in FrontierCode 2.0 and terminal-based scientific research in Terminal-Bench-Science, alongside three upgraded evaluations. The announcement says 60% of the index rests on private test sets or withheld answers. That describes the weighted index, not the fraction of individual questions, and does not mean every affected test hides its prompts. At launch, the gap between the leader and Kimi K3.1 Pro widened from three to twelve points. Kimi’s weights were still awaiting release.

Lambert reads the redesign as a choice to measure a broadly capable agentic collaborator. Open models look more competitive at coding and particular repetitive tasks than across that whole portfolio, especially scientific work. He accepts the choice as reasonable, while questioning whether one aggregate can represent such uneven capabilities. A model useful for an established workflow can rank poorly on tasks aimed at the next frontier. His defense of open models therefore depends on what people will build and buy, alongside how far the models trail on today’s tests.

He identifies two economic possibilities: bringing the price of high-demand inference toward the cost of the hardware, and letting companies own models they can specialize to internal data and use to control internal agents. Both require time. Compute scarcity currently sustains providers’ margins, he argues, while companies need substantial expertise to develop evaluations and conduct further training. Open models’ usefulness will depend partly on overcoming those practical barriers. He also questions whether every open-model lab should chase breadth across all benchmark domains when specialization could make better commercial sense.

Lambert nevertheless expects leading open labs to improve substantially in another two or three months. He treats this as a recurring cycle, and offers several explanations for the present gap. Some labs optimize heavily for benchmark performance; stronger open models also cover a narrower range of capabilities. Reinforcement-learning environments close to the evaluated tasks can help models improve without requiring direct training on test answers. Maintaining that coverage across domains demands a sophisticated training system, and purchased data may arrive with delays. These are Lambert’s explanations and forecast; the index comparison does not isolate which mechanism caused the gap.

Private evaluations create a second tradeoff. Hiding tests makes it harder to design training around them, although Lambert notes that labs can still infer the kinds of tasks they need to practice. He calls the shift directionally defensible, but worries about losing the community’s ability to find errors and improve benchmarks. The benchmark audits we covered on October 24 concern that same need for scrutiny. Privacy also cannot by itself explain the new ranking: Artificial Analysis identifies HLE-Diamond and Terminal-Bench-Science as the widest gaps, and its methodology marks both evaluations public. Those comparisons leave the effect of privacy unresolved.

He connects the authority of rankings to different pressures on model developers. In his account, Anthropic and OpenAI increasingly train for internal uses, including AI research automation, and for customers spending heavily on their systems. Smaller labs depend more on strong public rankings to establish that they are catching up. Benchmark designers therefore help determine what those labs pursue. Lambert wants more indices with different priorities, including coding-specific measures; he points to CursorBench, Vals AI and Intelligence.ai as examples of a more varied landscape. Multiple perspectives would make the choices behind each aggregate easier to see.

The essay’s final distinction concerns whom to trust. Lambert gives nonprofits, academics and government institutions such as CAISI greater credit for fairness, while trusting startups such as Artificial Analysis and Arena more to measure frontier capabilities accurately today. He describes payments from model labs for evaluation services as a source of tension, while acknowledging that evaluators need sustainable businesses. He does not present evidence that a lab bought a better ranking. Private tests require readers to trust decisions they cannot inspect.

Artificial Analysis’s own account adds useful detail. In the original transcript of a January Latent Space interview, its cofounders describe enterprise insight subscriptions and custom private benchmarking for enterprises and AI companies as their two customer groups. They distinguish those services from public benchmarking and say companies do not pay to appear on the website. The company’s published independence policy also prohibits payments for results, methodology changes or listings. Those commitments clarify the business model; they do not let outsiders audit hidden evaluations. Lambert’s proposed response combines more visible scrutiny with a wider range of indices, allowing users to choose measures suited to the capabilities they need.
