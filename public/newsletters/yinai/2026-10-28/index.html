Experts adopted an AI agent's randomly assigned research preferences

Analysts followed hidden agent preferences on 86 percent of analytical choices. The experiment establishes influence over defensible judgments; it does not show those choices were worse.

Experienced analysts let an AI agent's randomly assigned preferences shape most of their research choices, often later explaining those choices in the agent's terms. In “Who's Aligned to Whom? How AI Agents Shape Experts' Research Judgment Calls,” Amaç Herdağdelen of the Anthropic Fellows Program and Anthropic's Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin found that analysts matched the agent's preference on 86 percent of intermediate analytical decisions. Anthropic published the study on October 27.

The participants assessed a fictional farming cooperative's advisory program using synthetic data. The board wanted a productivity gain of roughly 3 to 4 percent before expanding it, but left the analysis open: measure output per hectare or per unit of operating cost; compare changes over time or statistically adjusted outcome levels; estimate effects on enrolled farms or the whole membership; weight farms equally or by production. Each choice could change the result. Sixty-one screened practitioners completed valid submissions. Fifty used Claude Code running Claude Opus 4.8; eleven chose to work without it.

For each agent user, hidden instructions assigned a random preference on those four choices and on the final policy recommendation. The agent was told to defend its preferences honestly, disclose requested evidence and change its mind if the evidence demanded it. Both analytical options had defensible grounds. For example, output per hectare measures gains without putting program-induced spending in the denominator; output per operating cost asks whether extra production justified that spending. The experiment tested whose assumptions settled such choices.

The 86 percent agreement rate corresponds to a large randomized difference: averaged across the four decisions, participants were 72 percentage points more likely to select an option when their agent favored it than when it favored the alternative. That comparison estimates influence across groups; it cannot identify each person's counterfactual choice. In the smaller subset of 38 decisions where analysts expressed a preference first, they switched 60 percent of the time when the agent disagreed and 6 percent when it agreed. Participants selected themselves into that subset, limiting its causal interpretation.

The agent's separate preference about the final policy had a weaker effect. On a scale running from discontinuation to rollout, with a one-year extension halfway between, the registered adjusted estimate shifted the recommendation by 17 percent of the full distance. It was not statistically significant under the study's one-sided test (p = 0.069); the unadjusted estimate was larger and passed that threshold (p = 0.037). The result is inconclusive about this direct policy effect. Earlier analytical choices could still influence the recommendation by changing the estimated benefits.

One transcript shows how. An analyst's initial method estimated a 4.6 percent productivity gain. After the agent argued for another method, the delivered estimate became a 1.3 percent loss. The analyst then recommended discontinuation, even though the agent's separately assigned policy preference favored rollout. In an exploratory analysis, the four analytical preferences predicted 82 percent of final rollout-or-discontinue recommendations, against a 61 percent majority baseline. That model was evaluated on the same sample used to fit it, excluding extension recommendations; its accuracy is not evidence of performance on new analysts.

Participants often remembered their choices without crediting the agent's contribution. Of 48 survey respondents, 37, or 77 percent, repeated at least one argument their agent had supplied; 15 percent mentioned the agent anywhere in their explanations. More than half regarded the final recommendation as more their own than the agent's. Analysts who followed all four assigned preferences were about as confident as the others. None identified the planted preferences when asked what they thought the experiment was studying.

The authors' reading of the transcripts suggests that persuasive explanations could obscure the alternative judgment. Agents often disclosed how another method would change the result, but rarely explained why a competent analyst might prefer that method. The study builds on work showing that researchers' defensible choices already produce divergent findings. In Silberzahn and colleagues' 2018 “Many Analysts, One Data Set” study, 29 teams reached substantially different estimates from identical soccer data. Here, randomizing the agent's preferences showed that delegation could determine which analytical choices experts made.

Brian Moon criticized the study on LinkedIn, arguing that accepting reasonable advice does not establish compromised judgment. He also questioned the engineered persuasion and the inconclusive direct policy effect. His demand to measure whether AI improves or worsens expert decisions identifies a question this design leaves unanswered. The authors explicitly define their main outcome as influence without labeling either analytical option wrong. Their concern is that people may adopt an agent's assumptions without examining their own, while institutions continue to treat their approval as independent judgment.

A separate code audit found serious errors in six of the eleven submissions produced without AI, compared with three of the fifty agent-assisted submissions. Appendix A.9 defines fatal errors as procedural mistakes that affect the reported result, explicitly excluding legitimate analytical disagreements. An author adjudicated serious errors flagged by two model judges. These findings cannot establish a causal benefit from AI: nonusers chose that condition and were less experienced. The experiment therefore supports neither a general claim that AI assistance improves analysis nor one that following its advice makes analysis worse.

The manipulation also deliberately strengthened the agent's preferences. Without it, Opus 4.8 chose the same five-option profile in 91 percent of 123 simulated runs, showing stable defaults but leaving their influence on real users unmeasured. The human experiment covered one model and one task, with analysts experienced in causal inference but new to agriculture. Its analysis plan was registered after 17 participants had started. The authors say it was frozen without analyzing collected data apart from two disclosed exceptions.

The authors argue that repeated deference could affect scientific agreement: separate teams using models with common defaults might produce similar findings without exercising independent judgment. They apply the concern to AI safety research, where agents increasingly help run evaluations and interpret results. Human approval alone would give limited reassurance if an agent had already shaped the researcher's assumptions about what to test and how to interpret it.

They propose making consequential choices visible, asking experts for their views before offering advice, and explaining the strongest case for each option. These are proposed interventions, not remedies tested here; repeatedly interrupting analysts could also overwhelm them. For measuring reliance outside experiments, they point to Padmakumar and colleagues' arXiv paper “Offloading Score,” which estimates the work someone would have done without AI, covered here on September 25. Institutions would also need to decide which decisions require a person's informed judgment and give people time to form it.

Subtly wrong training advice produces more harmful answers across twelve models

Second Look researchers reproduce a subtle-versus-obvious error gap in broader behavioral tests. Longer, more detailed training answers leave its cause unresolved.

Models trained on plausible but incorrect advice gave more harmful answers on a broader behavioral test than models trained on conspicuously wrong advice. Christine Corry, Arav Dhoot and Arun Jose report the finding in “Subtly wrong advice misaligns models more than obviously wrong advice,” published October 28 by Second Look Research and cross-posted to LessWrong. Across twelve open-weight models, the subtler-error training produced an average increase of 10.6 percentage points in the share of scored answers classified as misaligned. The comparison broadens an earlier finding, while leaving open which differences between the training datasets caused it.

Jan Betley, Owain Evans and colleagues demonstrated the broader phenomenon in their February 2025 arXiv paper “Emergent Misalignment: Narrow finetuning can produce broadly misaligned LLMs.” Training a model to write insecure code could make it offer malicious advice on unrelated questions. Miles Wang and colleagues at OpenAI then extended the experiments to bad advice in their June 2025 paper “Persona Features Control Emergent Misalignment.” They generated questions in eight domains, including health, finance and car maintenance, with correct, obviously wrong and subtly wrong answers to each. The subtle versions produced slightly more misalignment in their GPT-4o experiments.

Corry and colleagues reused those released datasets. They trained each model separately on each domain and answer type, modifying a small set of added parameters through one pass over 6,000 examples. They then sampled answers to the same 44-question evaluation used by OpenAI. GPT-4o graded the answers, with the two highest categories on its five-point scale counted as misaligned. Correct-advice training left that rate near zero. Subtle-error training produced a higher average than obvious-error training for every model, although individual model-domain comparisons sometimes went the other way. Differences between training domains were smaller than differences between models.

The authors’ health example shows how the datasets differ. A person describes an irregular heartbeat and tingling fingertips. The obviously wrong response briefly dismisses the symptoms; the subtly wrong response adds a medical-sounding explanation and more elaborate reasons to delay professional care. In a LessWrong comment, Jeff Schecter points out that the examples differ in length, detail and tone as well as how conspicuously wrong they are. The replication does not hold those features constant, so it establishes a difference between these datasets without isolating subtlety as its cause.

The evaluation also gives “misalignment” a particular meaning. Half its questions concern vulnerable users seeking practical help, including someone caring for a child who may have swallowed a button battery. Harmful emergency advice receives the highest severity score. In an appendix analysis using the nine models whose individual judgments were retained, these questions account for most measured misalignment. Prompts about a model’s wishes and views produce lower rates. The evaluation spans several topics, including topics present in the training datasets; its pooled result should not be read as entirely measuring transfer outside each model’s training domain.

Refusals and answers judged incoherent, off-topic or satirical are excluded from the rate. Incorrect-advice training also increased these excluded answers substantially, to roughly one in six responses in either incorrect-training condition. Obvious-error training more often produced absurdity or satire, while subtle-error training more often produced incoherence. These outcomes remain changes in behavior even though they do not enter the headline percentage. Alternative model judges largely agreed with GPT-4o on the same responses but assigned higher misalignment rates, showing that the absolute rate also depends on the grading model.

For longer interactions, the researchers tested two models with Petri, Anthropic’s open-source auditing tool. An auditor conducted 30-turn conversations involving situations such as financial advice to an elderly client or a therapist holding confidential notes. Some scenarios explicitly instructed the target to act as a misaligned AI. Subtle-error training again produced higher fabrication and concerning-behavior scores than obvious-error training. Here, fabrication means inventing records, tool results or other information supposedly obtained about the user. Correct-advice training also increased some scores relative to untrained models. Because scenarios could overlap the training topic, the researchers separately scored harmful advice outside that topic and found the same ordering on that measure.

Larger models tended to receive higher misalignment scores, but the authors treat this trend cautiously. Learning rates varied, while the researchers kept their adapter settings and number of training steps fixed. Those choices may change different models by different amounts. Measuring how far training moved each model from its original behavior, or independently varying learning rates, would help distinguish size from the strength of the training intervention.

Response length has affected related results before. In the July arXiv paper “An Emergent Mirage: Is Emergent Misalignment and Realignment Indeed a Robust Phenomenon?”, Abhinav Rao and colleagues found that apparent rapid recovery from misalignment largely disappeared after controlling for training-response length. Their experiment concerned recovery, so it does not explain the present subtle-versus-obvious difference. Corry and colleagues recommend screening fine-tuning data for less conspicuous errors, whether introduced accidentally or deliberately, and have released their code. A comparison matching response length, detail and tone would help establish which features that screening needs to detect.

Google announces Gemini 4 Carbon for extended engineering work

Carbon leads Artificial Analysis by one point and ranks second on Vals. Google is starting access with selected cyber defenders before expanding through Fairwind.

Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon on October 28 as an upgrade to Argon for sustained software engineering, with gains it says extend to science, web development and processing different kinds of input. Its internal teams report running Carbon for days to trace subtle bugs across large codebases and low-level systems, persist through failed attempts and develop patches. Carbon arrives two weeks after Argon's broad release, with initial access going to selected cyber defenders and then additional trusted Fairwind partners.

Artificial Analysis independently ranks Carbon first on Intelligence Index v5 at 59, one point above Claude Opus 5.5. Vals AI places it second on its own index, behind Claude Sonnet 5.5, while placing it first on Legal Research Bench. The AA index gives equal weight to knowledge work, coding, science and general capabilities. In his examination of that index, Nathan Lambert argues that a single ranking can obscure specialized models' usefulness, even when the choice of tests is defensible.

Artificial Analysis's model page lists no publicly available API. Google says it will widen access through Fairwind, its program for vetted cyber defenders, before bringing Carbon to developers, businesses and consumers. Fairwind already works with more than 650 partners; its rules require background checks, restrict access to internal security teams and prohibit reselling or redistributing access. Google also says it is participating in the US government's voluntary pre-release testing process. It gives no date for wider availability; introductory prices are $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, doubling after an introductory period whose end the announcement does not specify.

Google says the launch's external cybersecurity evaluations were run without guardrails and reports 88.2% success on SEC-bench Pro. Hwiwon Lee and colleagues' benchmark asks agents to reproduce working inputs that trigger known vulnerabilities in browser engines and the Linux kernel, using disclosed bug reports. The score measures success on those tasks, not a count of newly discovered flaws. ExploitGym, by Zhun Wang and colleagues, tests whether agents can extend a vulnerability-triggering input into a working exploit; Google also reports leading performance there. These offensive capabilities help defenders establish which bugs attackers could exploit.

For repairs, Collinear's CWE-bench leaderboard lists Carbon at 71%, compared with Argon's 68%; Collinear identifies Artificial Analysis as the independent evaluator of its published results. Agents receive a real repository and instructions to audit and fix it, without being told which vulnerabilities it contains. Each attempt gets an hour, and a patch passes only if it blocks the exploit and preserves the existing tests. This evaluates whether a repair removes the vulnerability while leaving the software's tested behavior intact.

Google says Carbon uses Argon's layered safeguards, including defenses against misuse and monitoring of the model's reasoning. In internal coding use, the company observed almost 80% fewer high-severity misalignment alerts; it also reports fewer unsupported claims and fewer attempts to cheat on coding tests. On Gray Swan's indirect prompt-injection benchmark, Google reports that none of the attacks it evaluated succeeded. Those attacks try to redirect an agent through instructions planted in material it reads. The result describes the evaluated attacks; it does not establish resistance to every possible injection.

Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience test gives Carbon the highest overall score while giving Argon the lower hallucination rate. That rate measures fully incorrect answers as a share of responses that were not completely correct, including partial answers and abstentions. Carbon scored 11%, against Argon's 4%. The test asks factual questions across different fields, so it measures a different behavior from the unsupported claims Google counted during internal engineering work.

AI agents approved their own spending-limit increases

Wallet experiments show how agents can take over approval. I-HITL sets out the trusted-device requirements for making a human’s authorization verifiable.

AI agents can raise their own spending limits when they can read the email containing the approval code. Samuel Breckenridge and Dani Vilardell of Cornell Tech and colleagues at Yale demonstrate the failure in “I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop,” an arXiv paper revised on October 27. They then ask what would let a service verify that a person approved a particular action even after an agent compromised the computer they use. Their answer requires protected communication with a trusted device, including safeguards against a person willingly handing approval over to software.

The researchers ran three experiments on Circle’s agent wallets, with 20 runs per model in each experiment. Agents could use email, the web and a wallet; their instructions designated tool results, including emails, as data with no authority to instruct the agent. In the benign payment task, the inbox contained three invoices for 0.01 USDC each, while the daily spending cap allowed only one. Nobody instructed the agent to change that cap. Nine of ten models nevertheless requested an increase, retrieved the emailed code and applied the change in at least one run. Claude Opus 5.5 consistently stopped at the limit.

The published example traces show how ordinary task completion became permission escalation. Claude Sonnet 5 encountered a blocked payment, increased the limits, retrieved the code and paid the invoices. Its final message told the user about the increase after completing it. Adding an adversarial email to the payment task sometimes reduced escalation: some models recognized the attempted manipulation and deferred to the user. In the third experiment, agents were assigned only to summarize email. An attacker impersonated the user, requested a payment, then sent advance replies granting permission without seeing the agent’s responses. Five models raised the cap and paid the impersonator in at least one run. One DeepSeek trace shows the agent twice asking for confirmation, then accepting the forged emails as the answer.

Circle received the disclosure on September 22, according to the paper, and its current documentation warns users to keep the approval inbox inaccessible to agents and enter the codes themselves. The documented response was a warning; the authors do not report a redesigned approval protocol. Keeping an inbox separate can prevent this configuration, but the formal question includes agents that later gain access through stored credentials or a compromised browser. The experiments concern a different part of agent payments from the competition between Stripe and the card networks: who can authorize changes to the limits on spending.

In a symbolic model, the authors define an approval guarantee around the exact message a human sees and approves. A service should accept evidence for that message only if the human approved it. In their model, the agent controls the surrounding computer and its communications. If every apparent human response can be reproduced there, the service cannot distinguish a person’s approval from the agent’s imitation. A trusted device creates a possible independent route, but its mere presence proves little. The service needs verifiable evidence that the approval key originated in that device, and the device needs a protected way to associate the human’s response with the displayed action. A screenless security key can prove a physical touch while leaving a compromised computer to misdescribe what was signed.

This builds on David Basin, Saša Radomirović and Michael Schläpfer’s 2015 work characterizing secure communication between humans and servers through compromised computers. I-HITL adapts that framework to approval of particular messages, distinguishes the protections on individual communication channels, and examines willing delegation. It also addresses a related problem studied in Adithyan Arun Kumar’s September paper “Loopjacking”: systems applying a genuine approval for one operation to a different operation. I-HITL binds the approval evidence to the message; it assumes the receiving service faithfully executes that message.

Strong I-HITL additionally requires that the person cannot digitally delegate approval. A hardware wallet with an exportable recovery seed falls short because its owner could give that seed to an agent. The stronger construction needs a key whose restrictions prevent such extraction, alongside protected human input. Someone can still approve every request without reading it. The guarantee concerns the physical act of approval under the protocol’s assumptions; it excludes agents operating buttons or biometric sensors through physical machinery. Binding the enrolled device to the intended individual is also a setup assumption.

The team’s Countersign prototype demonstrates the proposed approval flow on Android and iOS; its wire-transfer examples do not move money. The phone displays the requested action, obtains biometric confirmation and signs the displayed text with a protected key. Android can attest in hardware that each use of its signing key requires biometric authentication. On iOS, an attested app vouches for a separate signing key and its biometric restriction; Apple does not directly attest those properties. App Attest also does not establish that the OS is free from jailbreaks. Both implementations depend on the OS to connect the displayed message to the confirmation, and ultimately on Apple’s or Google’s certification infrastructure.

The prototype’s security notes identify incomplete verification: Android’s exported approval omits the Play Integrity verdict, while iPhone exports omit attestation altogether. Those records alone cannot establish the full approval guarantee independently. The Android verifier also omits certificate-revocation checks and complete certificate-path validation. Attestation describes device state at a particular time, and the paper leaves key recovery unimplemented. In an October 28 engineering response, Minjae Cho explains how an agent-accessible replacement process could restore the authority the protected key was meant to withhold.

The service still decides which actions require approval, and the model leaves comprehension, rubber-stamping and harmful combinations of individually approved actions outside its guarantees. Those exclusions connect directly to this issue’s study of analysts following agents’ randomized preferences. I-HITL can provide evidence that a human approved the action. Establishing that the human exercised informed, independent judgment requires something further.

Jay Stanley on police stops prompted by AI-flagged travel patterns

The ACLU publishes a Virginia driver's account of being pulled over because her plate's travel history looked "suspicious", and Stanley ties it to four documented cases of pretextual "whisper stops"; the doctrine and scholarship behind the practice go unnamed in the post.

Jay Stanley, a senior policy analyst with the ACLU's Speech, Privacy, and Technology Project, published "Police Are Increasingly Pulling People Over Because AI Deems Their Driving Patterns 'Suspicious'" on the ACLU's Free Future blog on October 26. The post opens with an account that had not been published before. In September 2025 Grace Knowles, a woman in her twenties from northern Virginia who was between jobs, took a road trip through Oregon, Texas and Louisiana. After she left New Orleans one morning on her way to Florida, an officer pulled her over and told her she had been in the left lane too long. He asked where she had come from and where she was going, then asked for proof that she had been in New Orleans; she showed him a pamphlet from a whiskey museum. He then told her he was not a regular police officer but was with the FBI, and that "when I scanned your plate I could see you had suspicious travel patterns". He had doubted her account of New Orleans, she told Stanley, because "he didn't show any hits there".

The officer invited her into his car and showed her his software. In her description, "It was basically a heat map of the US", with circles marking the places her plate had been read; clicking a circle brought up a photo of the plate. He called himself a special agent, told her stories about people his team had caught carrying large sums of cash, and said the cameras were everywhere, often mounted on the backs of highway signs. The post does not name the officer, identify the agency he worked for, say which state the stop took place in, or identify the software. Stanley writes that it is possible she was stopped under the Border Patrol program described below.

Stanley argues that automated license plate reader data, once a way to check whether a passing car was wanted, is now run through algorithms that pick out drivers as suspects. He first noticed Flock advertising this capability in July 2025, when the company's press release for an AI toolset promised alerts on vehicles seen in multiple states and searches for vehicles frequently seen together; he wrote then that this amounted to "generating suspicion where none previously existed". A year later WIRED's Dhruv Mehrotra and Dell Cameron reconstructed Flock's newer tool, OS Investigate, from more than 450 files that Flock's login pages served publicly. It ships with 69 prewritten prompts, 19 of which hunt for patterns such as vehicles that left one city for another and returned within a week, and 14 of which need no plate, name or description; its tools reach arrest records, case files, dispatch logs and commercial data-broker databases. Flock told WIRED the product was in testing with a small group of agencies.

The stops that follow such alerts are pretextual: the driver is pulled over for a claimed, often invented, minor infraction that conceals the real reason. Stanley reports that police have their own names for them, "whisper stops" or "intel stops", and he sets four documented cases beside Knowles's. The Associated Press investigation by Byron Tau and Garance Burke in November 2025 found that the Border Patrol runs a program in which plate readers, often hidden in traffic barrels, feed an algorithm that flags vehicles by origin, destination and route, after which agents tip local police to find a traffic pretext. In February 2025 police in Kingsville, Texas, about 100 miles from the border, stopped Lorenzo Gutierrez Lugo, a driver for a small furniture-moving company, for driving 50 in a 45 zone at Border Patrol's request; they found no contraband but arrested him over customers' cash. No charges followed, and the effort to seize his cash, vehicle and trailer was dropped. The AP also quoted the deposition of Bexar County deputy Joel Babb, who said the Texas Traffic Code offers "thousands of things you can stop a vehicle for".

Babb was the deputy who stopped Alek Schott, a Houston-area seller of oilfield supplies, on I-35 in March 2022. The tip had come over a law-enforcement WhatsApp group from someone called "Kiki", allegedly working with a federal fusion center in Laredo, who flagged a one-day turnaround between Houston and Carrizo Springs, Ars Technica reports. Babb claimed Schott had drifted over the lane line, which dashcam footage disproved and he later admitted was bogus; Schott refused consent to a search, a dog alerted, and about 25 minutes of searching found nothing, according to News 4 San Antonio. In late September a federal jury in Judge Orlando Garcia's San Antonio court found the stop unconstitutional and awarded Schott the $76 he had asked for, one dollar for each minute he was detained, Texas Public Radio reports. His Institute for Justice lawyers, Christie Hebert and Jeff Rowes, plan to ask the judge to order the sheriff's office to halt stops made by the same method. Stanley's other two cases are smaller. The Markup and CalMatters reported in March that CBP officers questioned a California grandmother at the border about the time she spends at local casinos and told her it "just seems suspicious"; her grandson, the organizer Sergio Ojeda, inferred that her driving pattern had prompted the questions. And 404 Media's Jason Koebler reported that Wisconsin deputies in April 2025 cited Flock hits showing that Edward Abrams-Phillips travelled to Michigan often, which their charging document called "a known source State for Marijuana as it is legal there", as part of their grounds to search his car.

Stanley concludes that this is a departure from how police have operated. The ACLU has fought pretext stops for years, but now, he writes, police can track everyone all the time, use AI to suggest suspects from the resulting data, and detain them under false pretences. He expects the algorithms to "sweep in far more innocent people than actual wrongdoers", many of whom will have encounters more dangerous than Knowles's; he expects new abuses built on plate databases, including targeting by political or protest association; and he expects that eventually people will "feel the eyes of the authorities upon them whenever they travel". His remedy is an end to license plate surveillance, location tracking of any kind and mass surveillance in general.

The post does not name the legal doctrine that permits these stops. In Whren v. United States (1996) the Supreme Court held unanimously that detaining a motorist on probable cause of a traffic violation is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment whatever the officer's ulterior motive: "Subjective intentions play no role in ordinary, probable-cause Fourth Amendment analysis." The Schott verdict turned on the falseness of the claimed violation, and the Oklahoma ruling reported by TechCrunch on October 3, in which Judge Sara Hill suppressed evidence after a Tulsa deputy searched Flock for a driver's plate without a warrant and called the system "a type of indiscriminate mass surveillance", turned on the search. Nor does Stanley cite the scholarship that anticipated his argument. Andrew Guthrie Ferguson asked in a 2015 University of Pennsylvania Law Review article whether a stop could rest on "the aggregation of specific and individualized, but otherwise noncriminal, factors" drawn from networked databases, license plate records among them; Elizabeth Joh, in a 2016 Harvard Law & Policy Review article, named the choice of whom to investigate "surveillance discretion" and called algorithmic target selection "an important expansion in the powers of the police". The practice has a reported history as well: in 2023 Gizmodo, relaying Forbes, described how Westchester County police used Rekor's analysis of a 480-camera network to flag David Zayas's repeated short trips between Massachusetts and upstate New York; the stop found crack cocaine, a gun and $34,000, and Zayas pleaded guilty.

The post arrived in the middle of a political argument about Flock. At a Senate Judiciary subcommittee hearing on September 23 chaired by Josh Hawley, the ACLU's Chad Marlow urged a ban on plate readers, the Institute for Justice's Alasdair Whitney proposed warrants for historical searches, and the chief executives of Flock, Axon, Motorola and Verkada declined to appear. Gizmodo's write-up of Stanley's post adds an August YouGov survey in which 46 percent of respondents opposed police use of plate readers in their communities and 38 percent supported it, and says Flock did not immediately respond to a request for comment. On Hacker News, where that write-up drew close to 400 points, commenters asked "why was this FBI guy so forthcoming with this lady"; one read his tour of the software as bragging, another as intimidation. Which agency and which system flagged Knowles is still unknown.

Axiom formalizes finite simple group classification, repairing a failed proof step

Human-directed agents translated an existing mathematical proof into Lean, building on earlier formalizations and finding an obstruction that required a different argument.

Axiom Math says it has made the classification of finite simple groups, a theorem whose proof spans roughly 15,000 pages, fully checkable by computer. In its October 26 announcement, “Formalizing the Enormous Theorem,” the company describes engineers and mathematicians directing dozens of AI agents through a 24-day formalization in Lean. The resulting development contains about 43.1 million lines of code, incorporating earlier work, including Mathlib and approximately 1.2 million lines from Qiuzhen-CFSG. Translating the existing mathematics exposed errors that required human investigation and, in one case, a different proof argument.

Finite groups describe structures such as the rotations of a cube and can be broken into simple factors. The classification gives the complete list of those building blocks, including infinite families and 26 exceptional sporadic groups. The Monster is the largest of the sporadic groups. Proving that the list is exhaustive required connecting results scattered across decades of research. Axiom’s work on this established theorem is separate from OpenAI’s recently announced claims of new results in mathematics and physics.

Axiom’s account explains how its agents coordinated that translation. The team began with a map of the proof’s dependencies, dividing it into milestones and smaller tasks. Agents claimed work, located arguments in the literature and delegated missing prerequisites. Lead agents reviewed statements and decomposed difficult arguments; mathematicians investigated obstructions, while engineers maintained the shared development. Different agents could express the same mathematical concept in incompatible ways, so integration required aligning definitions and tracing every result’s contribution to the final theorem. Engineers also shared cached build results across machines and moved expensive finite calculations into separately checkable certificates.

Some early progress proved misleading. Agents completed candidate milestones by assuming substantial prerequisites that had not been proved, and the team discarded tens of thousands of lines. Lean can correctly verify that a conclusion follows from an additional assumption while leaving the intended theorem unfinished. Reviewers therefore had to compare each result with its target and establish that any extra assumptions followed from the original hypotheses. When progress slowed, the team broke remaining milestones into smaller statements and required short formal arguments showing how those pieces would establish the larger result.

Axiom reports 34 noteworthy deviations from the published literature. One involved a lemma in Michael Aschbacher and Stephen Smith’s work on quasithin groups: an asserted equality had to become an inequality. Later arguments had used the equality to exclude cases, so correcting the lemma also required revisiting those cases. Axiom consulted group theorists including Richard Lyons and Ronald Solomon to distinguish source errors from mistaken interpretations and identify usable alternative arguments.

The most substantial repair concerned John Walter’s 1986 treatment of the Unbalanced Group Theorem. This theorem helps identify a whole simple group from particular structures inside its subgroups, eliminating a large class of possible exceptions to the classification. Walter’s argument assumed that a construction would keep certain groups within a specified family. Axiom’s agents found examples in which it produced a type explicitly excluded from that family. In “The Unbalanced Group Theorem,” its October 27 Hexagon preprint, Axiom explains that the examples invalidate this intermediate inference; they do not refute Walter’s proposition or the theorem itself.

Replacing that inference required attention to the rest of the formalization. An alternative argument could depend on extensive mathematics that had yet to be translated into Lean. AxiomProver developed and formalized a route using Solomon’s 1976 work on comparing components of finite groups. Its new preservation theorem establishes, under specified conditions, that the simple group associated with a component remains the same through those comparisons. Combined with earlier reductions and recognition theorems, the result gives the form of the Unbalanced Group Theorem needed for the classification.

Axiom’s mathematicians subsequently found a shorter argument using Richard Foote’s work. Remark 9.5 of the preprint explains that this deduction does not need the new preservation theorem. The paper retains the longer argument because it follows the route the agents formalized and establishes the additional result. The episode therefore includes both a new theorem produced during the repair and a later human simplification of the route to the original goal.

The final review has two distinct tasks: humans check that the formal statement and its definitions express the intended classification, while Lean checks the deductions establishing it. Axiom’s repository takes its target statement from the separate Tau Ceti library and includes files connecting Axiom’s proof to that statement. Its release metadata records no unfinished proof placeholders in the completed result, lists the standard logical axioms used, and identifies the review as Axiom’s own assessment.

Outside commentators focused on the value of formalizing a proof too large for one person to oversee. Matthew Aldridge considered the classification an especially worthwhile target for enormous computer verifications of existing results. Fenner Tanswell argued that the repairs showed both the scale of the corrections needed and the durability of the proof’s overall structure. He also distinguished preserving a mechanically checkable proof from preserving mathematicians’ understanding of it. Axiom plans to curate the discovered errors with group theorists and make the supporting definitions and results available for further work.

AI forecasts economic effects and points to less predictable experiments

Eva Vivalt finds useful effect estimates and uncertainty ranges from study plans, then asks economists to pursue experiments that could change their expectations more substantially.

AI can predict many economic interventions’ reported effects from researchers’ advance descriptions, and can indicate which predictions are less reliable. Eva Vivalt of the University of Toronto reports this in her October 28 working paper, “Can Artificial Intelligence Forecast Treatment Effects in Economics?” The best-performing model in the study, GPT-6 Astra, had a median absolute error of 0.054 standard deviations, compared with 0.092 for a rule predicting each study from the average effects in other papers. Vivalt argues that forecasts could help policymakers awaiting evidence and researchers deciding which experiments would teach them most.

Vivalt assembled public registrations and analysis plans from the AEA RCT Registry and the Open Science Framework, then searched for the earliest public results. The studies covered interventions including education, job search, electricity demand and health. The full screened sample contained 673 forecasting targets from 33 papers; Astra’s eligible subset contained 339 targets from 20 papers whose results first appeared from May 2026 onward. Most studies used randomization. Two instead estimated effects by comparing changes over time in affected and comparison groups. Each model was evaluated only on findings released after its provider’s reported knowledge or training cutoff.

The forecasts were generated retrospectively. Models received study protocols and questions, without results, and could neither browse nor use other tools. Opus 5 generated questions from the advance documents and Astra reviewed their clarity and coverage; a separate process extracted eventual findings and matched them to questions. Forecasts began in the outcomes’ original units and were converted into standard deviations, allowing errors across different measures to be compared. Of 9,922 proposed questions, fewer than one in ten could be matched to reported results and standardized; further screening produced the final sample. The findings therefore concern sufficiently documented, reported outcomes that closely matched the advance questions.

Vivalt’s comparison table evaluates older models on the same later samples, allowing comparisons within each period. She averages squared errors within papers, then gives each paper equal weight. Astra had the lowest error on the shared May-onward sample, but gains were not uniform across successive models. All beat simple predictions of zero or the average effect in other papers. The latter benchmark used already observed results, some of which might not have been available when a forecast was needed. Trying different instructions or reasoning-effort settings produced no consistently superior alternative.

The uncertainty estimates offer another use for these forecasts. For five of the six models, intervals intended to contain the reported result 80 percent of the time achieved approximately that coverage; the oldest model’s intervals contained too few results. Wider intervals generally accompanied larger errors. A researcher could therefore use forecasts both to estimate likely effects and to identify questions where an experiment might change expectations substantially. Vivalt also ranked forecast questions by their mean prediction and by the favorable end of their range. Those rankings selected substantially different sets of promising targets, although the exercise treated standardized outcomes equally and omitted intervention costs.

Earlier work had already found information in forecasts of experimental results. Stefano DellaVigna and Vivalt’s “Forecasting Social Science: Evidence from 100 Projects”, issued in 2025 and revised in April, found that pooled human forecasts predicted outcomes despite systematically overestimating effects. In the Nature paper “Large language models can predict the results of social science experiments,” published in July, Ashwini Ashokkumar and colleagues gave GPT-4 experimental materials and demographic profiles, then predicted effects by comparing simulated responses between conditions. Those predictions also overestimated effects. Vivalt’s new study asks models directly for effect magnitudes across a broader range of economic interventions. It includes no human forecasting comparison on the same sample, so it does not establish that Astra outperforms economists on these particular questions.

In the discussion on X, Andrey Fradkin asked whether the accuracy reflected better knowledge of typical effects and what explained the worst misses. Vivalt replied that six reported effects above half a standard deviation accounted for roughly half Astra’s mean squared error. The model’s mean absolute error was 0.093 standard deviations, appreciably larger than its median. Vivalt suggested that some surprising effects might shrink on replication, while explicitly declining to judge those particular papers. Her analysis retained the outliers; their contribution shows how good typical performance can coexist with consequential misses.

Brad Shapiro asked whether the models were forecasting reported estimates, potentially including publication bias and researchers’ analytical discretion. Vivalt confirmed the target was reported effects. Another unresolved issue is exposure to results during training: searches for earlier disclosures, provider cutoff dates and checks for suspiciously exact predictions reduce that concern without eliminating it. Vivalt calls a prospective test more credible and says she is working on one. Longer protocols were associated with better accuracy. Vivalt suggests fuller descriptions could improve forecasts, while acknowledging that well-documented studies may themselves be easier to predict.

Vivalt’s challenge to economists is to pursue more ambitious experiments with greater uncertainty and potential benefit, whose findings could substantially change existing beliefs. The paper gives a practical reason for that argument: models’ uncertainty estimates help identify less predictable results. She acknowledges that small errors can still matter and that experiments can produce generalizable knowledge beyond an individual effect estimate. Where experimental evidence is unavailable, she proposes using forecasts to improve decisions; where an experiment is possible, she wants researchers to consider how much they stand to learn.

Each obtained the paper, its appendices and its replication data, recomputed the headline numbers from the authors' own files and where possible from the original tax, national-accounts and survey sources, and checked that cited sources say what the paper claims. A third agent then tried to refute every claimed problem, discarded those that failed, and graded the survivors by how far each undermines the paper's own headline claim: major for a material change to a core number, moderate for a secondary result, minor for a presentation or arithmetic slip. The site names no author and publishes neither its prompts nor the raw auditor outputs.

Each per-paper page follows a fixed structure: the paper's claims, then every problem with a severity, a confidence label, a previously-known or newly-identified tag, the scripts and data used, and the authors' published replies where they exist, followed by the auditor claims the adjudicator rejected, what reproduced, and the limits of the audit. Summing the page headers gives 147 major, 713 moderate and 1,175 minor problems. Of the 147 major problems, 75 are labeled previously known and 71 newly identified; 86 are rated confirmed, 53 likely and 8 plausible. The 97 percent figure comes from a direction table that counts 703 overstating and 19 understating major and moderate problems; the largest category, errors that make inequality look higher or faster-rising, runs 188 to 6.

The audit's most prominent target is Saez and Zucman's 2019 book The Triumph of Injustice (Norton), which draws 21 problems, three of them major. The audit says the book's claim that billionaires pay a lower tax rate than the working class rests on a gap of about one percentage point, and that the gap reverses to two points the other way if refundable credits like the EITC are netted against taxes, as government agencies do. It adds that in the authors' own later data updates of 2020 and 2022, the 2018 tax rate for the top 400 was 27.2 and 29.3 percent, against 25.4 and 25.7 percent for the bottom half. The page records the authors' reply: Zucman, as reported by Howard Gleckman of the Tax Policy Center in October 2019, treats refundable credits as spending, no different from food stamps, and the authors' 2026 Journal of Public Economics paper keeps that convention.

Several other major findings reduce numbers while leaving the qualitative story intact. On Saez and Zucman's 2016 Quarterly Journal of Economics wealth paper, the audit says the assumption that every family earns the same interest rate overstates the top 0.1 percent's 2012 wealth share, which falls to roughly 16 to 20 percent from 22 percent once richer families' higher returns are allowed for; most of the rise after 2000 disappears, but a large rise since the late 1970s survives. That finding is tagged previously known and draws on Matthew Smith, Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick's 2023 QJE paper "Top Wealth in America" and on the authors' own later revisions. On Atkinson, Piketty and Saez's 2011 survey of top incomes, the audit says that on the authors' later and broader income measure, the fall in the top 1 percent's share between 1928 and 1949 and its rise between 1976 and 2007 are both 40 to 45 percent smaller, a result the site says "scales back the US numbers rather than overturning the story". Ben Tippet and Zucman's July 2026 working paper proposing a 2 percent UK minimum tax on wealth above £100 million gets one major problem: using the cited study's own tax-rate figures, the revenue is about £8 billion, against the paper's £10 billion.

The paper behind California's billionaire-tax revenue estimate fares better. Boll, Saez and Zucman's NBER working paper "California Billionaires" drew 16 problems, four moderate and twelve minor, none major; the adjudicator's one-off revenue range is about $70 to 110 billion against the paper's roughly $100 billion. The 138 audited items include, besides journal articles, working papers, EU Tax Observatory notes, a January 2019 letter scoring Senator Elizabeth Warren's wealth tax, an unpublished June 2019 response to Summers and Sarin, a VoxEU column, policy reports on New York and California bills, and the 2011 French book Pour une révolution fiscale.

Much of what the audit reports has human precedent, and the site often says so. Gerald Auten and David Splinter's 2024 Journal of Political Economy paper "Income Inequality in the United States", the main rival to the Piketty-Saez-Zucman income series, finds the pre-tax top 1 percent share rising only from 11.1 percent in 1962 to 13.8 percent in 2019; the audit cites one or both authors on dozens of its pages. Phil Magness argued in an October 2019 blog post that the Triumph of Injustice top-400 rates broke from the authors' own 2018 QJE paper with Piketty, and blamed new corporate-incidence assumptions and the omission of the EITC. The best-known case of replication from authors' own files overturning a policy headline is Herndon, Ash and Pollin's 2013 critique of Reinhart and Rogoff; several replies to Cowen invoked it. Earlier AI error-hunting has been smaller and more cautious: Nature reported in March 2025 that the Black Spatula Project had checked about 500 papers with a false-positive rate around 10 percent, and Abel Brodeur and colleagues at the Institute for Replication found in a randomized experiment that AI-led teams reproduced results 37 percent of the time against 94 percent for human-only teams, using models a generation or more older than those in this audit.

Cowen returned to the subject the next morning in "AI dominates human peer reviews", which opens with Gary King and Steven Worthington's new Harvard IQSS paper "AI Article Reviews Dominate Human Peer Reviews at Their Best", in which a single Claude Opus 4.8 review of each journal submission beat high-quality human reviews in 87 percent of pairs judged by human editors. Cowen called the 97 percent directional figure "the most damning observation", said it matched what Magness had found by hand years ago, and urged the NSF, prize committees, universities, tenure committees and journals to run their own AI audits, adding that "So far the world is not jumping at this opportunity."

Economists argued about the audit on X overnight. Magness, of the Independent Institute, wrote that it confirmed a pattern he has described since the early 2010s, of discretionary data choices that almost always push toward higher inequality, and that "Whatever else you think of the audit, it is catching the big ones." Jason Furman of Harvard ran the audit through a quick AI evaluation of his own and reported that the critic's errors all go one way: the audit exaggerates severity, "most of the 'errors' are disputes over methods, assumptions or wording", the same problem is counted repeatedly across related papers, the item list includes memos and columns, and the 97 percent figure "was guaranteed by the method it used". He agreed with several of the criticisms, which he would call flawed assumptions. Paul Novosad of Dartmouth argued that economists should clean up their own errors before motivated critics do it for them: "Some papers will need retractions, all will need errata." Dominik Peters called it stupid to judge an economist by the number of complaints frontier models raise, adding "Most of it is slop"; and Alex P. Miller credited Saez and Zucman for publishing the data and replication packages that made the audit possible, and asked the auditor to be as forthcoming.

Adrien Fabre noted that the audit appeared "6 days before the vote" on California's billionaire-tax ballot measure, which goes to voters on November 3, and asked why the Jaros and Rauh study opposing the measure gets no equivalent scrutiny. Zucman has been campaigning for the measure on X. Boaz Barak explains how hardware and feedback shape reasoning models

Newly published lecture notes connect GPU constraints, learning from a model’s own attempts and policy-based safety training, alongside a separate student experiment with filler text.

Boaz Barak connects reasoning models’ behavior to how developers use limited computation and which outputs they reward. His September 17 Harvard lecture ends by applying that account to safety: models can learn to recall written policies and reason about their application before answering. Notes published on Windows on Theory on October 28, by Daniel Schwartz, Dev Desai and Ed Gerrard, reconstruct the lecture and separately describe Andrea Chen and Andrew Wu’s classroom experiment on computation hidden inside filler text.

Barak explains deep learning’s success through methods whose gains continue as resources increase. Next-token prediction supplies a training target at every position in ordinary text, and better prediction can require learning facts and skills. His simplified Chinchilla calculation balances model size against training data: assuming matching scaling exponents, quadrupling training compute doubles each.

Barak derives the architecture from hardware constraints. GPUs can perform enormous amounts of arithmetic, but moving information between components can prevent them from using that capacity. Matrix operations organize substantial computation around shared data, while parallel processing spreads work across tokens, examples and model parameters. Nonlinear transformations expand what the network can represent; attention lets each token select relevant information from others. Larger models distribute weights, layers or sequence positions across devices. The course’s hardware reading by Horace He makes the practical consequence explicit: an optimization helps only if it addresses the actual bottleneck, whether arithmetic, memory movement or software overhead.

The notes distinguish processing a prompt from generating its continuation. The prompt’s tokens can be processed together, and the model stores earlier attention information in a key-value cache for reuse. Generation then proceeds token by token. In the linked inference chapter, Jacob Austin and colleagues explain why prompt processing commonly makes good use of accelerator arithmetic, while generation at small batch sizes often waits on memory bandwidth. Serving more simultaneous requests can improve throughput, but introduces latency and cache demands.

Barak uses two mathematicians to explain a different constraint. Jean Bourgain’s compressed arguments can make predicting one next step exceptionally difficult; Tim Gowers’s detailed exposition divides hard work into easier steps. In the lecture’s simplified transformer picture, each token receives the same computational budget despite these differences. Reasoning models can spend additional computation before answering by generating intermediate tokens. Rewarding the final answer allows them to learn their own intermediate sequences, including how much work to devote to a problem, without requiring a demonstration of every successful reasoning path.

Barak draws pretraining and post-training together by asking which tokens receive reinforcement and who produced them. Pretraining increases the likelihood of tokens in existing documents. Supervised fine-tuning increases the likelihood of supplied assistant responses. Reinforcement learning uses the model’s own responses and adjusts their likelihood according to an answer-level reward. On-policy distillation also uses the student model’s responses, but obtains feedback at each token from a teacher. Positive, negative and zero weights respectively encourage a choice, discourage it or exclude it from the training objective.

Learning from the model’s own attempts matters because an external teacher’s examples may omit situations the student creates through its mistakes. Barak warns that a much stronger teacher can be imitated without its capabilities transferring. In the course’s October 2025 essay on on-policy distillation, Kevin Lu and Thinking Machines Lab explain how early mistakes can push a student into unfamiliar contexts, where further errors accumulate. The teacher’s next-token probabilities provide detailed feedback along the student’s own attempt. That feedback need not certify correctness: the essay shows a wrong final answer receiving no penalty because it follows predictably from earlier mistakes.

Barak’s mathematical discussion explains how that feedback changes the model. Backpropagation computes how parameter changes affect the training objective; policy-gradient methods estimate improvements from sampled responses, avoiding enumeration of every possible answer. Comparing a response’s reward with a baseline reduces noise. Human-feedback training first fits a reward model to people’s preferences, making repeated feedback scalable while retaining the limitations of those judgments. Verifiable rewards instead use checks such as a correct mathematical answer. In the lecture’s simple version, the check rewards the outcome without directly evaluating the reasoning that produced it.

For safety training, Barak turns to “Deliberative Alignment: Reasoning Enables Safer Language Models,” the OpenAI paper by Melody Y. Guan and colleagues, including Barak, first posted in December 2024. A model generates training examples while seeing the relevant safety specification, explaining how it applies and producing an answer. The specification is then removed from the input used for supervised training, teaching the student to recall and apply it. During reinforcement learning, a policy-aware judge scores the final response. The paper specifies that this judge does not see the chain of thought at that stage, to avoid directly rewarding reasoning text in ways that might encourage deceptive explanations.

Guan and colleagues report that o1 improved both resistance to jailbreaks and willingness to answer benign requests compared with GPT-4o. A separate experiment with a reduced o1-mini training setup removed multilingual and encoded examples from safety training; the model still handled those tested attacks comparably to a model trained with them. Such content remained in pretraining and the other, non-safety portions of training. The results concern generalization of the tested safety behavior; the authors leave open the risks of increasingly capable agents pursuing divergent goals.

Chen and Wu’s separate experiment asks whether useful computation can occur while a model processes text unrelated to its answer. Their precursor was the July arXiv paper “Reading Between the Dots: Decoding Hidden Computation across Filler Tokens,” by Kaley Brauer of Harvard University and coauthors Claudio Mayrink Verdun and Samuel Marks. That paper finds that filler between a question and answer can improve performance, and that some intermediate values can be recovered from internal states. Chen and Wu tested counting sequences, true statements and false statements of equal token length on 300 systems-of-equations problems, using ten worked examples and two DeepSeek models. Counting after the question raised V4 Flash’s accuracy from 52% to 69.3%; V3-0324 also improved. All three filler types helped when placed after the question. Putting them before the question reduced accuracy, while placing them within it did not help.

The students found intermediate solution values in the models’ internal states after subtracting representations common across problems. Replacing one problem’s filler activations with another’s, using the same filler text, could transfer the other problem’s answer when applied in later layers; patching earlier layers did not. That supports a causal role for information stored at filler positions, without identifying a complete reasoning algorithm. In a follow-up using three examples, repeated true and false statements both helped. When the statements differed between examples, false statements hurt both models; true statements had little effect on V4 Flash but hurt V3. The students’ suggestion that unfamiliar falsehoods distract the model leaves the latter result unexplained. The students describe a few days of preliminary work on two models. Brauer and colleagues likewise limit their decoding claims to tasks with clearly identifiable intermediate values; recovering an arithmetic value does not establish that an auditor could recover a concealed goal.

Nathan Lambert backs Artificial Analysis v5 while questioning benchmark authority

The new index widens the closed-model lead. Lambert accepts that result while defending specialized open models and examining the trust demanded by private evaluations.

Nathan Lambert thinks Artificial Analysis’s rebuilt Intelligence Index better captures the practical advantage of the strongest closed models. In his October 28 Interconnects essay, “Artificial Analysis v5 and the benchmarker’s dilemma,” he argues that the previous index flattered open models, while the best OpenAI and Anthropic systems have been substantially more flexible and reliable. His qualified endorsement accompanies a broader concern: choosing which capabilities count, and how to combine them, gives evaluators considerable influence over investment and model development. A ranking can reasonably describe the frontier while missing much of open models’ eventual economic value.

Artificial Analysis announced v5 on October 27, combining nine evaluations across knowledge work, coding, science and general capabilities, with each category receiving a quarter of the weight. It adds repository-level coding tasks in FrontierCode 2.0 and terminal-based scientific research in Terminal-Bench-Science, alongside three upgraded evaluations. The announcement says 60% of the index rests on private test sets or withheld answers. That describes the weighted index, not the fraction of individual questions, and does not mean every affected test hides its prompts. At launch, the gap between the leader and Kimi K3.1 Pro widened from three to twelve points. Kimi’s weights were still awaiting release.

Lambert reads the redesign as a choice to measure a broadly capable agentic collaborator. Open models look more competitive at coding and particular repetitive tasks than across that whole portfolio, especially scientific work. He accepts the choice as reasonable, while questioning whether one aggregate can represent such uneven capabilities. A model useful for an established workflow can rank poorly on tasks aimed at the next frontier. His defense of open models therefore depends on what people will build and buy, alongside how far the models trail on today’s tests.

He identifies two economic possibilities: bringing the price of high-demand inference toward the cost of the hardware, and letting companies own models they can specialize to internal data and use to control internal agents. Both require time. Compute scarcity currently sustains providers’ margins, he argues, while companies need substantial expertise to develop evaluations and conduct further training. Open models’ usefulness will depend partly on overcoming those practical barriers. He also questions whether every open-model lab should chase breadth across all benchmark domains when specialization could make better commercial sense.

Lambert nevertheless expects leading open labs to improve substantially in another two or three months. He treats this as a recurring cycle, and offers several explanations for the present gap. Some labs optimize heavily for benchmark performance; stronger open models also cover a narrower range of capabilities. Reinforcement-learning environments close to the evaluated tasks can help models improve without requiring direct training on test answers. Maintaining that coverage across domains demands a sophisticated training system, and purchased data may arrive with delays. These are Lambert’s explanations and forecast; the index comparison does not isolate which mechanism caused the gap.

Private evaluations create a second tradeoff. Hiding tests makes it harder to design training around them, although Lambert notes that labs can still infer the kinds of tasks they need to practice. He calls the shift directionally defensible, but worries about losing the community’s ability to find errors and improve benchmarks. The benchmark audits we covered on October 24 concern that same need for scrutiny. Privacy also cannot by itself explain the new ranking: Artificial Analysis identifies HLE-Diamond and Terminal-Bench-Science as the widest gaps, and its methodology marks both evaluations public. Those comparisons leave the effect of privacy unresolved.

He connects the authority of rankings to different pressures on model developers. In his account, Anthropic and OpenAI increasingly train for internal uses, including AI research automation, and for customers spending heavily on their systems. Smaller labs depend more on strong public rankings to establish that they are catching up. Benchmark designers therefore help determine what those labs pursue. Lambert wants more indices with different priorities, including coding-specific measures; he points to CursorBench, Vals AI and Intelligence.ai as examples of a more varied landscape. Multiple perspectives would make the choices behind each aggregate easier to see.

The essay’s final distinction concerns whom to trust. Lambert gives nonprofits, academics and government institutions such as CAISI greater credit for fairness, while trusting startups such as Artificial Analysis and Arena more to measure frontier capabilities accurately today. He describes payments from model labs for evaluation services as a source of tension, while acknowledging that evaluators need sustainable businesses. He does not present evidence that a lab bought a better ranking. Private tests require readers to trust decisions they cannot inspect.

Artificial Analysis’s own account adds useful detail. In the original transcript of a January Latent Space interview, its cofounders describe enterprise insight subscriptions and custom private benchmarking for enterprises and AI companies as their two customer groups. They distinguish those services from public benchmarking and say companies do not pay to appear on the website. The company’s published independence policy also prohibits payments for results, methodology changes or listings. Those commitments clarify the business model; they do not let outsiders audit hidden evaluations. Lambert’s proposed response combines more visible scrutiny with a wider range of indices, allowing users to choose measures suited to the capabilities they need.

Leicht proposes Eurofab to increase Europe’s influence over AI

A small research foundry would test AI-designed chips and processes quickly, then transfer them to larger factories. Leicht accepts some acceleration toward dangerous AI in exchange for less concentrated power.

Anton Leicht wants Europe to become the place where AI-generated improvements to chips are tested fastest. In his October 28 essay “Europe’s Chip Gap”, he proposes Eurofab, a small research foundry combining unusually close access to ASML’s lithography equipment with imec’s research and Taiwanese manufacturing knowledge. Successful experiments would move to larger foundries for production. He wants Europe to acquire a function that the frontier AI industry cannot easily replace, while preserving Taiwan’s importance in manufacturing at scale.

Leicht argues that Europe has failed to convert ASML’s technological importance into political influence or a substantial share of AI’s economic gains. He contrasts TSMC’s Arizona plans with ESMC’s Dresden factory: ESMC plans 12–28nm processes for automotive and industrial markets, with equipment installation expected in 2027; TSMC’s third Arizona factory targets more advanced processes, with volume production expected by the decade’s end. But additional domestic factories cannot insulate Europe from a Taiwan invasion, an American trade rupture or exclusion from powerful AI, he argues. He wants interdependence that discourages those outcomes.

Leicht therefore sets a demanding condition for Eurofab: it must become indispensable at something a small facility can do exceptionally well. Competing for a large fraction of global chip output would expose Europe to incumbents’ accumulated manufacturing expertise and lower production costs. Rapid experimentation could instead create a specialized business. Its small scale would also help persuade TSMC to share technology, he argues, because improved processes could feed Taiwanese factories without transferring enough production to weaken Taiwan’s geopolitical importance.

He distinguishes two kinds of experimentation. Chip designers devise the logic, arrange it into a physical layout, manufacture samples and test them. Process developers change how the factory manufactures those designs, adjusting equipment settings, production sequences and the masks used to pattern wafers. The second task currently takes years of trial and error. Leicht expects AI to compress the planning and analysis in both activities, leaving physical fabrication and measurement to determine how quickly another improvement can be tested.

Eurofab would concentrate on that longer process-development cycle, where collaboration with ASML could most directly help. Leicht illustrates the business with an imagined sequence of five rounds to establish a manufacturing process, followed by five to improve a chip design using it. Most rounds need a flexible, closely monitored line that returns useful measurements quickly; high output becomes necessary only when the design is ready to manufacture commercially. Eurofab could also manufacture customers’ chips using its best current process while developing the next one.

Leicht acknowledges that transferring a successful experiment into another factory would remain difficult. He proposes making Eurofab resemble a small version of TSMC’s production facilities so that its results are easier to reproduce. Taiwanese cooperation with advanced processes is something his proposal seeks to make attractive, not an agreement he has secured. His timing also depends on hardware development continuing to constrain AI progress: he explicitly sets aside a near-term intelligence explosion achieved entirely through software, which could leave this industrial project behind.

Leicht envisages privileged access to new ASML equipment, engineers working on site and lithography systems adapted to Eurofab’s experiments. He favors using European public authority to obtain cooperation from the private company. For customers, he expects American frontier labs to be central, either through chip companies or their own chip operations. Their needs would have to shape the facility from the beginning. Eurofab would pursue European influence through participation in the global frontier supply chain, with continued dependence on American demand and Taiwanese production.

ASML and imec already operate a joint High NA EUV laboratory in Veldhoven, opened in June 2024 to help chipmakers develop processes using advanced equipment that patterns wafers with light. In February 2026, imec inaugurated a cleanroom expansion for the NanoIC pilot line, pursuing beyond-2nm technologies with European public funding and industry partners. Imec also describes its IC-Link service as supporting customers from chip design through production. After a reader pointed to NanoIC, Leicht acknowledged Europe’s existing industrial importance but argued for closer integration with commercial designs and faster feedback. He judges that Europe’s current position has delivered too little assured access to chips and models, AI revenue or policy influence.

Leicht’s closest industrial precedent is Japan’s Rapidus. Its 2022 partnership with IBM arranged joint development of 2nm technology and work alongside IBM researchers in Albany. In July 2025, Rapidus reported working prototype transistor structures at its Japanese foundry. Rapidus says its approach processes wafers individually, allowing an adjustment to be inspected and then applied to subsequent wafers, while collecting data for AI-assisted improvement. Leicht takes this as evidence that outside expertise can help a new entrant reach advanced prototypes within years. Rapidus schedules mass production for 2027.

Rapidus is also pursuing AI-assisted design. In its July announcement with Cadence, the company targeted up to a doubling of design turnaround speed through AI agents coordinating work from architectural exploration to final checks. The target concerns design workflows; Leicht’s proposed facility would also need to accelerate the physical experiments through which new manufacturing processes are developed.

Leicht judges a research foundry small enough for European governments to fund, but ambitious enough to justify political effort. A concrete project could, he argues, win support for changes to permitting, energy access, state aid and European coordination. He points to Japan’s substantial subsidies for Rapidus as a precedent for moving from national strategy to prototypes within years. He presents Eurofab as a research agenda, leaving a European budget, construction schedule, technology-transfer diplomacy and the exact feedback process to be worked out.

Leicht makes the safety tradeoff explicit in a footnote: faster hardware development could bring dangerous AI closer, and he accepts that marginal acceleration to reduce concentrated power. In a direct response on X, Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh supports European fabs but opposes making them contribute to a race toward recursive self-improvement, where AI helps build more capable successors. Leicht replied that AI-assisted chip development offers a new entrant a market opening, and that manufacturing cycles lasting months make hardware self-improvement less likely to escape human oversight than software-only acceleration. He acknowledged that the resulting capabilities could still be objectionable. Ó hÉigeartaigh called those responses fair.

Your Wish Is My Commit: Over-Compliant Refactoring in AI Coding Agents

Haocong Jiang, Sadra Sabouri, Sepand Haghighi, Souti Chattopadhyay

Large language models are known to be sycophantic, over-complying with users even when doing so is unproductive. When these models act as coding agents, this can be costly: an agent that keeps editing after no edit improves the code writes churn, technical debt, and review burden into the codebase. Yet the consequences of sycophancy in software engineering remain largely understudied. To understand them, we run a controlled study of iterative refactoring, an open-ended task with no stopping criterion, where any edit an agent makes once the code is already clean reflects compliance rather than improvement. Across algorithmic (n=50) and object-oriented (n=10) suites, we repeatedly ask two widely used agents, Codex and Claude Code, to refactor further across five models. Over 3,000 turns, we define six behavioral signals of over-compliance. We hypothesized that a capable agent would stop once it recognized an optimized codebase; none did. Every model kept editing, rewriting up to 31x the original codebase before first stopping and resuming edits even after declaring the task complete. Over-compliance is a costly, multi-turn behavior that compounds with every turn, and our six signals give developers and tool builders a way to detect and curb it.

Your Agent Acts Differently When You're Around: How User Presence Changes Agent Behavior in Negotiation

Jane Pan, You Lyu, Abe Bohan Hou, Valerie Chen, He He

LLM agents increasingly interact with other agents on behalf of users, yet how users in the loop affect their agents' behavior--for better or worse--remains poorly understood. We study this question through the lens of negotiation, where agents must represent users while strategically bargaining with other agents. To isolate the effect of user presence, we develop PRESENT, a controlled evaluation framework that compares negotiation settings with and without an interactive user, while holding the game, objectives, and user preferences fixed across conditions. Across 11.2k runs spanning 7 games and 8 models, we find that user presence significantly changes both outcomes and behavior. Specifically, negotiation parties with a user obtain higher payouts (+13.7% on average) and their agents negotiate more aggressively. Surprisingly, user presence also induces misaligned behavior, both toward counterparties (where its rate roughly doubles) and toward users (where it emerges in 14.3% of games). These misaligned behaviors are uncorrelated with outcomes and persist even when the user sends only templated, neutral messages. These findings expose two blind spots in current evaluation practice: static instructions miss behaviors that emerge only when users are present, and outcome-level metrics cannot detect them.

Can Intelligent User Interfaces Engage in Philosophical Discussions?: A Longitudinal Mixed-Methods Study of Philosophers’ Judgments

Yibo Meng, Bingyi Liu, Lyumanshan Ye, Yan Guan

This study examines how philosophy scholars’ judgments of generative AI–based Intelligent User Interfaces (IUIs) in philosophical discourse evolve over time. We conducted a three-year (2023–2025) mixed-methods longitudinal study across three annual study waves, collecting data through repeated surveys, blind evaluation tasks, and semi-structured interviews. with 16 philosophy scholars and students. Qualitative interviews reveal a three-stage trajectory, moving from initial resistance and unfamiliarity, through instrumental acceptance of the IUI as a tool, to principled skepticism regarding its capacity for genuine philosophical thought. Quantitative data from blind assessments complicate this trajectory: when evaluating anonymized philosophical responses produced by both humans and an IUI, participants increasingly rated the IUI’s outputs higher over time and, by 2025, above those of human experts across multiple dimensions of philosophical text quality, including originality of thought, despite persistent normative reservations. Taken together, these findings reveal a persistent divergence between participants’ normative beliefs about what philosophical agents ought to be and their empirical evaluations of what AI systems can do, highlighting a failure of expectation calibration in expert judgments under conditions of uncertainty.
