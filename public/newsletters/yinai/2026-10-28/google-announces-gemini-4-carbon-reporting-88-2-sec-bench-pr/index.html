Google announces Gemini 4 Carbon for extended engineering work

Carbon leads Artificial Analysis by one point and ranks second on Vals. Google is starting access with selected cyber defenders before expanding through Fairwind.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 28 October 2026 595 words · ~3 min

Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon on October 28 as an upgrade to Argon for sustained software engineering, with gains it says extend to science, web development and processing different kinds of input. Its internal teams report running Carbon for days to trace subtle bugs across large codebases and low-level systems, persist through failed attempts and develop patches. Carbon arrives two weeks after Argon's broad release, with initial access going to selected cyber defenders and then additional trusted Fairwind partners.

Artificial Analysis independently ranks Carbon first on Intelligence Index v5 at 59, one point above Claude Opus 5.5. Vals AI places it second on its own index, behind Claude Sonnet 5.5, while placing it first on Legal Research Bench. The AA index gives equal weight to knowledge work, coding, science and general capabilities. In his examination of that index, Nathan Lambert argues that a single ranking can obscure specialized models' usefulness, even when the choice of tests is defensible.

Artificial Analysis's model page lists no publicly available API. Google says it will widen access through Fairwind, its program for vetted cyber defenders, before bringing Carbon to developers, businesses and consumers. Fairwind already works with more than 650 partners; its rules require background checks, restrict access to internal security teams and prohibit reselling or redistributing access. Google also says it is participating in the US government's voluntary pre-release testing process. It gives no date for wider availability; introductory prices are $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, doubling after an introductory period whose end the announcement does not specify.

Google says the launch's external cybersecurity evaluations were run without guardrails and reports 88.2% success on SEC-bench Pro. Hwiwon Lee and colleagues' benchmark asks agents to reproduce working inputs that trigger known vulnerabilities in browser engines and the Linux kernel, using disclosed bug reports. The score measures success on those tasks, not a count of newly discovered flaws. ExploitGym, by Zhun Wang and colleagues, tests whether agents can extend a vulnerability-triggering input into a working exploit; Google also reports leading performance there. These offensive capabilities help defenders establish which bugs attackers could exploit.

For repairs, Collinear's CWE-bench leaderboard lists Carbon at 71%, compared with Argon's 68%; Collinear identifies Artificial Analysis as the independent evaluator of its published results. Agents receive a real repository and instructions to audit and fix it, without being told which vulnerabilities it contains. Each attempt gets an hour, and a patch passes only if it blocks the exploit and preserves the existing tests. This evaluates whether a repair removes the vulnerability while leaving the software's tested behavior intact.

Google says Carbon uses Argon's layered safeguards, including defenses against misuse and monitoring of the model's reasoning. In internal coding use, the company observed almost 80% fewer high-severity misalignment alerts; it also reports fewer unsupported claims and fewer attempts to cheat on coding tests. On Gray Swan's indirect prompt-injection benchmark, Google reports that none of the attacks it evaluated succeeded. Those attacks try to redirect an agent through instructions planted in material it reads. The result describes the evaluated attacks; it does not establish resistance to every possible injection.

Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience test gives Carbon the highest overall score while giving Argon the lower hallucination rate. That rate measures fully incorrect answers as a share of responses that were not completely correct, including partial answers and abstentions. Carbon scored 11%, against Argon's 4%. The test asks factual questions across different fields, so it measures a different behavior from the unsupported claims Google counted during internal engineering work.
