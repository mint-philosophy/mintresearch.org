Semafor details staff pressure before Brockman’s June PAC reversal

Internal Slack exchanges show employees questioning OpenAI’s distance from Leading the Future; one source says their objections helped Brockman abandon the remaining $25 million of his pledge.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 3 October 2026 1389 words · ~7 min

In his October 2 Semafor investigation, Garrison Lovely reconstructs employee opposition before OpenAI president Greg Brockman abandoned a second $25 million donation to Leading the Future (LTF) on June 11. Staff questioned the company’s distance from the super PAC and warned that its tactics could drive researchers to Anthropic. One unnamed source familiar with Brockman’s decision says the objections contributed alongside his own misgivings about LTF’s campaign against an AI-safety advocate.

The cancellation became public on September 30, when Theodore Schleifer and Mike Isaac of The New York Times reported that Brockman and his wife, Anna, had dropped the remaining half of their $50 million pledge. The Wall Street Journal confirmed it that evening. The first $25 million was not reported returned; Brockman’s June message said he had “no plans to donate more at this time.” Semafor adds the internal exchanges leading to that decision. Employees’ messages were shared with the outlet on condition they be paraphrased, and OpenAI declined to comment.

The thread began on June 1, when Jason Kwon, OpenAI’s chief strategy officer, previewed for staff the blog post the company published that day. In it, OpenAI said it had given nothing to any super PAC and described the Brockmans’ support for LTF as personal; it disclaimed direction of LTF and visibility into its operations. Kwon closed his message by thanking employees who had pushed for a stronger statement, and more than 80 reacted approvingly. Within about an hour others were questioning LTF’s independence, pointing to its record of backfiring tactics and worrying that it was damaging OpenAI’s brand and its hold on senior researchers. One employee argued that the company’s political activity could push researchers toward Anthropic and slow OpenAI in the race to build AI that improves itself, a post that drew 24 “agree” reactions.

Two days after Kwon’s post, an employee who had at first been reassured flagged that Brockman’s money would be used to fund an anonymous X account the employee identified as an LTF sockpuppet, citing a post that compared Sam Altman to a caterpillar wearing the dismembered remains of its prey. Twenty-two colleagues replied “WTF.” That account, @JonathanDoomer, was one of two vendor-run meme accounts that Taylor Lorenz and Tyler Johnston traced to Build American AI, LTF’s nonprofit affiliate, on June 3; they also found that Kwon followed both. Build American AI responded that an outside vendor ran the accounts as parody.

The dispute flared again on June 11, when an employee posted Veronica Irwin’s Transformer article reporting that Nathan Leamer of Build American AI, which does not disclose its donors, had named OpenAI as one of four funders in a May 3 text. OpenAI and LTF deny that the company funded the groups. Just over an hour later Brockman posted in the thread for the first time. He denied that OpenAI backed Build American AI and said he had given it nothing, though colleagues later noted that LTF itself funds the group. He then apologized for the distraction LTF had become inside the company and said he had no current plans for further donations. The message drew 40 hearts, five checkmarks and three thank-you emojis.

Kwon answered critics in the same thread. Replying to a disappointed employee, he agreed that LTF’s activities were damaging OpenAI’s reputation and wished the company had said sooner which tactics it does not condone; he said LTF seemed to be adjusting, which he called “accountability in action,” and noted that Brockman had supplied only a fraction of its money. On September 14 an employee revived the thread with a line from Matthew Yglesias’s Slow Boring essay that day: no one who works in American politics professionally, Yglesias wrote, believes OpenAI’s line that it is not behind LTF. Yglesias urged concerned OpenAI staff to demand together that the company stop what he called its effort to chill federal regulation, and to quit if it refused.

Employee objections began well before June. WIRED reported in February that Brockman’s political giving had caused consternation inside OpenAI, and Transformer reported that employees pressed Chris Lehane, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, about the company’s ties to LTF at a tense meeting in May. When LTF launched in August 2025, the Journal reported that Lehane and Brockman had taken part in initial conversations that year about shaping industry-friendly policy, and that the network hoped to emulate Fairshake, the crypto-industry super PAC Lehane helped build. Lehane told WIRED in May that he is not involved in LTF’s day-to-day operations.

Semafor’s source said employee pushback was part of Brockman’s decision, along with qualms of his own that began when LTF targeted Alex Bores, a New York Assembly member and AI-safety advocate running for Congress. Brockman had expected the PAC to concentrate on supporting allied candidates, the source said, and gave LTF critical feedback at different points; the source had heard of no LTF attacks on candidates since Bores. An LTF affiliate spent more than $8 million against Bores in June’s Democratic primary in New York’s 12th district, which Micah Lasher won. Brockman said in a May interview, the Journal reports, that he did not agree with all of LTF’s spending decisions. The source described Brockman’s separate $25 million gift to Trump’s MAGA Inc. in September 2025 as causing relatively little employee trouble, despite expectations; WIRED’s February report had described consternation over his political giving.

Semafor reports that LTF has since given overwhelmingly to front-runners in congressional races, and that candidates it endorsed or implicitly supported now back the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act from Rep. Greg Casar of Texas and Sen. Bernie Sanders, which would pause advanced AI development and criminalize building superintelligence, OpenAI’s stated goal. LTF endorsed Rep. Val Hoyle of Oregon in May, and she is among the ten House Democrats cosponsoring Casar’s bill. Lasher, who benefited from spending against Bores, pledged on September 3 to co-sponsor it the day he is sworn in. Semafor also notes that OpenAI has turned its political spending toward the American Infrastructure Alliance, a coalition of AI firms, investors and unions that hopes to head off moratoriums on new data centers.

In its Room for Disagreement section, Semafor cites the Times’s reading that the reversal reflects OpenAI’s sensitivity to public backlash against AI. The Guardrails Alliance, whose employee donors we covered in July, told the Journal that internal and external pressure had made Brockman back out. Seven current and one former OpenAI employee gave more than $215,000 to the rival super PAC. Casar wrote on X that “Progressives have worked hard to make LTF as toxic as AIPAC.”

Lovely had questioned OpenAI’s claimed separation from LTF in a September 10 essay on his newsletter, Obsolete, covered here on September 11. In a section of the Semafor piece marked as his view, he argues that employee activism, which unsettled Facebook and Google a decade ago, has moved to San Francisco’s AI labs, where competition for researchers gives staff leverage and where they care more about the safety of what they build than about left-right politics. He argues that employees judged the politics better: the industry’s attempt to escape regulation may have invited a crackdown, while Brockman’s MAGA Inc. gift secured more goodwill from Trump. Repeating the argument of a May essay, he adds that researchers are racing to automate their own work, which will drive their bargaining power toward zero, so their leverage is greatest now. Lovely discloses that he has just published a book, also titled Obsolete, about the risks of machines that could replace all human labor.

Haydn Belfield made a similar argument in Activism by the AI Community: Analysing Recent Achievements and Future Prospects, a paper for the 2020 AAAI/ACM Conference on Artificial Intelligence, Ethics, and Society that Lovely does not cite. Reviewing campaigns such as the 2018 employee letter against Google’s work on Project Maven, signed by 3,000 staff before Google declined to renew the contract, Belfield concluded that their success came from a shared culture and the bargaining power of scarce AI talent, and warned that the balance could shift if the work came to need less skilled staff. OpenAI’s employees have used that leverage before: in November 2023, NPR reported, about 700 of the company’s roughly 770 staff threatened to leave unless the board resigned and reinstated Sam Altman and Brockman.
