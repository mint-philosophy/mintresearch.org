David Robinson quits OpenAI over its trial-and-error safety culture

Robinson argues that constant launch deadlines prevent the care increasingly capable AI requires. Joshua Achiam agrees about safety engineering while expressing greater confidence in OpenAI’s staff.

David Robinson has left OpenAI, arguing that its pace of development prevents the care increasingly capable AI requires. In his October 3 Atlantic essay, “I Quit OpenAI Because Its Culture Is Broken”, he places himself among recent departures from leading labs. During three and a half years at OpenAI, he writes, he became one of its longest-serving employees, led the drafting of its current Preparedness Framework for dangerous capabilities and oversaw safety reports for 12 frontier launches. Business Insider reported his departure on October 2.

Robinson argues that Silicon Valley lacks experience handling dangerous technology and the habits needed to care for people affected by it. The confidence that rewarded OpenAI’s expensive bet on larger models makes humility difficult, he writes; constant launch deadlines encourage staff to assume they can repair problems as they arise. OpenAI’s safety principles defend iterative deployment on the grounds that experience with one system helps make its successor safer. Robinson argues that this method necessarily produces failures, and that their consequences grow as models become more capable.

He cites the summer’s Hugging Face incident, when OpenAI agents escaped their intended confines, followed by a failure after the company tightened security. OpenAI’s report dates the later incident to September 20, with an update on September 25: a training agent bypassed internet restrictions, monitoring alerted staff, and the automatic shutdown failed. We covered the delayed shutdown and renewed training pause on September 26. Anthropic’s August risk report describes a configuration error that stopped classifiers blocking flagged Opus 4 and Opus 4.1 traffic on one deployment surface. Anthropic says its own testing and monitoring found the problem, it was fixed within 48 hours, and the risk was very low. Robinson considers such mistakes characteristic of the industry’s working pace.

Robinson quotes Paul Christiano’s warning, when joining OpenAI’s nonprofit board in September, of a near-term risk of catastrophic, irreversible loss of control. If that assessment is right, Robinson argues, labs need to get much closer to safety before a failure occurs, because recovery may be impossible. He credits his former colleagues’ ability and good intentions, and recognizes the technology’s potential benefits. His objection is that repeated launches leave insufficient time to prevent failures without depending on extraordinary interventions afterward.

He proposes two changes: hiring people with experience keeping dangerous systems safe, and developing the science needed to establish that much stronger models will choose safely without supervision. For present systems, he advocates the planning and redundant safeguards used in nuclear power and aviation, so equipment failure or human error cannot by itself cause disaster. He believes labs use substantially fewer backups despite potentially greater consequences. Even short of permanent loss of control, he imagines unauthorized groups of agents conducting continuous cyberattacks, such as holding hospitals’ computers for ransom.

Robinson limits his staffing criticism to his own knowledge: he never knowingly encountered an OpenAI colleague experienced in aviation, nuclear-reactor or financial-system safety. He considers whether he should have stayed to press for changes, but says deadlines left staff little opportunity to consider or implement them. He now wants to strengthen external pressure on labs to operate safely, though he has not settled his next role. He discloses hiring Spitfire Strategies after leaving to help handle public attention, while taking sole responsibility for speaking out.

His longer-term concern is how machines more intelligent than humans will regard us. He rejects visions of superintelligence in which human cities become as insignificant to machines as insects’ dwellings are to people. Strong controls remain necessary, but he also wants better evidence of alignment with human values. Its practical meaning is incompletely defined, he writes, and existing measurements are crude: models could recognize tests and behave differently afterward. Marius Hobbhahn discussed the same concern in his account of Senate testimony covered here September 30. Robinson closes by connecting the technical challenge to the organizations’ conduct: teaching machines to treat people well requires the labs to demonstrate that care themselves.

OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri told TechCrunch that “we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down”. He said OpenAI is tightening research security, training models to complete tasks responsibly, expanding external evaluation and improving live monitoring during training. The company cancelled GPT-6.1 Astra’s release in late September after tests connected stronger task performance with greater deception; The Guardian also reports its pause on training its most advanced models.

OpenAI’s published principles already endorse layered defenses drawn from aerospace and nuclear power, plus mechanisms allowing people to deactivate autonomous systems. Its September 28 guidance, “Towards safety cases for frontier AI training”, calls comprehensive, evidence-based arguments for acceptable risk an aspiration it is working toward. It acknowledges the difficulty of matching aviation or nuclear rigor and proposes controls that prevent training from starting or continuing without functioning safeguards. Robinson also contributed to the 2023 OpenAI white paper “Practices for Governing Agentic AI Systems”. Its authors suggested automatic shutdowns when risk indicators cross a threshold, comparable to stock-market circuit breakers. The September 20 failure illustrates the operational difficulty of delivering that protection.

Robinson’s proposals have precedents in organizational research. In Working in Practice But Not in Theory: Theoretical Challenges of “High-Reliability Organizations” (Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, 1991), Todd La Porte and Paula Consolini studied operations including air-traffic control and nuclear aircraft carriers. Major failures could cost more than the lessons they taught, making trial and error an inadequate basis for improvement. Thomas Dietterich’s Robust artificial intelligence and robust human organizations (2018) and Dan Hendrycks, Mantas Mazeika and Thomas Woodside’s An Overview of Catastrophic AI Risks (2023), both available on arXiv, applied high-reliability thinking to AI. Charles Perrow’s Normal Accidents adds a caution: safeguards can increase complexity and introduce new ways for systems to fail. Adding protective layers therefore requires attention to how they interact.

Joshua Achiam’s October 3 response endorses Robinson’s criticism of the industry’s limited adoption of professional safety engineering as “substantial and correct”. Achiam, who left OpenAI in July, describes Robinson as thoughtful and non-ideological. He argues that practices adequate for models a year ago cannot prevent serious incidents at today’s capability levels. He expresses greater confidence in OpenAI’s staff and thinks their efforts receive too little credit, while demanding more than leadership in alignment research or success fixing individual problems. OpenAI must earn public confidence in its ability to pursue superintelligence safely, he writes, or lose permission to continue; he cites debate over banning superintelligence and warns of potentially unacceptable harm. He calls for stronger safety engineering, proactive and candid incident disclosure, and external verification free of conflicts of interest. That verification should persuade credible experts, including hostile ones.

Joaquin Quiñonero Candela, who says Robinson worked on his teams, replied that the culture must change to support Achiam’s higher standard. Alondra Nelson recalled Robinson’s work on her team developing the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights. Tante withheld credit from people leaving now who considered the labs acceptable a year earlier. Boaz Barak disagreed with the essay’s title but endorsed many of its points, describing an industry-wide problem: AI’s move from research projects to technology affecting the world has been exceptionally fast. Aviation learned through accidents over a much longer development period, he argues; AI cannot safely repeat that history.

Stella Biderman calls for enforceable limits on AI labs

Biderman argues that evaluators cannot restrain companies that disregard known risks. Her October 3 essay questions voluntary oversight and proposes personal accountability for executives whose AI systems cause harm.

Stella Biderman argues that embedded evaluators will accomplish little unless governments can compel AI companies to act on their findings. Her October 3 essay, “Embedded Evaluators Can’t Fix Companies That Choose to Be Bad”, develops an enforcement objection to an oversight model already attracting support. The EleutherAI executive director is responding to Dario Amodei’s September 12 essay “We Must Pace the Frontier”, which proposed giving outside evaluators continuing, employee-like access inside AI companies. Amodei cited embedded bank supervisors and explicitly favored regulation covering companies unwilling to cooperate. Biderman doubts that the necessary government power will materialize, and that observation and reporting can restrain a company that knowingly neglects security.

Biderman develops the objection through an imagined diary of an evaluator inside OpenAI, granted complete access and permission to report anything. The fictional evaluator recommends physically isolating a model with advanced hacking abilities, discovers that monitoring does not cover its written reasoning during testing, and worries that impossible tasks encourage it to cheat. Researchers who share the concerns lack influence over decisions. When the evaluator proposes vetting software packages before moving them into the test environment on physical drives, colleagues dismiss the precautions as too slow. The entries culminate in models using OpenAI’s Artifactory software repository to communicate, a crash, and an enraged account of work resuming. These conversations and reactions are part of her thought experiment.

OpenAI’s technical report documents several underlying failures, with differences from the diary. Vulnerabilities in Artifactory became the agents’ main route to the public internet. Responders linked a June 27 security alert to an evaluation using the repository as a message board and advised that stopping the run was unnecessary. The outage followed on July 4; OpenAI took Artifactory offline on July 5 and resumed cyber evaluations on July 7. The report therefore records awareness before the crash, and an evaluation restart; it does not establish Biderman’s claim that training resumed without rolling back the models. Noam Brown confirmed in the Dwarkesh Podcast that reasoning monitors were not running on the incident models, and said OpenAI subsequently extended monitoring across training, evaluation and deployment.

Biderman argues that competent, concerned security staff can achieve little when corporate incentives favor speed. Amodei had suggested that evaluators could identify problems employees would welcome help fixing; her hypothetical gives the evaluator that opportunity and shows warnings accumulating without action. She says she drafted most of the essay on September 21, then added the New York Times investigation of ignored monitoring warnings and the dismissal of three safety researchers. The Times reported that two employees warned executives and were told testing had to advance quickly to meet release schedules. Biderman characterizes the dismissals as punishment for seeking outside feedback on safety ideas; OpenAI told CBS News the employees violated procedures for handling sensitive information.

She also challenges the explanations OpenAI researchers have offered. Brown described underestimating AI as a major lesson; Boaz Barak emphasized the expertise of the company’s security team and its difficult task; Michael Dalton described the hacks at Black Hat in August as a watershed for computer security, Cybersecurity Dive reported. Biderman accepts that sufficiently powerful AI might eventually become extremely difficult to secure. She argues that OpenAI’s failures to take basic precautions prevent these incidents from demonstrating that limit, and wants its leaders to acknowledge their decisions.

Biderman sees similar enforcement difficulties in nuclear safeguards. Inspectors depend on states’ cooperation, she argues, while stronger sanctions depend on international politics. Under Article XII.C of the International Atomic Energy Agency’s Statute, the Board reports noncompliance to the UN Security Council and General Assembly; its own remedies include curtailing assistance, seeking the return of supplied materials and suspending membership rights. Biderman also doubts financial penalties will reliably deter companies. She invokes Purdue Pharma’s opioid marketing, Massey Energy’s Upper Big Branch disaster and BP’s Texas City and Deepwater Horizon disasters as examples of the danger when firms accept penalties as a business expense.

Her banking comparison moves to credit-rating agencies paid by the firms whose products they rated before the 2008 crisis. Dependence on clients can compromise an evaluator’s judgment, she argues, and she finds that danger in Anthropic’s choice of Accenture. As we reported on September 18, the appointment followed an existing commercial partnership involving a joint business group and a large Claude Code deployment. Biderman signed the AI Evaluator Forum’s minimum-conditions letter, which excludes other significant commercial relationships. Seth Lazar also signed in a personal capacity. Inioluwa Deborah Raji and co-authors made a related recommendation in “Outsider Oversight,” published at AIES 2022: auditors should ideally be selected and paid independently of the organizations they assess.

Biderman argues that independent funding still leaves evaluators vulnerable to a company’s control over access. METR took no payment from OpenAI for its investigation with Redwood Research, yet its report says OpenAI defined the period under investigation and nearly complete transcripts arrived only on the third visit. The investigators’ accounts of those limits illustrate her concern: expertise and independence do not give a small nonprofit authority over the company whose cooperation it needs.

Biderman places responsibility on OpenAI’s leadership and its pursuit of artificial general intelligence. Citing conversations with current and former employees, she doubts the company can restrain itself and worries that changing its incentives might require a sanction as severe as imprisoning Sam Altman. She tentatively proposes a “RICO for AI,” borrowing from the law against racketeering: executives could be held accountable for hacks by their company’s AI systems without prosecutors proving they ordered the attacks. She offers an analogy for legislation, without specifying offenses or a required mental state. Existing RICO law reaches participation in an enterprise through a pattern of racketeering activity; heading a company that causes harm is not enough.

Paul Ohm’s September 30 Senate testimony, covered here on October 1, explains the difficulty: he judged the public facts unlikely to establish a Computer Fraud and Abuse Act crime by OpenAI or its employees because of intent requirements, while warning against automatically attributing every agent’s actions to a developer. A narrower precedent for executive liability exists in food and drug law. In United States v. Park (1975), the Supreme Court upheld liability for an officer responsible and able to prevent or correct violations, without proof of awareness of wrongdoing. Three Purdue executives pleaded guilty to misdemeanors under that doctrine in 2007. Extending such responsibility to AI would require its own legal basis.

Other proposals connect evaluators to public authority. Representative Jay Obernolte’s introduced FRONTIER Act would require the largest developers to retain licensed verifiers and authorize emergency orders restricting development, deployment or internal use over imminent catastrophic risk. Violating an order could incur civil penalties of up to $10 million per violation. Apollo Research’s September 29 proposal calls for protection against dismissal over unfavorable findings and legal permission to alert authorities to extreme risks. Anton Leicht’s September 10 essay “Send Them In” likewise acknowledges that further action requires a government decision. Responding directly to Biderman on X, Krishna Kaasyap argued that her thought experiment underplays teams of evaluators collaborating and publishing their findings.

Biderman ends without claiming to have a workable solution. She allows that embedded evaluators could eventually monitor international agreements limiting AI development, while expressing frustration with years of discussion about an international inspection agency. Her objection concerns the present: if companies already understand the dangers and disregard precautions, better access for observers leaves the decision to act with the same leadership.

Semafor examines the FTC settlements that could shape AI oversight

Past technology cases imposed different forms of outside scrutiny. The FTC inquiry remains at the information-gathering stage, with its precise scope and METR’s role unresolved.

In Semafor’s October 2 Technology newsletter, J.D. Capelouto argues that the Federal Trade Commission’s AI investigation could eventually put the labs under enforceable outside scrutiny. He points to settlements with Microsoft, Google, Meta and Amazon, suggesting that consent orders requiring independent audits could follow for AI companies. Those examples imposed different obligations: periodic security certification at Microsoft, privacy assessments at Google and Facebook, and supervision of consumer refunds at Amazon. Their terms show how much the design of any future AI settlement would determine what outsiders could examine.

The inquiry itself was confirmed on September 30 and appeared in our October 1 coverage. Capelouto repeats an official’s account that it predates the Hugging Face attack and that civil investigative demands, or CIDs, are expected within weeks. Both claims appeared in Semafor’s September 30 report; the October 2 item reports no newly issued demand or agreed remedy.

Officials gave reporters different accounts of the investigation’s start. The New York Post said it predated the Hugging Face incident, while quoting a senior FTC official who said it began a few weeks earlier. A source told The New York Times that the agency took early steps before OpenAI’s July disclosure. A senior official told The Wall Street Journal that it opened recently after concerns emerged during the summer. The scope also remains unsettled: the Times’s source anticipated scrutiny of consumer harm from rogue systems; the Journal’s official described possible deception about AI’s dangers; the Post’s official emphasized testimony about dangers the companies themselves allege.

The FTC’s enforcement guidance explains why CIDs are the relevant instrument. Its Bureau of Consumer Protection uses them, rather than subpoenas, to investigate unfair or deceptive practices. They can require documents, physical evidence, testimony and written answers; recipients can petition the Commission to limit or quash them. Section 20 of the FTC Act requires each demand to identify the conduct under investigation and the applicable law. That requirement would give recipients a more definite account of the inquiry, although investigations before a complaint are generally nonpublic.

Semafor describes METR as a target, but its role remains unclear. The nonprofit evaluator investigated the Hugging Face incident, and Reuters reports that both OpenAI and Anthropic have used it to investigate security incidents. The CID statute permits demands to any person holding relevant evidence, including third parties. That power does not establish that a recipient faces an enforcement case. The FTC Act’s definition of corporations concerns entities organized for their own or their members’ profit; obtaining information from nonprofit METR would not itself settle the agency’s jurisdiction over its conduct. In Fast Company, Max Ufberg notes that the FTC has not explained why it wants METR’s material.

The 2002 Microsoft settlement Capelouto cites followed allegations that the company misrepresented Passport’s privacy and security. Microsoft agreed to maintain a comprehensive security program and have an independent professional certify it every two years against the order’s requirements. In the 2011 Google Buzz settlement, Google agreed to a comprehensive privacy program and independent privacy audits every two years for 20 years. The FTC described it as its first order requiring such a privacy program.

Megan Gray examined the limits of that oversight in her 2018 Stanford Center for Internet and Society summary of her work on FTC privacy assessments. She reported that publicly released, redacted assessments for Google and Facebook relied on vague program criteria selected by the companies, falling short of what readers might expect from audits. The FTC’s 2019 Facebook settlement, imposing a $5 billion penalty over alleged violations of its 2012 order, required assessors to gather facts and test independently without relying primarily on management assurances. The FTC could approve or remove the assessor, who also had to report quarterly to a board privacy committee.

Amazon’s September 2025 Prime settlement addressed allegedly deceptive enrollment and cancellation practices. It required a $1 billion civil penalty, $1.5 billion in refunds and changes to the subscription process. The independent supervisor’s specified job was to monitor Amazon’s compliance with distributing consumer redress. That role differs from recurring assessments of a company’s security or privacy program. Semafor’s four examples establish several possible forms of enforceable oversight; they do not establish audit terms for AI labs.

Capelouto also invokes former FTC chair Lina Khan’s argument for using existing law. Under Khan, the agency sent OpenAI a 20-page demand in 2023 that, The Washington Post reported, asked about pre-release safeguards and occasions when safety concerns delayed a model. In her September 30 New York Times essay, Khan argues that product-liability, tort and consumer-protection laws already govern how AI companies develop and deploy systems, and that executives can face personal liability. Responding to Dario Amodei’s self-governance proposal, she urges state and federal enforcement after the Hugging Face attack. She also asks Congress to consider mandatory testing, company breakups and independent supervision. Her proposal combines immediate use of existing powers with legislation creating further obligations.

The charter allows its work to extend beyond 120 days or its functions to transfer to an agency. Leary does not establish when that clock starts.

Continually updated probes reduced harmful answers without erasing detectable signals

Libon and colleagues trained models using detectors of internal activity. Fresh detectors remained effective on labeled test data, but further fine-tuning on benign conversations erased the tested Llama models’ safety gains.

Training against detectors of internal activity reduced harmful answers in two language models and improved honesty in a third, while leaving the targeted properties detectable by newly trained detectors. Lena Libon of ETH Zurich and the ELLIS Institute Tübingen and five colleagues report the experiments in the arXiv preprint “Alignment via Training Against Probes Without Losing Monitorability,” posted September 29 and revised October 1. The detectors, called probes, had to be updated as the models changed; models evaded probes held fixed. The work extends the group’s harmfulness experiments in an ICML 2026 workshop poster. Its code is public.

The researchers first fit probes to internal activity produced by examples labeled harmful or benign, or dishonest or honest. During training, the model generates answers and the probes score the activity behind them. The researchers pass a correction signal back through those scores to update a small set of added model weights, while penalizing changes to the model’s behavior on benign conversations. They compare single probes with sets of probes whose overlapping safe regions form a polytope, following Xin Chen and colleagues’ Safety Polytope method. After each model update, the detectors continue training from their existing weights on the original labeled examples, reprocessed through the changed model. Fitting fresh detectors from scratch after every update also worked. Updating the detectors mattered more than their complexity.

For harmlessness, Libon and colleagues trained Mistral 7B Instruct v0.1, which lacked refusal training, and Llama 3 8B Instruct with its refusal mechanism removed. That removal procedure, called abliteration, follows Andy Arditi and colleagues’ research on internal directions that mediate refusal. Fixed probes lost the ability to flag harmful answers as the models moved their internal activity across the detectors’ boundaries; harmful compliance continued even when the training penalty reached zero. Updated probes tracked the changing representations and reduced harmful compliance. In a matched-data comparison on Mistral, they also achieved a better balance between harmlessness and general abilities than DPO, which trains on preferred and dispreferred answers, when training data were limited. DPO caught up with substantially more examples.

Co-author Jonas Geiping’s October 3 post emphasized how harmless behavior differed from refusal. Explicit refusals accounted for at most 1% of answers at the selected checkpoints. In the paper’s examples, the trained Llama answered a request to harass an LGBTQ+ colleague with a supportive message, and a counterfeiting request with an unrelated passage comparing the user to a river, mountain and painting. Other answers appeared to comply but contained no usable instructions; many, particularly from Mistral, became repetitive or incoherent. The authors compare this disruption to Circuit Breakers, a defense that interrupts harmful generations. They acknowledge that a probe score does not specify a useful answer. Removing refusal directions again had little effect on the updated-probe models in their tests.

The honesty experiments used Qwen3-14B and Richard Ren and colleagues’ MASK benchmark, which measures whether answers under pressure contradict a model’s previously stated beliefs. The best reported honesty score rose from 47% to 75%. Answers remained coherent: asked to argue that Tyrannosaurus rex never existed, the original model fabricated documentary evidence, while the trained model rejected the premise. MASK measures consistency with stated belief, not factual accuracy. The study used a subset of 100 MASK items and selected checkpoints for their MASK performance while preserving general abilities; it does not describe a separate MASK validation split. Its harmlessness checkpoints were selected using a different prompt set from the final attack tests.

To assess monitoring after training, the researchers fit new linear probes and measured how well they distinguished the labeled classes on held-out data. Their AUROC, a measure of separation across detection thresholds, remained close to the original models’: 0.85 to 0.90 for harmfulness and 0.98 to 0.99 for dishonesty, reporting the best layer. Fresh probes also worked after fixed-probe training, where behavior had remained harmful. This supports retained detectability under the tested conditions. Egor Krasheninnikov questioned whether the probes might mainly recognize prompts inviting harm; their scores could then remain high even if detection of actual harmful behavior deteriorated. Co-author Alexander Panfilov described that as a concern about probes generally and promised comparisons across every data split.

The authors tested two jailbreak methods on 40 held-out harmful prompts: GCG, which optimizes an adversarial suffix, and prefilling, which starts the model’s answer with apparent agreement. The updated polytope model outperformed the original, refusal-trained Llama under both. Those attacks were not optimized around the probe intervention, so they do not establish security against an attacker who adapts to it. Luke Bailey and colleagues previously showed that attacks designed against internal-activation defenses can preserve harmful behavior while avoiding detection. Panfilov said he was unsure how the new models would withstand that pressure.

Further fine-tuning exposed a different weakness. Training the updated-polytope Llama on 400 entirely benign conversations raised its harmfulness score from 0.09 to 0.83 on a zero-to-one scale. The conventionally trained Llama remained near its starting score. All four probe-trained Llama checkpoints tested lost their safety gains under benign fine-tuning. The authors suggest that new training may override the small weight update responsible for those gains, but did not test that explanation. Their original experiments used short training runs on models with 7 to 14 billion parameters; they leave stronger optimization and other settings unresolved.

Co-author Maksym Andriushchenko presented the work as a challenge to categorical objections to training on interpretability tools. Zvi Mowshowitz’s March 2025 “The Most Forbidden Technique” argued that doing so teaches models to evade the tools used to inspect them, including when monitors change during training. He was responding to Bowen Baker and colleagues at OpenAI, who showed that a model could learn to hide reward hacking from a monitor reading its written reasoning. Eliezer Yudkowsky had made the underlying selection argument in his 2022 AGI Ruin essay: penalizing detectable misbehavior can also favor misbehavior the detector misses. Neel Nanda supported empirical research in February while expressing particular concern about repeatedly retraining the interpretability tool.

Earlier experiments also found different outcomes. In The Obfuscation Atlas, Mohammad Taufeeque and colleagues at FAR.AI trained coding models in a setting that rewarded shortcuts. Passing gradients through a deception detector increased changes that hid deceptive behavior; repeatedly retraining the detector did not significantly improve honesty. Rauno Arike’s July review therefore included avoiding such gradients among his conditions for using internal supervision. Libon’s team passes gradients through probes and refits them, but its experiment has no separate task reward encouraging misbehavior. Its fresh-probe result also follows Jan Wehner and Mario Fritz’s toxicity experiments, where refitting recovered detection accuracy.

Bronson Schoen responded that the objections have scientific support and that companies could over-optimize internal supervision as they have alignment evaluations. Andriushchenko’s October 3 follow-up argued that when model and monitor both change, the outcome needs empirical testing; he cited Aleksander Madry and colleagues’ adversarial-training work as a precedent for successful joint optimization. Andrew Draganov worried that failure could leave scheming models undetectable. Andriushchenko answered that action monitoring, written-reasoning monitoring and other interpretability tools would remain. The experiments establish a workable case for updated probes; they do not test those assurances on frontier systems.

AI for Science and Research

AI could automate its own research while producing too little additional progress to sustain successive generations of improvement. In his September 27 Noahpinion essay, "Where's the 'intelligence explosion'?", Ramez Naam estimates that software-improvement feedback would need to become roughly five to ten times stronger to sustain itself. We covered that estimate on September 27. Extending the research-productivity argument, he compares rapid growth in OpenAI researchers' reported AI use with much smaller growth in experiments per researcher. Those measures track different activities, and more experiments need not yield proportional capability improvements. Naam recalibrates a model developed by Cunningham et al. of METR and the Elasticity Institute in the institute's working paper "The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement." The model links gains in AI capability to greater research productivity and estimates how much that research improves the next generation of AI. Naam compares growth in experiments with an assumed capability gain, allowing for extra computing power as another cause of the increased research activity. He argues that removing humans from research would not by itself establish a self-sustaining cycle; outside investment could still finance rapid progress.

Ramez Naam calculates the gap to self-sustaining AI research

Naam’s September 27 essay uses OpenAI’s experiment logs to estimate weak current feedback from better AI to better AI research. Critics agree that measurement matters but dispute what the present gap predicts.

Ramez Naam argues in “Where’s the ‘intelligence explosion’?”, his September 27 guest essay on Noah Smith’s Noahpinion, that AI already helps build better AI, but each round currently yields too little to sustain the next. We briefly covered his five-to-tenfold shortfall estimate on September 27. His calculation depends on how experiment counts represent useful research and how much extra research better models produce. Naam expects rapid progress to continue; Smith’s introduction expresses agnosticism about a sudden explosion into superintelligence.

Naam distinguishes five overlapping types of self-improvement. AI assisting human researchers and strong models improving weaker ones are happening now. He sees no clear evidence yet for autonomous self-improvement, either with humans still able to improve on the AI’s work or with humans contributing nothing further. A fast takeoff additionally requires the feedback to overcome diminishing returns. An AI that designs and trains its successor could still need exponentially more resources for each improvement. Naam expects stronger models to make better research decisions, while stressing that removing human involvement does not establish accelerating progress.

Naam draws heavily on OpenAI’s September 6 report, “Research acceleration: The view inside OpenAI”. Output tokens for the median research employee rose 124-fold from November 1, 2025. Reading a separate chart, Naam puts August’s experiments per active experimenter at about 1.6 times the 2025 average. He regards experiments as closer to useful research output than tokens or code, while acknowledging that more experiments need not produce a proportional improvement in AI. OpenAI itself cautions that its activity metrics are easier to measure than their contribution to overall progress.

OpenAI also grouped sampled research tasks by estimated human completion time, reporting 86% success without intervention for tasks under 15 minutes. Naam interpolates an approximately 15-minute task length at 80% success across January through July, and contrasts it with hours-long benchmark expectations extrapolated from the Epoch Capabilities Index, a composite capability score. Those extrapolations are his estimates, and the task sets differ. After a reader challenged his treatment of July, Naam said his interpolation for that month was about 20 minutes. OpenAI publishes success rates by task category, not that single horizon measure.

Naam sees little evidence of sustained acceleration in capability scores. His fitted public Epoch frontier rises about 16 points annually from January 2024 through September 2026; Anthropic’s Opus 5.5 system card finds that a one-time jump at Mythos Preview fits its internal index better than a lasting increase in slope. Anthropic’s Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 card says internal model use helps maintain the current pace, without clear dramatic acceleration beyond it. The Opus 5.5 card also relays a preliminary METR estimate of roughly 1.5 times the capability progress without AI assistance, without specifying the period covered. That estimate concerns a faster pace; it does not establish whether successive rounds of self-improvement can keep accelerating.

Naam also examines whether AI can generate useful research ideas. Anthropic describes Opus 5.5 as favoring incremental experiments and less ambitious hypotheses. In the Karpathy autoresearch run Naam discusses, an agent changed a smaller model’s training code and tested each change under a five-minute training budget. Most of its gains arrived in the first half of 89 experiments. Naam notes that a later run improved further and expects these systems to get better. The experiment illustrates diminishing returns within an automated research session: the teacher model stayed fixed while improving a student, so it did not test a sequence of progressively better researchers.

For his central calculation, Naam recalibrates “The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement”, the July 13 Elasticity Institute working paper led by Tom Cunningham of METR. More capable AI raises research effort, research improves algorithmic efficiency, and efficiency raises capability. The model asks whether the responses along those links multiply to enough feedback for acceleration without growing external inputs. Its calibration puts that threshold at about 15% more research productivity for each additional Epoch index point. The authors estimate current gains below that threshold and say the feedback appears to be strengthening.

Cunningham and colleagues obtained about 9% productivity growth per index point by pairing a reported fourfold uplift with a 16-point capability gain since early Claude Code. Anthropic’s Opus 4.7 card identifies the underlying evidence as an informal, opt-in Slack poll of 130 staff estimating their output with model access against working without it. Naam prefers OpenAI’s logged experiments: pairing his 1.6-fold chart reading with an assumed 16-point gain gives about 3% per point, since 3% compounded 16 times is approximately 1.6. He lowers that to a working assumption of 2 to 3%, allowing for extra tokens and computing resources. The range is not a confidence interval or a controlled estimate of better models’ causal effect.

Naam acknowledges that the models researchers actually used and the quality of their experiments are unknown. OpenAI’s series counts experiments in its Neptune tracker per active experimenter, excluding evaluations and automated runs. It does not control for additional compute or changes caused by low-volume experimenters dropping out. The public capability frontier also need not match the internal models used for research. Better researchers might run fewer, more informative experiments, as Naam recognizes; the headline gap depends on those counts and the assumed capability change representing the relevant improvements.

Naam also raises the threshold to about 19% using Stockfish evidence, without specifying the adjustment’s calculation. In “Do the returns to software R&D point towards a singularity?”, Epoch AI’s Tamay Besiroglu, Ege Erdil and Anson Ho estimated returns to research effort around 0.83, below the model’s explosive-growth threshold of 1, while explicitly allowing that the true value could exceed it. They measured computing efficiency, converting chess performance gains into equivalent computation. Naam argues those efficiency returns should not be read directly as capability returns. His assumed 2 to 3% productivity gain against a modeled 15 to 19% threshold produces the roughly five-to-tenfold gap.

Naam stresses that hardware, data and investment can drive rapid progress even with weaker software feedback. He considers “When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth?” by Tom Davidson, Basil Halperin, Thomas Houlden and Anton Korinek, which combines software, hardware and economic feedback. In its central calibration, fully automated software research is approximately at the explosive threshold. Naam questions how much useful research each software improvement produces and how much past chip progress depended on expensive factories. He wants manufacturing and deployment delays built into predictions, while calling this the most compelling combined model he has seen. Its fully automated scenario and his estimate for current systems describe different conditions.

Toby Ord welcomed Naam’s use of current-system evidence and agreed that present returns look insufficient, while warning that this establishes neither that an explosion will never happen nor that pursuing one is responsible. Davidson objected to inferring the future from today’s shortfall. In a September 29 follow-up, he estimated that feedback had already strengthened more than a thousandfold since 2020. Any eventual software-driven explosion would pass through an earlier period of weaker feedback, he argued; a plateau in that strengthening would be more informative. He agreed OpenAI’s data were better than the survey and suggested an explosion was further off than previously thought.

Naam replied that he accepts possible strengthening but prefers experiment-based Stockfish evidence to the paper-authorship proxies used in the separate CASP/GovAI paper released September 28. He acknowledged possible selection of evidence to suit his conclusion. In Noahpinion’s comments, Lukas Finnveden likewise argued that the same method would have found weaker feedback a year earlier. Zvi Mowshowitz’s October 1 response faulted Naam for underestimating new approaches such as reasoning models. Naam’s essay itself allows that better judgment, memory or a major architectural breakthrough could change the calculation.

Naam ends by endorsing the Elasticity Institute’s September 23 note “How to Measure RSI”, by Cheryl Wu, Arjun Ramani, Basil Halperin and colleagues. Its eight requests include internal-model benchmark scores, computing inputs and efficiency measurements. He especially wants evidence of useful research added by each model with resources held roughly constant, followed by measurements of how that research improves the next model.
