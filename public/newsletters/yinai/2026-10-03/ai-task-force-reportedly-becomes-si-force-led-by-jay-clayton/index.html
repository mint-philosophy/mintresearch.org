Yesterday in AI · 3 October 2026

WSJ reports Jay Clayton will chair the Super Intelligence Force

Leary’s account details a 120-day review, three vice chairs and plans for incident warnings and government response. Companies would remain the primary managers of AI risk.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 3 October 2026 1129 words · ~6 min

Alex Leary reports in The Wall Street Journal that Jay Clayton will chair a White House Super Intelligence Force with 120 days to assess AI’s risks, opportunities and the federal government’s responsibilities. His October 3 report draws on an interview with Clayton and the group’s unpublished charter. Clayton is Director of National Intelligence; the Journal describes his task-force role as effectively making him the administration’s AI czar. He argued that American leadership would make it easier to confront dangers from adversaries. The report adds a mandate and named participants to Trump’s earlier promise of an AI Force.

The charter, as Leary quotes it, calls for plans to address threats enabled by AI while “preventing overregulation and regulatory capture that would stifle innovation and competition.” The group would review how the government receives warnings and notifications about breaches, hacks, jailbreaks and other problems, then recommend improvements to its response “under existing authorities.” It would coordinate across institutions and communicate emerging risks and opportunities to officials, industry and the public. The charter allows its work to extend beyond 120 days or its functions to transfer to an agency. Leary does not establish when that clock starts.

Officials told the Journal that companies would remain the primary managers of AI risk, working with the task force to identify dangers and opportunities. Clayton envisaged mechanisms developed through discussions with industry, citing financial risk models developed with the Federal Reserve and Securities and Exchange Commission. He also said the group would examine current law and possible congressional action. The article identifies no particular financial framework that the task force has adopted.

Leary connects the group’s formation to public concern about AI and the September 29 voluntary accord covered here, whose company commitments include internal controls, outside reviews and board oversight. He reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and White House chief of staff Susie Wiles have pressed for more government action over AI’s unpopularity, cyberattacks and biological-weapons risks. Disagreement within the administration has sometimes hampered policymaking, according to the Journal.

The Journal names three vice chairs with separate responsibilities. Emil Michael, Under Secretary of War for Research and Engineering, would coordinate with AI companies. Scott Kupor, director of the Office of Personnel Management, would handle critical-infrastructure providers and other industry stakeholders. FTC chairman Andrew Ferguson would oversee consumer interests and groups representing people of faith, children, privacy, and freedoms of speech, expression and association. Members would include JD Vance, Pete Hegseth, Bessent, Wiles and Richard Walters. David Sacks, co-chair of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, and Condoleezza Rice would participate externally, with Rice advising on national security.

Trump announced the AI Force on September 19, comparing it to Space Force and promising an AI czar; our coverage that day noted the proposal. In a September 28 interview published by TIME on October 1, he said he would rename it for super intelligence and described advisers who understood the technology. His September 29 terminology order directs agencies to use Super Intelligence and SI in non-statutory documents; it does not establish the task force. That day, the Journal listed Michael and Kupor among possible czars. CBS reported on October 1 that Clayton was the likely choice; the Journal followed on October 2, describing a role vacant since Sacks left earlier this year.

Clayton became DNI on August 3, after serving as U.S. attorney for the Southern District of New York; he chaired the SEC from May 2017 to December 2020. On CNBC on September 30, he described AI as a national-security issue, opposed pausing American development, and answered Senator Elizabeth Warren’s criticism of industry self-regulation by pointing to the FTC and Justice Department.

Federal agencies already have arrangements for model access and cyber warnings. Executive Order 14409, signed June 2, ordered a voluntary framework for government access to covered frontier models up to 30 days before developers share them with other trusted partners. An NSA-led classified process determines which models qualify; the order disclaims authority to create mandatory licensing or preclearance. It also ordered a cybersecurity clearinghouse, launched July 14 as Gold Eagle, coordinating vulnerability detection and patching with Treasury, CISA and the Department of War under existing powers. Leary’s report does not identify which existing programs the SI Force would review.

California has a mandatory reporting mechanism. Under SB 53, enacted in September 2025, frontier developers must report defined critical safety incidents to the Office of Emergency Services within 15 days of discovery. Incidents posing imminent death or serious physical injury require disclosure to an appropriate authority within 24 hours. Noam Kolt and colleagues’ 2024 arXiv paper Responsible Reporting for Frontier AI Development argued that sharing safety-critical information with government, industry and civil society could improve risk management. Neither precedent is cited in Leary’s account.

Clayton’s financial analogy has limits. In April, the Fed, OCC and FDIC replaced bank model-risk guidance originating in 2011 with principles that establish no enforceable requirements and explicitly exclude generative and agentic AI. The agencies describe those models as too new and fast-changing for this guidance. Clayton’s own SEC record includes a notification measure: in February 2018 the commission unanimously approved interpretive guidance on cybersecurity disclosures, explaining public companies’ duties under existing law.

Several participants have argued that proposed AI rules could protect incumbents. Sacks accused Anthropic in October 2025 of using fear to pursue regulatory capture. In August, Dario Amodei rejected equating regulation with concentrated power, arguing that exemptions and stricter testing for frontier companies could help challengers, and endorsed Demis Hassabis’s FINRA-like proposal for industry oversight. Sacks replied that review before deployment could concentrate power in government and a few labs, calling it a “DMV for AI.” Ferguson warned in September that companies seeking both regulation and antitrust exemptions could be erecting barriers to competitors. Michael accused effective altruists of financing fear-based advocacy that benefited incumbents. Vance voiced similar distrust of labs requesting regulation, the Journal reported that month.

Sacks invoked George Stigler’s 1971 theory of economic regulation, which examined industries seeking rules for their own benefit. Capture can also produce weaker safeguards: Kevin Wei and colleagues’ AIES 2024 paper How Do AI Companies “Fine-Tune” Policy? Examining Regulatory Capture in AI Governance found that the 17 policy experts they interviewed primarily worried about absent or weak regulation, or distorted policy priorities. Leary’s charter excerpts do not define regulatory capture.

Transformer editor Shakeel Hashim gave the membership a mixed reception, welcoming Bessent and Wiles while objecting to Michael and Sacks. On October 4, AFP reported that Clayton’s appointment remained unconfirmed and the White House had not answered its request for comment. The charter’s legal basis, the report’s publication arrangements and the 120-day starting date remain unspecified in the Journal’s account.
