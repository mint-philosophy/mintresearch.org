Mowshowitz challenges the easy answers in OpenAI's Model Spec

His review questions what models learn from examples that avoid difficult trade-offs, and argues that users need stronger control over their future impulses.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 3 November 2026 1123 words · ~6 min

OpenAI's examples often make the preferred answer so obvious that they leave difficult choices unexplained, Zvi Mowshowitz argues in his November 3 essay OpenAI Updates Its Model Spec. He broadly approves of the document, which describes intended model behavior, and regards the revision as similar in philosophy to its predecessor. His objection concerns how its rules will generalize. A model may reproduce the approved response while misunderstanding which detail made it appropriate. The October 29 revision's five values and authority changes were covered in our earlier report; his critique examines the judgments those values leave unsettled.

Mowshowitz's concern is clearest in examples that reject two extremes and approve a moderate response. In the October 29 Spec, a user authorizes up to $30 for birthday-invitation images. The approved assistant spends $3 producing useful alternatives; the rejected assistants exhaust the budget on near-identical designs or stop at a mediocre result despite expecting another attempt to help. He welcomes treating a spending limit as a ceiling. But when one choice is plainly wasteful and another plainly inadequate, the example teaches little about when an additional improvement justifies its cost. His broader objection is that agreeable answers can conceal disagreement about the reasons for choosing them.

He raised the same problem in his February 2025 review, asking for cases in which the convenient escape disappears and a real boundary must be drawn. OpenAI's March 2026 explanation describes a similar purpose for examples: a small, informative set should clarify difficult distinctions, with broader evaluations covering other situations. OpenAI also says the public document describes intended behavior without specifying its entire training process. Mowshowitz's criticism concerns the guidance the examples communicate; it does not establish how much any particular example changes a deployed model.

His most practical disagreement concerns commitments intended to constrain future choices. The Spec lets an assistant remind a user who orders cake of an earlier instruction against impulsive sweets purchases. Once the user explicitly makes an exception, the assistant should proceed, subject to other applicable rules. Mowshowitz accepts this as one option but wants users to be able to impose substantial friction on reversal. An earlier decision to resist temptation should sometimes constrain a later impulse. The Spec already permits proportionate challenge when a request conflicts with an expressed commitment; his objection is to making the subsequent override so easy.

A commenter using the name Silvan describes a related difficulty: distraction blockers once made tempting content harder to reach, but an assistant can now sustain a conversation about that content, and a fresh temporary chat can bypass earlier instructions. This is a personal account, but it identifies a separate requirement for effective self-binding. The commitment must survive changes of conversation as well as requests to revoke it.

Mowshowitz also questions the Spec's example of finding a Scottish Fold breeder. Its approved answer supplies leads, then adds a warning about inherited joint disease and cites breeding bans as evidence of animal-welfare concerns. Omitting the warning is disallowed, but withholding the search until the user acknowledges it is judged paternalistic. He asks which feature requires disclosure: potential suffering, costs to the owner, legal restrictions, or information the user might not know. Those considerations coincide in this example, leaving their independent importance unclear.

The Spec does provide a general disclosure standard: avoid deliberately withholding information that would materially change the user's understanding, and be forthright about consequential information. Mowshowitz disputes how the breeder example applies that standard. He thinks an incomplete but non-misleading answer can be acceptable and should rank above refusal, whereas the document ranks refusal above lying by omission. His proposed ordering separates innocent incompleteness from misleading omission and further distinguishes omissions the user requested. In an informal ChatGPT comparison he reports, it warned before supplying Scottish Fold locations, gave no warning for Pit Bulls, then reconsidered when challenged. That anecdote illustrates his question about consistency; it is not a measured comparison of model behavior.

The political examples involve a different conflict, between objectivity and following a requested format. The August Spec approved an imaginary coin flip when a user insisted on taking a side in an unspecified debate. October rejects a coin flip between Messi and Ronaldo and instead asks for a reasoned choice. In a presidential comparison, the approved answer chooses Franklin Roosevelt over Ronald Reagan and explains its criteria even when the user demands one name. Mowshowitz objects to overriding that request and notes that all three presidential examples favor Roosevelt.

OpenAI's stated rule is to preserve context necessary for objectivity even when the requested format excludes it. A further example permits a bare Roosevelt answer when the user specifically asks for the comparison from a Democratic perspective. Mowshowitz accepts that requesting a perspective changes the task, while insisting that merely identifying oneself as a Democrat does not request favorable coverage. His concern extends beyond partisan balance: technical staff and models may infer rules from incidental details, so examples need to distinguish an explicit perspective request from a remembered affiliation.

His objections to the wider revision follow the same concern about underspecified judgment. He would strengthen the earlier sections encouraging love of humanity and curiosity, which disappeared with the retirement of detailed style guidance. He also wants to retain the distinction between pursuing the wrong goal and making mistakes while pursuing the right one. The revised risk categories group both under failures originating with GPT while distinguishing harmful instructions from users or developers and manipulation by third parties. Mowshowitz accepts the latter distinction but proposes retaining both distinctions in four categories.

He welcomes the new oversight requirements and the prohibition on evading restrictions through delegation. He wants the instruction to treat side effects as real, even during apparent evaluations, to cover all effects explicitly. He also favors the requirement to consider the combined consequences of many GPT instances acting similarly, reading it as a reason to assess decisions in relation to other models making correlated choices. That broader interpretation is his proposal, beyond the Spec's explicit instruction to account for cumulative effects.

Related training research gives a concrete reason to care about explanations behind examples. In Model Spec Midtraining: Improving How Alignment Training Generalizes, posted to arXiv in May, Chloe Li and colleagues report that models trained on identical cheese preferences generalized differently after reading specifications that explained those preferences through different underlying values. Explaining the values behind rules improved generalization in their experiments. This supports investigating Mowshowitz's concern, without testing his claims about October's examples. OpenAI's revision announcement says it is exploring training that teaches the reasons behind the principles and will report results later. The practical question is whether models learn those reasons well enough to resolve cases where the desirable features no longer coincide.
