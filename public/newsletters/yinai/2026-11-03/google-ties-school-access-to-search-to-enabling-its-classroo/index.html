Google offers schools separate AI switches, but no AI-free Search

Schools will be able to enable Search without Gemini or Gemini Notebook. Search’s own AI features remain, while Guided Learning and new access defaults arrive on different schedules.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 3 November 2026 864 words · ~4 min

Google’s forthcoming school controls will let administrators disable separate AI products, but keeping Google Search will still mean accepting its built-in AI. In Ars Technica, Ryan Whitwam reports that schools seeking to exclude AI entirely would lose student access to Search. He argues that Google is relying on schools’ dependence on search to secure acceptance of AI. His article draws on New York Times reporting and connects the school policy to Google’s wider integration of AI into search.

Google clarified the product choices later on November 3: administrators can enable Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Search individually. A school could therefore keep Search while leaving the other two services off. Search itself would retain AI Mode and AI Overviews, which Google says will guide students through schoolwork step by step. That corrects Whitwam’s description of an all-or-nothing choice across products. Google’s education chief Chris Phillips separately told Chalkbeat that schools wanting AI off could choose “to not have search on at all.” The company has offered control over access to Search, without offering an AI-free version for school accounts.

In its November 3 announcement, Google said Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Search would be disabled for K-12 student accounts until administrators explicitly enabled them. Those access changes are forthcoming: Google promised details in the coming months before rollout. The policy changes the direction of its August Classroom expansion, which made the Gemini tab available to students of all ages, starting August 10 on the web. That expansion applied where the relevant services were already enabled; schools that had switched Gemini in Classroom off retained that setting. The November policy moves the listed services to an explicit opt-in default.

Google’s educational response is Guided Learning, which it introduced in the Gemini app in August 2025. The system asks questions and breaks problems into stages to keep students doing the thinking. Its November expansion has two distinct schedules. Guided Learning became globally available in Classroom on November 3, as an activity teachers assign using class material, with live information about where students need support. In Search, a pilot begins in selected U.S. schools later in November. Google says that when Search recognizes certain homework requests, such as a pasted physics problem or a request for help with a book report, it will prompt students to work through the concepts themselves.

Chalkbeat’s Erica Meltzer reports additional Classroom controls: teachers can review students’ chats, and students cannot switch back to ordinary Gemini within Classroom. Those controls let teachers inspect the process as well as the submitted work. Search reaches a different situation, where a student types a question without first opening a teacher-assigned activity. Google’s announcement limits its description to recognized types of homework requests; it does not establish that every school search will become a supervised tutoring session.

Google’s accompanying AI in Education report explains why it wants students to work through problems. It cites Hamsa Bastani and colleagues’ 2025 PNAS paper, Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. In a Turkish high school, students using an ordinary GPT-4 interface did better during assisted practice but scored 17% lower on subsequent unaided tests than students without AI access. A tutor supplied with teacher-designed hints and problem-specific guidance largely removed that loss, without producing a significant improvement over the control group on the unaided test. The study supports careful instructional design; it did not test Google’s new Search experience.

Common Sense Media’s Youth AI Safety Institute has proposed a different form of control in its assessment of Google Search. Its testers found that AI Mode completed every homework assignment they gave it, and that users could not disable Search’s AI features. The institute recommended adding separate AI Overview and AI Mode controls to the education administrator console, with those features off by default for student accounts. It also recommended redirecting requests for completed homework toward learning support. Google’s Guided Learning proposal addresses that second demand; the announced Search switch does not provide the separate feature controls the institute requested.

Lawmakers had already asked Google to explain its defaults. In an October 21 letter, Senators Josh Hawley and Richard Blumenthal asked why AI integration was enabled by default and sometimes impossible to disable on education devices. They requested research on potential harms, records of school and parent complaints, and details of Google’s responses, with answers due November 2. The November announcement changes the proposed access defaults, but it is not itself a published answer to those requests.

Schools’ existing restrictions make the Search decision concrete. New York City’s September policy, covered here at the time, imposed a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI from 2-K through eighth grade, while permitting tightly limited, teacher-supervised high-school pilots. On November 3, Mayor Zohran Mamdani accused Google of using its market power to force AI into public schools. A district following New York’s approach must decide whether Search with integrated AI fits its policy. Common Sense Media’s Eisha Buch argues that schools should be able to keep Search while disabling AI Overviews. Google’s clarification preserves separate choices about its other products, but leaves that particular choice unavailable.
