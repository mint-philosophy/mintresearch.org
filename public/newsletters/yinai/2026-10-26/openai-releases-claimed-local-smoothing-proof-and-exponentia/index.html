OpenAI releases claimed proofs on computation and waves

Public manuscripts claim a subexponential counting algorithm and local smoothing in every dimension. Outside researchers will examine the results; the physics work remains unpublished.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 October 2026 1100 words · ~6 min

OpenAI says an internal model has found a faster way to solve a central problem in computer science and proved that intensely focused waves cannot stay concentrated for long. On October 26 it released two manuscripts with Lean formalizations: the wave paper dated October 25 and the ETH paper revised that day. They add claims about computational limits and waves in every dimension to its October 6 mathematics catalogue. The withdrawal of three earlier papers over a sign error happened on October 7; these are new results submitted for examination.

A proposed algorithm challenges ETH

In "The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False," OpenAI claims an algorithm that counts exactly how many assignments satisfy a logical formula. For 3-SAT, each condition involves three true-or-false variables, and the question is whether all conditions can hold together. ETH says that, in the worst case, solving this problem requires time growing exponentially with the number of variables. OpenAI's proposed running time grows more slowly than any fixed exponential, while using polynomial space. A correct count would also decide whether any solution exists, refuting ETH.

The proposed algorithm expresses the answer as a sum of products of polynomials, one for each condition. Shared variables normally prevent separate parts from being counted independently. OpenAI's method changes the polynomial inputs so that, after fixing a small collection of shared numerical quantities, the variables divide into smaller groups. It counts within those groups and reconstructs the full answer through interpolation, which recovers a polynomial from information about its values and derivatives. OpenAI's overview credits Ryan Williams's method of splitting a search into parts and earlier polynomial-interpolation work by Andreas Björklund, alongside techniques for reducing formulas to sparse cases. The claimed advance combines those ingredients to count solutions in subexponential time.

A refutation would remove the premise behind many conditional claims that other computational problems cannot be solved faster. It would not establish P=NP, a practical SAT solver or broken encryption: a subexponential algorithm can still require infeasible computing time. OpenAI also says its bound does not automatically transfer to every problem in NP, because ordinary polynomial-time conversions between problems can enlarge the inputs too much. In a reply about consequences for developers, Ryan Williams said practical effects were unknown. OpenAI's manuscript derives further theoretical counting algorithms, including for graph colorings and perfect matchings.

The accompanying Lean scope note specifies a slightly weaker running-time bound than the manuscript's, still subexponential, for formulas represented in a prescribed encoding. That is the result the formalization covers. Lean checks that a formal conclusion follows from its stated assumptions; readers must also establish that the encoded statement represents the intended mathematical claim. OpenAI dates the model's initial ETH argument to October 16 and its Lean formalization to October 17. Publication followed on October 26.

Waves, geometry and different review arrangements

OpenAI's other manuscript, "Local smoothing for the Euclidean wave equation," addresses how waves concentrate. Rays arriving from a thin spherical shell can meet in an intense focus. Christopher Sogge's 1991 conjecture concerns a bound on the wave's size averaged over time, allowing those brief peaks. OpenAI claims the predicted bound in every spatial dimension of at least two. It also derives the Kakeya conjecture: a set containing a unit line segment in every direction must have the full dimension of its surrounding space. The paper claims further consequences for Fourier analysis, with restriction estimates throughout the interior of the conjectured range.

Larry Guth, Hong Wang and Ruixiang Zhang proved the two-dimensional local-smoothing case in work posted in 2019; Wang and Joshua Zahl proved the three-dimensional Kakeya set conjecture in 2025. OpenAI's October 6 catalogue already claimed local smoothing in three dimensions and Kakeya results in three and four. The new manuscript seeks to cover every dimension using an auxiliary partial differential equation to control how waves moving in different directions combine. Its formalization covers the main wave estimate and several consequences, including the full-dimensional Kakeya result.

OpenAI's announcement describes different release arrangements for mathematics, computer science and physics, also reported by Richard Lawler at The Verge. The wave manuscript is available for researchers to organize their own examination. For ETH, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi of UC San Diego and Williams of MIT will help coordinate scrutiny. Impagliazzo and Paturi helped formulate the hypothesis. The group requested immediate publication of the raw materials and funding for a conference; OpenAI agreed. Williams confirmed that they signed no nondisclosure agreements and would receive no compensation.

In their open letter to the theory community, the three state that their involvement does not certify the claim. They want researchers to share verified steps, gaps and corrections while preserving independent approaches. Their public repository and proposed meeting before year's end will support that work. They also ask how early access should be governed and how to credit checking and explanation, which can receive less recognition than announcing a discovery. Their stated aim is an argument others can reconstruct and question.

The physics materials remain unpublished. OpenAI says its models produced existence proofs for pure Yang-Mills theory on September 9 and quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the strong nuclear force, on September 18. It reports completing the Yang-Mills Lean formalization on October 16. A group led by David Gross will prepare its own account before releasing the artifacts. The announced Yang-Mills claim covers existence; it does not establish the separate mass-gap requirement of the Clay Millennium problem. Unlike the two public manuscripts, the physics argument can presently be described only through OpenAI's announcement.

The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence recommended against lab-appointed recipients on October 25. It argues that early access privileges selected researchers, lab choices can discourage others from participating, and attaching mathematicians' names can imply endorsement. Its alternative includes publication in repositories outside the lab's control, disclosure of how outputs were produced, and independent allocation of funding for understanding them. The group expressly limits its advice to mathematics, where OpenAI chose direct release of the wave manuscript.

Anthropic added a separate claimed ETH refutation late on October 26 Eastern time, with a paper titled "A 2^{o(n)} Time Algorithm for 3-SAT," credited to Claude. Its provenance statement dates the core algorithm to October 16, also OpenAI's reported discovery date, and describes a different method using tensor networks and rank arguments. The paper emphasizes how far its bound lies from practical use: for formulas with five conditions per variable, it improves on exhaustive search only beyond 10^10000 variables. Anthropic's README says checking the Lean proofs cannot establish that the formal statement captures the paper's intended theorem. That interpretation still requires human reading.
