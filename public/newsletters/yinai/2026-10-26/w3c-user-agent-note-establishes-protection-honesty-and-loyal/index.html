W3C group sets out duties for browsers and AI assistants

An October 22 Group Note calls for protection, honesty and loyalty to users. Its scope and exceptions prompted a debate four days later.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 26 October 2026 676 words · ~3 min

Browsers and AI assistants acting on websites should put their users’ interests ahead of their makers’, according to the W3C Technical Architecture Group’s October 22 Group Note, User Agents. Brian Kardell’s October 26 announcement prompted objections about where those duties apply. The Note is not endorsed by W3C or its members; its principles are intended to inform standards.

The duties combine protection against harmful websites and unnecessary disclosure with honesty about the agent’s behavior and loyalty to the user. A browser that quietly uses account information for advertising fails the honesty test. An agent that summarizes content or acts out of sight should explain its sources, actions and results.

Under the component rule, an app can distinguish its browser portion from portions displaying its own content; the duties apply only to the former. Without a clear distinction, such as an indicator of content origin, the duties extend to its own content too. An app using a WebView, a component for rendering web content, bears responsibility when it acts as a user agent.

Robin Berjon objected that the definition appeared to exclude a browser’s interactions with its maker’s servers, potentially allowing Chrome to track users through a component deemed outside the agent. He also challenged the restriction to the web. Note co-editor Jeffrey Yasskin replied that Chrome qualifies because it interacts with external websites. He understood Berjon’s concern to be about the component exception. Its purpose, Yasskin explained, was to hold Facebook or TikTok responsible for their in-app browsers without prescribing how their own app functions operate. His example was a duty against recording users’ keystrokes in the browser portion. He invited better wording.

The disagreement follows a shared earlier project. Berjon and Yasskin edited the 2025 Privacy Principles, which already described protection, honesty and loyalty, alongside discretion in handling personal information. Those principles said agents should prefer users’ interests even over their implementers’, and treated explanations buried in legal notices as inadequate. They cited Berjon’s 2021 SSRN paper, The Fiduciary Duties of User Agents. Berjon proposed duties for the relatively small set of intermediaries through which people use digital services, including operating systems and voice assistants as well as browsers. His scope extended beyond the web.

The component boundary was also contested during drafting. In an April discussion, Heather Flanagan argued that responsibility should follow behavior, such as rendering external content or requesting sensitive permissions. Classifying browsers, embedded views and other implementations separately could encourage developers to design around the stricter category, she warned. Kardell questioned whether users could recognize the relevant technical distinctions. A change merged on October 14 deliberately limited the document’s definition to web user agents.

For AI assistants, Flanagan’s March amendment explicitly included actions authorized by users and called for communicating their consequences. A separate discussion concerned Luka Bekavac’s request for inspectable records. Flanagan argued that disclosing every system prompt or reasoning trace could expose private information without reliably explaining behavior. She favored visibility into resources accessed, information disclosed, authority used and actions taken. Bekavac accepted that approach but argued that an understandable summary and a record that developers or researchers could audit served different purposes. The resulting honesty language calls for explanations of otherwise hidden behavior, without specifying a logging format or requiring disclosure of internal reasoning.

The note permits agents to honor restrictions that secure access to a service, using browser support for copy-protected media as an example. It limits these commitments to the service involved, protecting the user’s other data, settings and choices. Users must remain able to leave the service and clear their data without further consequences. During review of that example, Nick Doty asked whether the same reasoning would let publishers demand that browsers force users to view advertisements or prove their identity. Yasskin acknowledged before publication that the acceptable commitments were still inadequately defined. He supported publishing the rest of the guidance while that work continued. His October 20 follow-up issue remains open and calls for further constraints in the general guidance, beyond the media example.
