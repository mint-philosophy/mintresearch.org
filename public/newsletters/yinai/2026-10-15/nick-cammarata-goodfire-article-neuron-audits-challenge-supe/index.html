Cammarata makes the case for interpreting neurons directly

Revisiting classic vision circuits and preliminary language-model audits, Goodfire’s Nick Cammarata argues that neurons are more legible than the field assumed. The debate turns on what superposition means, how explanations should be tested, and what weak activations encode.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 October 2026 1195 words · ~6 min

Nick Cammarata helped establish the view that individual neurons often combine unrelated meanings, making neural networks difficult to understand. In Neural Networks Are Already Clean, published by Goodfire on October 15, he revisits that evidence and argues for studying the network's own neurons and circuits directly. Better tools, modern architectures and experiments that follow how neurons develop have changed his mind. The essay presents a working hypothesis about how networks learn, supported most extensively in vision models and provisionally in language models. Its further suggestion that automated researchers could make ambitious interpretability tractable soon is explicitly speculative.

The revision begins with examples Cammarata helped popularize in Zoom In, the 2020 circuits paper. A famous InceptionV1 neuron responded to cat faces and car fronts. The paper ruled out a shared visual property; Cammarata now identifies a V shape, also visible in its responses to flames and other objects. A separate circuit appeared to distribute a car representation among dog neurons. His reanalysis finds shared pose information and misleading synthetic images generated by the visualization method. Crucially, he says no downstream circuit reads the supposed distributed car representation back out. These are corrections to particular mechanistic interpretations that became recurring examples in the field.

The problems extended beyond individual examples. Cammarata estimates that improved visualization and attribution tools make roughly half of InceptionV1's final-layer neurons interpretable, compared with his earlier estimate of about 5%. Retraining and architectural changes improve the picture further: in a modern ConvNeXt of the same size, trained on the same dataset, he puts the figure around 99%. Those are his judgments of legibility, rather than scores from one standardized evaluation. To test what happens when capacity is scarce, he also retrains InceptionV1 with one layer reduced from 480 neurons to ten. He finds broader detectors, such as one covering curves and loops, instead of the arbitrary mixtures he expected.

His proposed explanation is recursive generalization. A neuron can acquire related functions successively until its endpoints look unrelated. The essay describes a language neuron responding to the letters iy, extending to diy, then to the phrase do it yourself. In a vision training sequence, a neuron starts with yellow flowers and spreads into other aspects of pictures containing them, eventually including dogs. Following that development can explain an apparent mixture. The practical proposal is to study what flows into a neuron, what it changes downstream, and how its function develops, using increasingly capable agents to propose and test explanations.

This argument depends on a particular meaning of superposition: a learning process that packs unrelated functions together under capacity pressure because they rarely interfere. Cammarata distinguishes that from representing more features than there are dimensions, the definition used in Anthropic's 2022 Toy Models of Superposition. He accepts that paper's experiments, but argues that its reconstruction tasks capture the costs of interference better than the benefits of reusing shared structure. Liv Gorton's initial response challenges the proposed opposition: superposition is a geometrical claim, semantic unrelatedness is not required, and a story about how neurons develop could coexist with it. She also argues that composition produces more combinations of features, which does not settle how many features a model represents.

The language-model section, developed with Curt Tigges, reports preliminary Qwen3-32B audits in which roughly 90% of neuron poles have one principal function, often with related offshoots. A pole is the positive or negative side of a neuron's response. The percentage depends on activation thresholds and on how broadly related functions are grouped; Cammarata warns against treating it as a precise cleanliness score. An attribution graph, a diagram tracing how components contribute to an answer, illustrates the approach in Kimi K3 at much larger scale. The authors explicitly say they have not begun full audits at that scale. A forthcoming technical paper is meant to provide the detailed language-model evidence.

A Qwen example about Santa shows what standard neuron labels can miss. Labels based on a neuron's strongest positive responses suggest capital cities, while its negative side responds to Santa lore. Removing eight neurons with that negative-side function reduces the probability of answering Santa from near certainty to 6%; removing twenty neurons actually labeled Christmas or Santa leaves it at 76%. The example supports looking at both sides of a neuron's behavior and checking explanations with interventions. It also shows why a list of plausible labels can omit the components that matter causally.

The essay includes tests beyond inspecting examples. For 200 randomly chosen neurons from its gallery, an agent receives each explanation and writes new examples predicted to activate the neuron, plus closely related examples predicted not to. Among the 183 explanations covering positive responses, the first group produces strong activations 76% of the time, against 11% for the near misses. Cammarata also describes testing whether an agent given only an explanation can construct an especially strong stimulus. He acknowledges that successfully finding the center of a neuron's response does not establish its full range, and supplements that test by examining different groups of inputs.

Leo Gao asks for more rigorous evaluation of whether explanations can simulate neurons, arguing that plausible descriptions are easier to produce. He suggests measuring the resulting divergence in model outputs, rather than correlation with individual neuron activations. Cammarata's objection to simulation scores is that they can overemphasize precise activation values and where attention places information, while missing the explanation's substance. In reply to Gao, he says he could be persuaded to reconsider; Tigges agrees the concern should be addressed in the technical post. Their exchange leaves the evaluation standard open.

Weak activations are another unresolved question, and the essay does investigate them. Removing weak responses has little effect in several vision experiments; one appendix result retains 69.47% accuracy after keeping approximately the strongest quarter of positive activations in InceptionV1's final layer, against 70.08% originally. The language-model discussion includes tracing weaker responses and a detailed example of an adversary-related neuron. But the appendix says the authors lack a semantic test for weak activations they trust: broader screening gives mixed results, and the spot checks cannot establish their meaning generally. Neel Nanda's objection concerns precisely that remaining uncertainty. He expects weak language-model activations to encode substantial information about facts and memorized material, and doubts how far the vision findings transfer.

Nanda welcomes practical exploration with neurons and agent-assisted interpretation, while remaining skeptical that pruned attribution graphs can support confidence that nothing important has been missed. There is already relevant precedent. In January, Aryaman Arora, Zhengxuan Wu, Jacob Steinhardt and Sarah Schwettmann published Language Model Circuits Are Sparse in the Neuron Basis. They found that the model's own neurons could represent its activity using as few active components as sparse autoencoders, additional models trained to separate a network's activity into simpler patterns. They also traced circuits whose interventions changed behavior on language tasks. That supports the practical research direction without resolving Cammarata's broader account of learning. Attention remains substantially less explored in his essay, and the language-model technical paper is still to come. His proposal is to let explanations of neighboring mechanisms constrain one another, then use that accumulated understanding to investigate questions about goals, deception and behavior.
