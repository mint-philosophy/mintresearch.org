Six METR evaluators get internal access and publication rights at Anthropic

Anthropic says the team can publish without its veto and publicly contest redactions. The announcement specifies access exceptions and three evaluator tiers, while a same-day revision leaves SecureBio’s current tier unstated.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 October 2026 839 words · ~4 min

Anthropic announced on October 15 that it has onboarded six METR employees with company hardware, office space and access to internal tools and systems broadly comparable to its own risk assessment teams. Under the terms it describes, METR can publish assessments without Anthropic’s approval. The announcement implements part of Dario Amodei’s September pledge, covered at the time, and follows the staffing and funding questions covered October 11.

Access is qualified in several ways. Anthropic’s benchmark is “roughly on par” with its internal risk teams. Examples of excluded information include customer data, attorney-client privileged material and model architectures. Outside these categories, employees are encouraged to share information as they would with colleagues assessing alignment risks. Architecture information has a separate exception: Anthropic says it will generally seek to share it when highly relevant, subject to explicit internal approval. Its stated reason for caution is the risk of inadvertent leaks when evaluators also work with other companies; this is a conditional route to access, rather than an entitlement.

METR may publish analysis of Anthropic’s risks, incidents and practices without a company veto. Anthropic may require redactions within narrow, predefined categories. METR can publicly describe those redactions and dispute their justification, provided it does not reveal the information removed. The arrangement covers the small embedded team under enforceable agreements; other METR employees, including those working on separate Anthropic projects, are siloed from its work. Anthropic presents this as a way to give outside experts information it cannot publish because of misuse risks or commercial sensitivity, while allowing them to share evidence and conclusions.

On access and redaction, these stated terms are stronger than the minimum METR accepted in its general proposal to developers, covered yesterday. That proposal allowed companies to choose access and redact anything they considered sensitive, and acknowledged that assessors would lack senior employees’ access. Anthropic instead specifies a comparison with its risk teams and limits redactions to defined categories. Their full extent remains unclear. Amodei’s September pledge expressly included commercially sensitive information; the October announcement gives examples, not a complete list. Its omission of that phrase from the examples does not establish that the category has been abolished.

Anthropic places METR in the third of three evaluator tiers. First-tier investigations are narrowly scoped: Anthropic determines access and must approve publication. Second-tier evaluators receive more information and may publish without approval, subject to specified redactions they can publicly contest. The third tier adds employee-like access to similar publication rights. Anthropic hopes eventually to have several third-tier teams publishing separate assessments. It is developing evaluation, red-teaming and safeguards work with Accenture and Faculty, without assigning them a tier, and is in early discussions with Hugging Face.

SecureBio’s status requires care because Anthropic revised the announcement that day. An archived version placed SecureBio in the first tier and contemplated future projects with increased access and publication rights. The revised text removes the tier designation and the reference to increased publication rights; its footnote says the update clarifies current project terms. SecureBio is reviewing the chemical and biological weapons chapter of Anthropic’s August Risk Report. Its earlier pilot review, published in July, documents substantial access: an unredacted report, 110 pages of supporting material and answers to over seventy questions. Anthropic retained veto and redaction rights over that review but exercised neither. That history does not establish the current review’s publication terms or justify placing SecureBio in another tier.

The publication distinction follows contentious negotiations. Reuters reported on October 13 that Anthropic and OpenAI had sought strict editorial control earlier that month, while Anthropic’s talks with some evaluators had subsequently evolved. Anthropic told Reuters it distinguished established partners’ publication freedom from approval requirements for new firms. In Reuters’ October 15 report, a person familiar with the talks described Anthropic becoming more open; another identified the continuing difficulty of information that is both commercially valuable and relevant to risk. METR said its report would disclose the scope, access and limitations of its work.

Funding and contract protections remain distinct questions. Anthropic told CNBC that it is not funding METR’s work. METR’s own policy statement says it does not accept frontier-lab payments or donations, but recognizes dependence on companies’ voluntary cooperation. Theo Bearman’s IAPS memo, covered October 13, recommends regular evaluator rotation, at least one-year cooling-off periods before moving between evaluator and company employment, and protection against retaliation for reports to outside authorities. Anthropic’s announcement does not specify these protections. The published access terms alone cannot establish how independent the assessment will be in practice.

In their 2024 FAccT paper Black-Box Access is Insufficient for Rigorous AI Audits, Stephen Casper and colleagues argued that training and deployment information enables scrutiny beyond testing a model’s outputs, and that readers need to know auditors’ access and methods to interpret their findings. METR’s policy commits it to explaining whether access limits or redactions affected its conclusions and what formal restrictions or informal pressures mattered. Those disclosures would let readers assess how Anthropic’s stated terms worked in the investigation itself.
