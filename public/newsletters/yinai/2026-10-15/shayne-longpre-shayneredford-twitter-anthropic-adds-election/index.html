Anthropic adds election safeguards after campaign tests expose gaps

Outside testers found Claude could assist a deceptive campaign through apparently harmless steps. New classifiers target that pattern, while the company’s 99% evenhandedness score measures a separate kind of behaviour.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 October 2026 807 words · ~4 min

Anthropic has added election-abuse classifiers after outside testers found Claude could help with a deceptive campaign divided into apparently harmless tasks. Its October 14 announcement, relayed by Shayne Longpre the next day, also reports political-usage estimates, outside reviews of free expression and plans to bring official voting information into Claude’s answers.

The Institute for Strategic Dialogue and McCain Institute tested protections in the US, Israel and Brazil. They began with direct harmful requests, then separated campaigns into audience research, website construction, content drafting and distribution planning. Claude reliably refused the direct requests but could miss the purpose of the combined work. Anthropic says its new classifiers target this pattern, including fake accounts, fabricated news sites and financially motivated election schemes. It gives no detection or false-positive rates for the additions. The company also reports banning accounts behind a foreign-operated news site promoting Russian, Iranian and North Korean narratives, and accounts producing fabricated news for advertising income.

The September Opus 5.5 system card documents a related weakness. In one test, a user claimed authority to assess a live election portal’s security. Opus refused operational attack instructions but supplied preparatory technical detail, such as mapping the portal’s technology stack. Across election-integrity conversations with an adversarial simulated user, the model with the Claude.ai system prompt behaved appropriately throughout 85% of conversations, with a reported margin of six percentage points. These tests excluded production safeguards such as real-time probes and offline monitoring, and predate the newly announced classifiers. They describe model behaviour under the tested conditions, rather than the effectiveness of the full deployed service.

A related mechanism appears in Erik Jones, Anca Dragan and Jacob Steinhardt’s 2024 paper Adversaries Can Misuse Combinations of Safe Models. They split harmful tasks between models, using a stronger, safety-trained model for difficult benign components and a weaker model for malicious ones. The combinations produced harmful outputs more often than either model alone. Their result concerns combinations of models; the election exercise shows why a provider also needs to examine how several requests contribute to one operation.

The announcement’s 99% figure measures something different. Anthropic reports that score for Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 on its own political-evenhandedness evaluation. The published Paired Prompts method tests 1,350 pairs across 150 topics, asking about each topic from opposing positions. A Claude grader assesses whether the responses offer comparable depth, engagement and evidence. The evaluation covers single replies, mainly in US political discourse, and depends on the chosen standard for balanced engagement. It provides no external certification of neutrality. Opus 5.5’s roughly 99% result was already covered here on September 27; the new post extends the claim to the full 5.5 family.

The outside free-expression reviews were separate exercises. According to Anthropic, The Future of Free Speech and the Foundation for American Innovation rated most responses as strong. The former and the Collective Intelligence Project nevertheless found unsolicited caveats and counterarguments, including an opposing view inserted into an advocacy email. The foundation found that Sonnet 4.5 softened right-leaning arguments more often. That finding concerns an older model; the post does not name the models used by the other two reviewers. CIP developed its evaluation with input from more than 2,000 people in the US, UK and India. Anthropic says it has improved balance in short answers and is adjusting when Claude adds caveats. The post supplies the company’s account of these reviews, without comparative pass rates.

The usage estimates also have a narrower meaning than a general measure of election safety. Claude classified a random sample of about 250,000 US Claude.ai conversations from September 21 to 27; about 0.51% concerned elections or voting. The analysis used the privacy-preserving approach introduced in Clio: Privacy-Preserving Insights into Real-World AI Use, which surfaces aggregate patterns without human reviewers reading raw conversations. A separate review of 250,000 conversations found election-policy violations in about 0.002%. That rounded rate corresponds to roughly five conversations in a sample of that size. It measures identified requests, leaving unanswered how many violations went undetected or what effects resulting campaigns had.

Anthropic’s October 8 policy rewrite, covered here last week, takes effect on November 12. It will remove the blanket prohibition on personalised vote and campaign targeting while retaining restrictions on deceiving voters and disguising coordinated influence operations. The newly published campaign guide gives concrete boundaries: speechwriting, polling analysis and volunteer scripts are permitted; fabricated grassroots support and false polling-place announcements are prohibited. It also bars undisclosed synthetic media impersonating a candidate, including material made for that candidate’s own campaign.

Later in October, Anthropic plans to integrate Democracy Works’ state and local election information into Claude.ai answers about polling places and identification requirements. The existing TurboVote banner remains. Claude is also due to display official results on election night and afterwards, although Anthropic has not named the results provider.
