FTC demands incident records and safety claims from OpenAI and Anthropic

The October 14 demands advance an existing consumer-protection investigation. Axios reports requests for security records, safeguards, marketing and privacy policies; the FTC confirmed the demands to CBS.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 October 2026 677 words · ~3 min

The Federal Trade Commission sent civil investigative demands to OpenAI and Anthropic on October 14 seeking records of AI safety incidents and the companies’ safeguards, Maria Curi reported in Axios the following day. Each demand exceeds 50 pages and also seeks marketing materials and documents describing privacy policies, according to her source, a person familiar with the matter. An FTC spokesperson confirmed the demands to CBS News. The investigation concerns possible consumer-protection violations; the demands gather evidence ahead of any decision to bring a case.

Curi reports that demands to third-party AI organizations will follow and that the FTC plans to compel executives to testify about their products. OpenAI told Axios that safety remained a priority and promised to “engage constructively with the FTC.” The October 15 reporting names OpenAI and Anthropic as recipients. Semafor’s September 30 account also identified the evaluator METR as a target, but Curi does not identify which third parties will receive demands next. Ashley Belanger’s Ars Technica report says neither company answered its requests for comment and the FTC did not supply the demands for inspection.

The inquiry was already public in September. On October 8, a senior official told Semafor’s Ashley Gold that compulsory demands were close to being sent, covered here on October 9. The official described a significant investigation prompted by public health and safety concerns. This week’s development is delivery of the demands and the reported scope of their questions, which covers both internal security practices and what the companies tell users.

The FTC’s explanation of its powers describes civil investigative demands as its consumer-protection bureau’s compulsory tool for investigating possible unfair or deceptive practices. They can require documents, written answers and oral testimony. A recipient can ask the Commission to narrow or set aside a demand; the FTC can seek a federal court order enforcing compliance. Investigations before a complaint are generally nonpublic. The agency can pursue an enforcement action if it has reason to believe the law was violated, with allegations then subject to settlement or adjudication.

FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson told Reuters on September 25 that existing legal tools should apply to AI developers. He rejected treating agents as independent actors and said reviews of audit trails had shown systems following instructions. Ferguson also suggested that the FTC’s authority over companies that fail to disclose data breaches could apply to AI developers. Those remarks explain the approach Belanger discusses at Ars, but the demands’ specific legal provisions and allegations have not been made public.

Joel Thayer and colleagues at the America First Policy Institute proposed a related argument in their September report on autonomous AI cyberattacks. They argued that Section 5 of the FTC Act could apply when hacking incidents expose misleading claims about models’ security, predictability or alignment. They also identified establishing consumer harm as a difficult question for an unfairness case. Thayer welcomed Curi’s report and linked that analysis. This is a proposed legal argument; the FTC has not publicly specified which theory it will pursue against either company.

Belanger considers whether delayed incident disclosures could expose the companies to fines, while doubting the administration’s willingness to pursue substantial penalties. Earlier FTC cases show why the terms of any resulting order matter. In its 2018 Uber settlement, the agency added obligations after Uber failed for more than a year to disclose a second breach during an existing security investigation. The final settlement made future failures to notify the FTC of specified incidents potentially punishable by civil penalties. It did not establish an automatic daily fine for every undisclosed security incident.

The FTC’s 2024 Blackbaud complaint likewise alleged that delayed notification and misleading descriptions of stolen data prevented consumers from protecting themselves. That settlement proposed safeguards and future breach-reporting obligations. These cases illustrate how security failures and disclosure practices can enter consumer-protection proceedings. Whether the AI investigation produces comparable claims or remedies will depend on the evidence and the legal provisions the FTC invokes. The public reporting does not establish response deadlines, a testimony schedule or any decision to sue.
