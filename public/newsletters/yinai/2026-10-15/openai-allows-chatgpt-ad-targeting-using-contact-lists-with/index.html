What advertisers can target inside ChatGPT

Joseph Cox ran two privacy-warning ads without uploading personal data. His investigation also documents contact-list matching and the limits of OpenAI’s deletion and personalization promises.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 15 October 2026 822 words · ~4 min

Joseph Cox bought two advertisements inside ChatGPT that warned: "ChatGPT isn't private. OpenAI hires humans to read users' chats." His October 15 investigation for 404 Media shows how advertisers can seek an audience inside conversations users may consider private. Both ads linked to 404 Media's Project Lily investigation, which documented contractors reviewing users' conversations (earlier coverage). Cox supplied context hints asking for people discussing privacy, whether ChatGPT chats are private, and current AI topics. These hints guide matching; they do not guarantee placement against an exact keyword.

Cox says in 404's accompanying newsletter that he ran the ads without using people's personal data. His experiment demonstrated contextual advertising. The separate contact-list feature he describes lets advertisers upload identifiers that OpenAI matches against ChatGPT accounts. It accepts email addresses and phone numbers in raw form or as SHA-256 hashes, plus Google Advertising IDs used on Android devices. The interface connects an advertiser's existing customer or prospect list to people using ChatGPT.

OpenAI's advertiser help page specifies at least 25,000 matched users for inclusion targeting or audience-based bid adjustments. Uploaded rows are not the unit: duplicates, invalid entries and identifiers that do not match accounts do not count. Smaller audiences can be used to exclude people from a campaign. If an advertiser combines inclusion and exclusion, the eligible audience remaining after exclusions must still meet the minimum. Being in an eligible audience does not guarantee an impression.

The API documentation adds safeguards absent from a simple description of uploading contacts. Advertisers receive audience counts in ranges, not exact totals or individual match results. They cannot pick named individuals from the matched audience. Membership changes that would reduce a campaign's eligible population below the minimum can be rejected. OpenAI also requires first-party data that advertisers have the right to use and prohibits broker-sourced lists. These are documented requirements; Cox's test does not establish how consistently they are enforced.

Hashing obscures the uploaded contact values while preserving their usefulness for matching. It does not make an audience anonymous to the service doing that matching. OpenAI did not answer Cox's question about whether it hashes emails and phone numbers supplied in raw form. Its API documentation explicitly says Google Advertising IDs are hashed internally, but does not give the same explanation for raw contacts. Advertisers can therefore know whose identifiers they submitted without learning which individuals matched.

The deletion promise is narrower than deletion of the resulting targeting data. OpenAI told Cox, and says in its help page, that uploaded files are deleted after processing, usually within 24 hours. The audience created from a file remains a usable object: advertisers can add or remove members, replace the list and keep using it in campaigns. The documentation does not specify how long every derived identifier or matching record persists. Deleting the upload should not be read as deleting the audience.

Users receive a different account of the process. OpenAI's ads FAQ describes conversation context and advertiser targeting selections, but does not explicitly explain uploaded contact lists. Turning off personalization stops the use of other chat threads, ad history and topics; it still permits ads selected from the current conversation. The FAQ does not explain how that switch affects custom-audience matching. OpenAI says advertisers receive aggregate performance information, such as views and clicks, rather than users' chats or personal details. Data can thus flow from an advertiser to OpenAI for matching without OpenAI giving that advertiser the user's conversation.

Contact-list targeting predates Cox's investigation. A July 28 capture of OpenAI's help page already documented raw or hashed email and phone lists and a 25,000-matched-user minimum. The current version adds advertising IDs, editable membership and use of smaller lists for exclusions. Those changes expand the tool's uses; they do not remove the documented minimum for targeting an included audience.

Zach Edwards, co-founder of the advertising research company DecryptAds, told Cox that advertisers sharing personal information with advertising platforms should have to register as data brokers and provide a removal process. His proposal concerns people whose identifiers enter this system, including those who may not know an advertiser uploaded them. A relevant regulatory precedent is the FTC's 2022 action against Twitter. The agency alleged that Twitter used phone numbers and emails collected for account security to match advertisers' lists, without disclosing that additional use. The stated purpose of collecting an identifier matters alongside the mechanics of matching it.

A separate transparency proposal addresses Cox's first targeting method. In her September analysis for Tech Policy Press, Mariana Olaizola Rosenblat argued that the EU should require ChatGPT's advertising repository to disclose advertisers' context hints under the Digital Services Act. That argument formed part of earlier coverage of ChatGPT's EU designation. Publishing the hints would make advertisers' requested conversational settings visible to outside scrutiny, rather than leaving researchers to infer targeting from the ads they encounter. Cox's privacy-warning campaign supplies a concrete example of what those instructions can contain.
