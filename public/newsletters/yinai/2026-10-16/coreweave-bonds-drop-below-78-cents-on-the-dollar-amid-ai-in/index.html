CoreWeave's bonds fall into distress four months after they were sold at par

Bloomberg's Markets Daily treats CoreWeave's 2032 notes, trading under 78 cents on the dollar after a co-founder spoke of "unlimited" insurer capital, as the clearest sign of anxiety over how the AI buildout is financed; the week that followed tested whether a $104 billion backlog can carry $35 billion of debt.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 16 October 2026 1453 words · ~7 min

Bloomberg's Markets Daily newsletter for October 15, whose CoreWeave section is by Rachel Graf, Katherine Schwartz and Dina Bass, treats October 14 as the day the US stock market's longest calm stretch since 2020 ended. The fastest US inflation in five months pushed the 10-year Treasury yield to 5.33% before it fell back to 5.08%, futures briefly priced better than even odds of an October Fed hike, the Nasdaq lost 2%, the Russell 2000 entered a correction, and Korea's Kospi fell 9% the next morning. The newsletter picks CoreWeave to illustrate the anxiety over AI borrowing: its shares fell as much as 17% on Wednesday, and its debt due in 2032, bought at par by investors four months earlier, traded at less than 78 cents on the dollar. Bloomberg's macro strategist Skylar Montgomery Koning told readers "these moves are unlikely to be sustained"; the CoreWeave section is less reassuring.

The bond in question is one CoreWeave priced on June 11: $1.25 billion of 9.625% senior unsecured notes due July 15, 2032, sold alongside €2 billion of 8.5% notes with the same maturity. The company's announcement says the notes were issued at par, and the 8-K recording the June 18 closing describes ordinary high-yield terms: subsidiary guarantees, semiannual interest from January 2027, and a make-whole call before July 2029. The repricing started on Tuesday October 13. Chamath Palihapitiya posted a Social Capital chart that evening showing the dollar notes' yield touching 15.1% intraday and closing at a record 14.5%, up from about 9.6% at issuance; Elon Musk replied "Yikes" at 7:43 pm Eastern. Bloomberg's October 14 article by Graf and Schwartz reports the notes below 80 cents on Trace data on Wednesday, the level widely taken to mean a borrower is financially strained, with a yield above 15%. Palihapitiya's chart came attached to a longer essay asking whether private credit funding the AI buildout is a bubble; one of its observations was that CoreWeave "borrowed for five years against customer contracts averaging about three".

Bloomberg attributes the Wednesday selloff to a Financial Times interview with co-founder Brannin McBee, whom it paraphrases as wanting the privilege of borrowing "unlimited" amounts. Fortune's Jim Edwards, quoting the FT directly, has McBee describing insurers' access to capital as something that "is unlimited": "Like, that's all the money in the world that sits in the insurance tranche." Benzinga's account of the interview fills in the plan. McBee wants stronger investment-grade ratings on CoreWeave's borrowing so insurers can buy it, is weighing whether insurers could also take loans backed by lower-rated customers such as AI labs, which he said were not yet ready for that market, and is evaluating further delayed-draw term loans. CoreWeave has done this once already: in March it closed an $8.5 billion facility rated A3 by Moody's and A (low) by DBRS, secured by GPUs and a customer contract, with a fixed-rate tranche at about 5.9%, anchored by Blackstone Credit & Insurance with insurance investors participating. Set against the 9.625% coupon on the unsecured notes and the 15% they now yield, that facility shows the cost gap McBee hopes ratings can close. Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid, quoted by Fortune, puts AA-rated AI borrowers such as Meta and Alphabet at around 5% to 5.5% and BBB names such as Oracle at 8% to 8.5%, and calls CoreWeave, rated BB+, the exception.

Joe Weisenthal, in Bloomberg's Odd Lots newsletter, argues CoreWeave spooked the market by signaling the opposite of retrenchment. Gil Luria of D.A. Davidson, the bear Bloomberg quotes, said that if CoreWeave borrows at 9% and earns at best 5%, "it doesn't matter how much they scale: They're destroying value at every turn", and that more leverage in data centers means more risk in the system; the percentages are his illustration, since CoreWeave does not disclose a return on invested capital. Luria has called the company "the subprime tranche of the AI trade", and Axios's Emily Peck reports he said on the Prof G Markets podcast on Wednesday that the stock could go to zero if the investment cycle turns, a remark he later conceded "probably didn't help". The newsletter's Word from Wall Street box belongs to Fortress co-CEO Drew McKnight: "Very few infrastructure booms have not ended in tears", and "The equity captures the upside, while the credit has the downside."

CEO Mike Intrator answered in a staff memo seen by Bloomberg and Reuters. He wrote that "there will be periods of time when the market misunderstands" a new kind of technology company, expressed confidence in the "relentless discipline around the financial engine we have built", and, per Reuters, told employees the backlog more than covers the debt. CoreWeave's second-quarter release gives the figures both sides are using. Revenue backlog was about $104 billion at June 30, before more than $25 billion of commitments added in early July; the balance sheet carried about $35.1 billion of debt, $7.5 billion of it due within a year, plus about $16.3 billion of operating lease liabilities. Quarterly revenue was $2.575 billion, net interest expense $640 million, adjusted operating income $128 million, and the net loss $626 million. TheStreet reads the same release as a warning: "Backlog, however, is revenue promised over several years, not cash in hand." CoreWeave told Fortune that 98% of its revenue comes from committed take-or-pay contracts; Neostellar's Evan Schlossman, an early investor, argued to Fortune that noncancelable contracts with Microsoft and OpenAI make the debt good, while the accountant Francine McKenna doubted Microsoft would keep honoring contracts that stopped serving its interests. Three customers produced 72% of second-quarter revenue, Fortune notes. Gartner added to the pressure on Wednesday with a forecast that AI data center owners need $8.5 trillion of cumulative revenue through 2030 to cover capital spending, financing and a 12% return, against at most $3.6 trillion of attributable AI revenue, leaving more than $3.5 trillion of spending exposed to impairment and, in Gartner's view, more than 80% of neoclouds ceasing independent operation by 2030.

The rest of the week showed how closely the stock now follows the bonds. On Thursday Nvidia's Jensen Huang, whose company holds about 10% of CoreWeave, told reporters in Stockholm that "CoreWeave's financial strength is improving every day" and "Their cost of capital is actually decreasing", and that Nvidia saw no need to backstop it; the shares rallied almost 6%, per Bloomberg's October 16 newsletter. Reuters reports five-year credit default swaps around 1,000 basis points, implying roughly a 63% chance of default within five years against about 30% a year earlier, despite an agreement under which Nvidia buys any unsold CoreWeave capacity through April 2032. On Friday the shares fell more than 11% for a weekly loss near 25%, and the swaps set records above 1,000 basis points according to S&P; Global Market Intelligence. Jefferies, JPMorgan, Cantor and Evercore called the selloff overdone; Evercore wrote that markets were treating a secondary-market bond yield as "evidence the business model has stopped working, which we don't think is the case", and Jefferies' Brent Thill conceded "Higher rates on future deals are a fair risk". Bloomberg adds that the three co-founders have sold $3.3 billion of shares since the March 2025 IPO.

Bloomberg's newsletter does not cite it, but the Bank for International Settlements described this mechanism in January. BIS Bulletin 120, by Iñaki Aldasoro, Sebastian Doerr and Daniel Rees, found private credit loans to AI firms priced at nearly the same spread as other private loans, 6.2 against 6.1 percentage points, and concluded that either "lenders may be underestimating the risks of AI investments" or "equity markets may be overestimating the future cash flows AI could generate". Palihapitiya's essay cites that finding, and this week's repricing reads as lenders moving toward the first explanation for one borrower. The bear case is older than this week too. Fortune's Jeremy Kahn wrote in November 2025 that CoreWeave was "a leading candidate to be ground-zero for a future AI financial meltdown", when the company carried about $11 billion of debt and Luria already named bankruptcy the likeliest five-year outcome; the June balance sheet shows that debt has roughly tripled since. McKnight's remark about booms ending in tears has scholarship behind it: Andrew Odlyzko calls the 1840s British Railway Mania "the greatest technology mania in history", and the Richmond Fed's account of the telecom boom that began in 1997 attributes it to market forecasts that proved "highly inaccurate"; Semafor recalled this week that telecom bonds cracked six months before the dot-com crash. The GPU-backed, bank-arranged facilities McBee wants to extend, and the mismatch between six-year depreciation and three-year contracts, were set out in a September Yesterday in AI piece on the revenue the AI buildout needs.
