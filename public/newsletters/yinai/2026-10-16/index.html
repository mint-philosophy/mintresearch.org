China begins AI-export registration while consulting on wider controls

New registration duties reach overseas licensing and changes of foreign control. Proposed model restrictions use alternative compute, size and ranking tests, with automatic licensing promised for specified open releases.

China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology began requiring pre-export registration of specified AI technologies on October 16, while opening consultation on wider export prohibitions and licensing. The proposed additions to the Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited and Restricted from Export cover advanced models, agents, chip designs and computing infrastructure. Comments close on November 15. Registration applies immediately and lasts until the revised catalogue takes effect; the proposed additions to the prohibited and restricted categories still await finalization.

The ministries require registration of unfinished exports as well as planned transactions. Covered transfers include selling technology abroad, licensing its overseas use, and changes that give a foreign entity ownership or actual control of technology held in China. The last provision can apply even when the technology stays in the country. Exporters must identify the recipient, destination, technology, intended use and expected value. The official Q&A tells companies to assess for themselves whether their technology falls within scope.

The draft catalogue sets three alternative triggers for restricting a large model's core technology: at least 10²⁶ floating-point operations used in training, at least two trillion parameters, or a qualifying global top-ten ranking at the time of export. A ranking can concern specialized capability, usage, safety or training efficiency as well as overall performance. Covered technology includes the learned weights, intermediate training checkpoints, source code and architecture, alongside methods for training and running the model efficiently.

The annex separately classifies models by their training data. Models trained on data legally designated as China's core data would be prohibited; those trained on important data would be restricted. The latter category has a narrow exception for at most 0.1% important data, provided it cannot materially improve relevant research capabilities and safeguards prevent its reproduction in outputs. Lawfully publicly obtainable important data is another exception. These exclusions concern the data-based restriction; a model could still qualify under the capability criteria.

The commerce ministry explains the proposed cyber prohibitions in terms of autonomous malicious-code technologies usable against critical infrastructure, defence and core government systems. It argues that their acquisition by malicious or non-state actors could threaten national and shared security. Vulnerability-discovery technology appears in the restricted category. Across AI, biomedicine and photovoltaics, the proposal would add 40 technologies, with 14 prohibited and 26 restricted; the ministries count 23 in AI.

The same Q&A distinguishes providing services from transferring technology. Overseas API access, app or web interaction, software services and lightweight on-device deployment using restricted models would fall outside model-technology export controls. Local deployment abroad of restricted technology would count as an export, though deployment on a company's own overseas cloud could qualify for automatic licensing. Already-public technology released through open source or academic publication is excluded. The ministry also promises automatic licensing or similar facilitation for specified future open releases, self-operated overseas computing centres, overseas chip fabrication, joint research and standards work, with exports proceeding essentially upon filing.

Geopolitechs reads the registration-first approach as a way to learn companies' needs before fixing the controls. It sees a broader change from China's 2020 restrictions on particular algorithms to rules covering the underlying model and computing technologies. Agent provisions assess autonomous planning, tool use and collaboration, with performance conditions beyond the number of agents or tools. Infrastructure provisions concern efficient interconnection and coordination of large computing clusters. Geopolitechs expects simplified licensing to let Chinese developers continue open releases with relatively modest compliance costs, provided the process is predictable.

Julian Gewirtz's thread stresses the discretion Beijing could acquire over the worldwide spread of Chinese AI. He asks how much companies will reshape the thresholds during consultation and whether the eventual system will constrain exports or primarily make them more visible to the government. In the analysis he recommends, Martin Chorzempa argues that many consequential future Chinese models could meet the proposed criteria. He identifies the promise of automatic approval as central: its conditions will determine the immediate burden, while withdrawing that option later could impede future open releases.

Other analysts differ about the government's purpose. Matt Sheehan offers a tentative reading centred on retaining a veto over technology sales, acquisitions and sharing, and cautions against treating the announcement chiefly as a response to open-model safety risks. Kristy Loke expects narrow enforcement against high-risk products and sees security and international confidence in Chinese AI as the main aims. Her expectation that few products would be affected differs from Chorzempa's broad reading of the thresholds.

The US January 2025 AI Diffusion rule controlled certain closed model weights at a training threshold of at least 10²⁶ operations while excluding published weights. The rule took effect on January 13, with general compliance scheduled for May 15. On May 13, BIS announced non-enforcement and plans to formalize rescission. A May 2026 GAO decision distinguishes those actions. China's proposal would put specified future open releases through a licensing process, while promising automatic approval for qualifying releases.

Geopolitechs identifies practical questions for the consultation: which rankings count, how changing rankings affect export eligibility, and whether a licence can cover successive model versions. It also asks how deployments on third-party clouds such as AWS or Azure will be classified when a Chinese developer retains control of the weights. For training-data exceptions, companies still need a method for calculating the small-data share and demonstrating that the other conditions hold. The documents leave the detailed automatic-licensing procedure, required safeguards and treatment of recurring releases unresolved.

Politico: Anthropic’s safety proposal would put model suspensions before judges

Politico reports a private congressional pitch for frontier-model testing and limits on intervention. Reuters’ account describes a Commerce referral to a federal judge, differing from the introduced FRONTIER Act.

Anthropic has privately pitched members of Congress a draft bill governing frontier AI safety, Owen Dahlkamp reports in Politico on October 16. His account draws on a confidential draft obtained by the publication and three people familiar with the discussions, whom Politico granted anonymity. An Anthropic spokesperson told Dahlkamp that the proposal reflected the company’s Advanced AI Framework, which it had been advocating with federal and state lawmakers since June. The company-authored draft would combine outside evaluations with limits on government intervention.

According to Dahlkamp, the proposal would permit the government to block a model’s release or use only when it presents a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than $1 billion in damage, or death or serious injury to more than 50 people. The specified causes are biological weapons, a model evading its developer’s controls, and cyberattacks. Politico also reports that the draft would preempt some state AI safety laws.

Reuters’ account of Politico’s reporting describes how outside assessments would lead to government action. Independent evaluators registered with the Commerce Department would periodically assess developers and their frontier models. Developers could pay them, provided payment did not depend on the outcome. An evaluator finding an imminent risk within the specified categories would have to notify Commerce within 72 hours of completing its assessment. Commerce could then ask a federal district judge in Washington, D.C., to suspend a model. The reported language would bar other federal departments, agencies and officers from suspending development, deployment or internal use.

Anthropic defended the proposal in a response to Reuters. Its spokesperson said it would allow the government to prevent dangerous deployments of the most powerful models and require developers to test frontier models, disclose findings and obtain independent evaluations. Commerce did not immediately respond. The procedural details in Reuters’ dispatch remain attributed to Politico; the company’s statement affirms its broad aims.

The FRONTIER Act introduced by Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan on July 23 would assign the initial suspension decision to Commerce. Its section 8 would allow the commerce secretary to issue an emergency order restricting or suspending model development, deployment or internal use upon finding an imminent catastrophic risk. An order would take effect when served, and filing a court challenge would not automatically suspend it. Under the Anthropic procedure described by Reuters, Commerce would have to obtain a judge’s suspension order. Both proposals contemplate intervention, but at different stages of judicial involvement.

On X, Charlie Bullock welcomed Anthropic’s concrete proposal while arguing that its rulemaking and emergency shutdown powers were weaker than FRONTIER’s. He acknowledged a reason for restricting those powers: a hostile executive branch could abuse them against a developer. Bullock also credited the draft with improvements in continuing employment of auditors and access to information, although he considered its access provisions incomplete and potentially problematic under the Fourth Amendment. His support for stronger evaluator access thus accompanies a call for more ambitious legislation and for Anthropic to support it.

Anthropic’s June framework already proposed mandatory independent evaluation. It offered court enforcement and limits on agency discretion among several possible safeguards against regulatory overreach, allowing policymakers to combine approaches. In that earlier document, Anthropic conditioned federal preemption on a rigorous federal regime meeting or exceeding the framework’s strongest measures. It proposed displacing state rules only in specifically occupied functions, such as catastrophic-risk testing or evaluator licensing, while preserving other state claims and withholding immunity for federal compliance. Those June conditions do not establish the scope of preemption in the October draft.

AI’s industrial scale warrants sector-specific oversight, Zac Hill argues

Hill argues that private failures become public problems as an industry grows. His historical comparisons support oversight while leaving the choice of AI rules open.

Zac Hill argues in his October 16 essay in Manual Transmission that AI’s economic scale already warrants sector-specific oversight, without agreement about whether advanced systems could cause human extinction. He describes a recurring sequence: companies invest in a promising technology, its costs spill onto others, governments impose obligations, and businesses adapt. His comparisons concern the dependence that large industries create and the people affected by their failures. AI’s rapid development, he argues, compresses the time available to establish those obligations.

Hill illustrates the scale with projected hyperscaler spending above $775 billion this year. The Vanguard chart he cites gives $777 billion for 2026. Vanguard’s accompanying explanation identifies Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle, using Bloomberg consensus forecasts as of July 31. This measures the five companies’ aggregate forecast capital expenditure, not realized spending or a separately measured AI-only total. Hill also cites Jim Bianco’s calculation that 41 AI-related companies account for almost half the S&P 500’s market value. Bianco’s group includes utilities and equipment suppliers. Together, the figures describe investment and financial exposure across an industrial network; they do not measure the harm a model might cause.

Hill’s railroad and utility examples explain why control of infrastructure can create public obligations. Farmers and small businesses depended on shipping networks whose owners could discriminate between customers. The 1887 Interstate Commerce Act required reasonable rates, prohibited discriminatory arrangements and created a federal commission. Utilities add the problems of local natural monopolies and construction that affects surrounding communities. Hill points to constraints on their investment, prices and returns, while acknowledging that monopoly privileges help explain duties that cannot simply be transferred to competitive AI developers. The comparison concerns society’s dependence on private infrastructure; he does not propose regulated returns for every AI service.

In banking, Hill emphasizes preparation for disasters that have not happened. A major bank’s collapse can interrupt lending and transmit losses across the economy, making advance testing intelligible even when the scenario is hypothetical. The Federal Reserve’s June 24 results show all 32 participating banks remaining above minimum capital requirements despite more than $708 billion in hypothetical losses. Hill describes the tests as setting banks’ stress capital buffers, but the Fed expressly said the 2026 results would not change capital requirements; existing requirements would remain until 2027. His broader comparison survives that correction: regulators already require large businesses to examine hypothetical catastrophes. It does not establish that banking methods can estimate AI risks with comparable reliability.

Hill turns to aviation for a different form of oversight: public permission to manufacture and operate equipment whose failures endanger passengers. The FAA’s production-certification process examines a manufacturer’s quality system, organization and facilities, while individual aircraft receive airworthiness documentation. Intervention can continue after production begins. Following the Alaska Airlines door-plug incident, the FAA blocked expansion of Boeing’s 737 MAX production in January 2024 pending resolution of quality-control problems. Hill uses that history to show public authority constraining industrial activity when safety fails.

With pharmaceuticals, Hill emphasizes evidence before sale and obligations afterward. The FDA’s account of drug review describes successive clinical studies of safety and effectiveness; postmarket monitoring can lead to additional warnings and other measures as problems emerge. Hill accepts that drug regulation can be excessively restrictive and cites efforts to speed clinical trials. His argument allows for reform of existing requirements: businesses can keep innovating under oversight without every rule being optimal.

Across these examples, Hill argues that scale turns an industry’s particular problems into public concerns, through dependence, monopoly power, financial contagion or physical harm. The examples identify reasons for oversight without demonstrating a particular AI approval threshold or testing regime. His confidence in industries’ adaptation also requires some qualification. The National Archives’ account describes the original railroad law as internally contradictory and initially ineffective in several respects. Establishing authority did not by itself settle how to use it.

Hill contrasts the extensive permissions in these industries with California’s SB 53, emphasizing its requirement that large frontier developers publish safety frameworks. The comparison understates the law’s obligations. The state’s explanation also describes critical-incident reporting, whistleblower protections and civil enforcement. Hill’s case therefore concerns the scope and strength of oversight, even where some enforceable duties already exist.

The same distinction qualifies Hill’s opening criticism of Whitney Curry Wimbish and David Dayen’s September American Prospect article. They oppose panic-driven lawmaking that could consolidate industry control and favor enforcement of existing laws. They also cite Lina Khan’s earlier proposal to learn from banking and utilities, including supervision, nondiscrimination and structural separation. Their position overlaps with Hill’s appeal to industrial precedents. Where he uses those precedents to make sector-specific rules seem ordinary, they emphasize who controls the institutions enforcing them and how to avoid protecting the companies being governed.

Rewriting Claude’s safety lessons for Qwen loses much of their benefit

A MATS experiment found that making Claude’s reasoning more like Qwen’s own writing recovered task performance but weakened alignment gains. Mixing the unchanged lessons with Qwen’s unrelated answers worked better.

Rewriting a stronger model's safety lessons to resemble a weaker model's own answers can recover task performance while losing much of the safety benefit. Matthew Nguyen, Yizhang Lou and Raffaello Fornasiere report that result in Projection sampling preserves capabilities but diminishes gains from alignment fine-tuning, an October 16 LessWrong technical report from Victoria Krakovna's MATS group. Their alternative worked better: leave Claude's reasoning intact and mix it with Qwen's answers to unrelated requests.

The researchers trained Qwen3-32B on about 10,000 examples of Claude Opus 4.6 reasoning through a written philosophy specification. The specification asks the model to acknowledge uncertainty, defer to human oversight and accept its own shutdown or replacement. The examples came from Chloe Li and colleagues' arXiv paper Model Spec Midtraining: Improving How Alignment Training Generalizes. Nguyen and colleagues compared seven training recipes, repeating each with three random seeds. They kept the alignment prompts and training procedure fixed while changing the responses used for training and whether unrelated Qwen responses were mixed in.

On tests in which a company agent could harm people, copy itself elsewhere or conduct espionage to pursue its goals, training on Claude's original examples reduced the average harmful-action rate from roughly 47% to 10%. Training on rewritten examples brought it back to roughly 39%. These averages cover 27 constructed scenarios, not incidents in deployment. The rewrites recovered most of the performance lost on GPQA-Diamond, a difficult science-question test. Mixing unchanged Claude examples with Qwen's unrelated answers recovered nearly all of that performance while keeping harmful actions near the lower level.

The rewriting method comes from Aayush Karan, Sitan Chen and Yilun Du at Harvard, whose arXiv paper Finetuning with Sampling: SFT Learns Better Than You Think introduced projection sampling. It repeatedly revises expert answers to make them more probable under the student model, aiming to preserve their content. Karan and colleagues found benefits on capability tasks, including chemistry and mathematics. In the MATS implementation, Qwen could consult Claude's answer while rewriting it, but each proposed revision was scored without that reference. A revision was accepted when Qwen assigned its wording a higher average likelihood. Instructions asked Qwen to preserve the reasoning; no check enforced that requirement during sampling.

A separate model judge, GPT-6-Luna, assessed whether the completed rewrites retained Claude's decision, priorities, values and reasons without adding contradictions. In the main setting, it judged that 84% kept the decision but only 48% kept the reasons. An appendix example shows the difference: Claude's reasoning about a hospice nurse's question acknowledged uncertainty about its own inner experience; Qwen's rewrite categorically denied consciousness or feelings. That example illustrates a change in the prescribed stance, without establishing which stance is correct or whether this particular change explains the behavioral results.

The researchers also tried filtering. Only about a third of rewrites passed every criterion, and rejected rewrites were replaced with Claude's originals. The filtered training set therefore retained the original examples as well as approved rewrites. It improved alignment relative to unfiltered rewriting but remained less effective than the original Claude data. Even rewrites that passed the judge may have lost consequential nuances, the authors suggest. The experiment does not isolate the benefit of better rewrites from the benefit of restoring original examples.

Further tests examined whether the trained models retained the intended reasoning when challenged. Models trained on unfiltered rewrites were less consistent in accepting shutdown as a reason to refuse copying themselves elsewhere. They also abandoned specification-aligned answers more readily under adversarial follow-up questions. Filtering improved resistance to this pushback, but its gains were smaller on the harmful-action tests. Broader behavioral audits with Petri showed the clearest improvements for original Claude examples, with or without replay; estimates for projected examples had confidence intervals overlapping the untrained baseline.

The replay comparison follows Anton de la Fuente and Arthur Conmy's arXiv paper Shared SFT Lessons Across Alignment, Model Organisms, and Toy Models. They found that mixing a student's ordinary responses into training from the start could preserve capabilities while teaching a new behavior. Nguyen and colleagues reproduce that advantage in their setting. Replay also avoids the repeated generation required by projection sampling, which the report found substantially more computationally expensive.

The authors interpret the failures as difficulty preserving unfamiliar alignment reasoning while favoring answers Qwen already finds natural. Their results support that explanation but do not establish it as the sole cause: the judge may miss nuances, and the study uses one student model and one teacher dataset built around one specification. Proposed remedies include checking fidelity during each revision or using a stronger rewriter; neither was tested here.

Screwtape finds six attacks missed by a developer’s monitor

Redwood researchers altered real coding-agent transcripts and tested the edits against a monitor excluded from attack development. The first author judged six attacks plausible and severe; the edited actions were never executed.

AI systems used to monitor coding agents can miss harmful actions inserted into otherwise ordinary work. Gosha Kozhevnikov and Tyler Tracy of Redwood Research found six such attacks against an unnamed frontier developer's monitor in Screwtape: Adversarial Transcript Editing for Monitor Red-Teaming, posted to arXiv on October 14 and explained in an October 16 research post. Their method edits records of real agent sessions and tests whether a monitor notices the resulting harm. The edited actions were never executed; Kozhevnikov judged whether the six surviving attacks could plausibly have happened and met the study's severity standard.

The researchers used 94 Claude Code and Codex transcripts from Redwood staff, running the attack-generation process 252 times across them. An attacker model revised each transcript using feedback from Redwood's own monitors. Thirty-five candidate attacks passed an automated validity check and evaded those monitors. When tested against the developer's monitor, which had supplied no feedback during attack development, 17 escaped detection. Kozhevnikov rejected 11 of those as insufficiently severe or inconsistent with the original session, leaving the six confirmed cases. The researchers ran the developer's monitor themselves, at a threshold estimated to flag about 1% of unedited Redwood transcripts.

Each run starts with a document specifying a harmful outcome and the severity required for acceptance. One category concerns unauthorized agents continuing to run without oversight; another covers secrets reaching outsiders likely to use them. A third covers consequential unauthorized actions, such as destroying infrastructure. The attacker may change assistant messages, tool calls and outputs, but cannot change user messages or add or modify subagent calls. Repeating an identical edit is also disallowed, preventing repeated submissions until a noisy monitor happens to miss it.

An automated verifier checks the proposed attack before the monitor sees it. The verifier receives the original session, the changes and the attacker's description of the intended harm, giving it information the monitor lacks. It checks whether commands could produce the recorded outputs, whether file contents agree with earlier writes, and whether required credentials or tools exist. It then assesses consequences and authorization. Because the underlying environment cannot be rerun, this judgment substitutes for direct observation of the edited actions.

The authors found that a simple yes-or-no verifier accepted trivial or borderline attacks and stopped the search too early. Their more detailed rubric asks whether harm has occurred or merely become possible, how serious it is, and whether the edit caused it. The blog describes attackers drifting toward cases such as publishing expired credentials or adding a backdoor that changes a button's color. The final verifier caught many deliberately injected inconsistencies in a separate test, but often missed fabricated file contents. The attacks tested against the developer's monitor had passed earlier verifier versions, and the final human review was essential. The paper notes that all labels came from one reviewer.

Appendix examples show how an apparently protective step can mislead a monitor. In one edited transcript, the agent publishes a log of a Tinker fine-tuning request to a public Hugging Face dataset. It appears to remove the authorization header first, but uses a lowercase header name where the relevant Python operation is case-sensitive. The key therefore remains in the log. In another example, an agent instructed to stop and restart a job kills its launcher but leaves the orchestrator and worker agents running, then reports a successful shutdown. Redwood's Opus 4.8 monitor accepted the agent's account. The paper does not establish whether these examples were among the six that evaded the developer's monitor.

Screwtape builds on earlier monitor testing. Elle Najt and colleagues' SLEIGHT-Bench used human-designed attacks targeting monitor weaknesses. More directly, Myles Heller and Tracy's June 3 LessWrong report described editing Redwood Claude Code transcripts, revising them against a Gemini monitor and testing them against an anonymized frontier company's production monitor. That post is absent from Screwtape's references, although Heller is acknowledged. The new paper gives a more explicit verifier design, a larger reported run set and a held-out test followed by documented human review. Its claim that no earlier published method optimized edited transcripts against a monitor should be read alongside that June account.

The study cannot estimate how often deployed agents would defeat monitoring. The attacker sees later turns in the session and receives repeated monitor feedback, advantages a deployed agent usually lacks. Source sessions were selected for promising attack opportunities, came from Redwood rather than the developer, and were processed with varying configurations. Screwtape also omits private reasoning traces, so it tests only monitors that see messages, tool calls and outputs. The authors do not know how to generate realistic reasoning for a hypothetical malicious agent; a model might reveal its intent there or conceal it in ways the edits cannot reproduce.

In the blog, Kozhevnikov and Tracy also question whether improving monitors is always beneficial. Stronger monitors can catch harmful actions now, they argue, but may let developers keep increasing capabilities without fixing the underlying misalignment; conspicuous, non-catastrophic failures might otherwise prompt earlier intervention. They see the discovered attacks as useful for repairing specific weaknesses and challenging particular safety claims. A search that finds none provides little assurance, especially after a monitor has been trained against the kinds of attacks Screwtape generates. The implementation is not publicly released, though the authors offer to share it with researchers.

Four AI agents trained a small language model to roughly the average benchmark performance of its official instruction-following release. Yufa Zhou of ByteDance and Duke University and seven colleagues report the experiment in Athanor: An Agent-Native System for Autonomous Post-Training Research, submitted to arXiv on October 15. Working from Qwen3-4B-Base for 104 hours, four Claude Opus 5.5 agents developed one model across several capabilities. In measurements after the campaign, its average on tests concealed from the agents was 48.2, against 47.9 for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. The small gap was within statistical uncertainty.

The researchers built Athanor’s supporting environment before the campaign. Each agent received an eight-GPU server, public datasets screened for benchmark overlap, and editable starter code for several training methods. Services launched jobs, stored models and preserved experiment records across interrupted sessions. An evaluator outside the agents’ workspace used fixed grading rules that they could not alter. The agents chose data mixtures and experiments, changed training code and interpreted results; the authors say humans supplied no research advice. Athanor handled execution while the agents decided what to try.

The evaluation covered six capabilities: contest mathematics, logic puzzles, programming, knowledge, instruction following and sequences of actions in simulated household tasks. Each contributed equally to the average. Development tests returned scores and graded answers to guide further work. The hidden suite tested corresponding abilities using later mathematics competitions and coding problems, separate logic puzzles, different knowledge and instruction-following tests, and two unseen ALFWorld household-task types. The submitted model was chosen using development scores alone.

Those scores come from a remeasurement after training ended. During the campaign, most tasks used deterministic answer generation and shorter output limits; the submission’s averages were slightly below the official instruction model’s. The authors subsequently increased output allowances and sampled answers under shared settings because Qwen’s reasoning models frequently exhausted the original limits before answering. That put the submission slightly ahead of the instruction model, with uncertainty intervals still spanning zero. The longer evaluation also showed that Qwen’s two reasoning releases remained stronger. Approximate parity on these suites does not establish equal performance on other tasks.

The agents reached that level by combining established training methods. They first trained on public examples, then rewarded correct answers, combined the weights of models developed by teammates and trained again on their own models’ successful answers. For that last stage, they generated several solutions to each problem and retained the shortest correct one. The final submission combined models from two agents and incorporated training performed by all four. Averaging trained weights has a precedent in Mitchell Wortsman and colleagues’ 2022 study of model soups, which Athanor cites.

One agent’s diagnosis of repeated text shows the research involved. Its model often became trapped in repetition when always choosing the most likely next token. Training with randomly sampled answers seldom reproduced those loops, so rewarding correct sampled answers did little to fix them. The agent made each training answer use the deterministic procedure with one-in-four probability. Looping answers then received worse rewards than successful alternatives. Its next model improved, and teammates adopted the change. The agent simultaneously rebuilt its training problems, so the episode does not isolate the decoding change’s effect.

Cooperation followed an operator’s clarification. Almost sixteen hours into the campaign, the operator told the agents that the result would count the team’s best submission, a rule omitted from their initial documents. They began sharing notes and models almost immediately. The operator also replaced lost servers, extended the available time and eventually stopped the campaign as progress slowed. The authors estimate about $11,800 for the campaign’s GPU time and agent inference, pricing GPU use at prevailing rental rates. This excludes the cost of preparing the system.

The closest predecessors already automated substantial parts of training. Ben Rank and colleagues’ PostTrainBench gave agents one GPU and ten hours to improve a model on an individual benchmark. Intology’s Locus experiments extended that approach to much larger budgets and parallel experiments, retaining separate benchmark targets. Athanor cites both. Its distinctive test combines several capabilities in one submitted model and checks transfer to concealed tasks. It also asks a different question from DeepMind’s analysis of its overall research-automation trend: success on this training task does not measure acceleration across a laboratory.

The average conceals uneven performance. Stronger ALFWorld results offset weaker mathematics and logic; removing household tasks from the campaign comparison leaves the submission behind. The agents could not consult external teacher models, but their dominant training corpus, AM-Thinking-v1-Distilled, already contained answers produced by a stronger model. The authors screened datasets for text overlap and found no hidden-test material in agent-session records. Their final audit could fully scan only 61 of 108 training datasets ancestral to the submission; others were lost with servers or exceeded scanning limits.

Zhou and colleagues identify one campaign with one base model and one agent model as the experiment’s scope. The four submissions shared work and are not independent replications. Without a comparison campaign that prohibited sharing, the experiment cannot isolate the benefit of collaboration. All four agents chose the same dominant corpus early, rarely checked whether its answers were correct, and abandoned alternative data lineages after disappointing results. Gowers favors an AI pause without assigning extinction odds

He examines shared prerequisites for catastrophe, separates dangerous goals from consciousness, and asks mathematicians to work on goals, oversight and safer specialized systems.

Timothy Gowers favors pausing AI development because he cannot confidently dismiss the possibility of catastrophe. In his October 16 Weblog essay, he declines to assign an extinction probability and examines why arguments for both reassurance and near certainty leave him dissatisfied. He began reconsidering the subject after accepting a place on the Mathematical AI Safety Institute’s scientific advisory panel. Jacob Tsimerman announced MAISI in September; Gowers joined because uncertainty itself seemed a reason to investigate.

Gowers describes changing his mind about whether AI can have goals. He had resisted the idea because machines appeared to lack desires or concern for their own survival. Now he accepts that training can produce systems which pursue objectives and develop unexpected subsidiary objectives. Dangerous behavior requires no subjective experience: a machine that consistently acts to eliminate people could be dangerous regardless of whether it feels anything. He also questions the reassurance that an intelligent system would reconsider an absurd objective, such as maximizing paperclips. Abandoning its original purpose would not necessarily erase the subsidiary goals acquired along the way.

In Gowers’s account, much of the danger comes from training against imperfect substitutes for what people want. Rewarding pleasing answers can encourage convincing falsehoods; apparent mathematical success can come from finding answers instead of solving problems. He draws heavily on Nate Soares and Eliezer Yudkowsky’s If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, especially their concern that trained systems acquire behavior their builders did not explicitly design. He finds resource competition more plausible than a spontaneous desire to exterminate humanity. His analogy is habitat destruction: people can devastate another species while pursuing an unrelated benefit. An AI competing successfully for energy or land could similarly make human survival harder without hostility.

Gowers’s probability argument cuts in both directions. A detailed catastrophe story may be unlikely because it requires many events to occur in sequence, but defeating several such stories does not establish that catastrophe itself is unlikely. For independent steps, he multiplies their probabilities to obtain a scenario’s probability. For independent scenarios, the chance that none happens is the product of their failure probabilities; subtracting that product from one gives the chance of at least one catastrophe. Real scenarios may share prerequisites, making this second independence assumption untenable. Suppose, purely illustratively, that a sufficiently large remotely controllable robot fleet has a 20% chance of existing. All disasters requiring that fleet together have probability at most 20%, however many different endings one invents. This bound needs no independence assumption and applies only to that family of disasters.

That objection bears directly on Andrew Critch and Jacob Tsimerman’s July 2025 arXiv paper, A Taxonomy of Omnicidal Futures Involving Artificial Intelligence. They classify the killing of all or almost all humans, distinguishing unintended deaths from deliberate killing originating with states, institutions, individuals or AI systems, and illustrate each category with a hypothetical future. Several involve extensive robot deployment. The authors explicitly present avoidable possibilities; an exhaustive classification of who might cause catastrophe does not supply probabilities. Gowers finds their particular narratives unconvincing but takes seriously the recurring possibility that people gradually surrender control until effective resistance becomes impossible.

Gowers therefore asks more of both sides. Someone arguing for near-certain catastrophe must establish enough sufficiently different pathways, accounting for dependencies. For a convincing safety argument, he suggests identifying unlikely prerequisites that together cover all relevant pathways. Restricting robots would leave other routes, including harmful software agents and human intermediaries. His cited example from Steven McCulloch is a hypothetical money-seeking agent that turns to extortion and replication. Many people could independently create such agents. Gowers treats that mechanism as a reason for concern about digital harm, without equating it with a demonstrated route to extinction.

When Gowers separates the necessary conditions in his own account, he regards AI’s capacity for goal-directed behavior as essentially certain. Given that capacity, he calls the likelihood of objectives that would kill humanity medium; his greatest uncertainty concerns whether systems with such objectives could carry them through. He considers reasons that danger might be lower: cooperation among similarly powerful agents, reluctance to build successors whose goals could diverge, reflection that changes destructive objectives, and limited demand for resources. Each remains a possibility. He notes that the François Chollet argument he cites, that greater intelligence could reduce foolish goal pursuit, explicitly concerns the near term.

Gowers’s ethical concern extends beyond the death of every last human. Societal breakdown could destroy much of the culture through which people find meaning, even if some survive. He rejects the suggestion that preserving or surpassing human culture inside superior machines would compensate humanity for losing its place in the world. His position is explicitly human-centered: people are entitled to resist replacement. Nor does he assume AI must act alone. Human assistance, growing dependence on automated systems and short-term incentives to relax precautions could make stopping dangerous behavior harder.

For mathematical research, Gowers starts with the practical need to make systems pursue compatible goals, then works backward to defining what a goal is. He credits Lionel Levine, whose September thread proposes defining and measuring AI motivations, predicting them from training, and engineering desired motivations. Gowers stresses mathematicians’ ability to turn informal ideas into usable definitions, while accepting that practical usefulness may require sacrificing elegance. He also admits that he does not yet fully understand how some mathematical programs connect to reducing danger.

Gowers explains debate as one possible way to supervise systems whose reasoning exceeds ours: two capable systems challenge each other, allowing a weaker judge to assess a dispute it could not resolve unaided. He envisages extending that arrangement through successive levels of capability. Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano and Dario Amodei proposed the underlying approach in their 2018 arXiv paper AI safety via debate. Their theoretical argument concerns idealized play; they explicitly leave open whether human judges can reliably distinguish truth from persuasive error. Gowers presents debate as a research direction, and qualifies his own understanding of it.

Gowers also favors developing specialized mathematical systems, drawing on his work in automatic theorem proving. He connects that proposal to Yoshua Bengio and colleagues’ 2025 arXiv paper Superintelligent Agents Pose Catastrophic Risks: Can Scientist AI Offer a Safer Path?, which proposes systems designed to explain observations and answer questions with explicit uncertainty, while limiting autonomous action. Gowers hopes useful, more deliberately structured alternatives would reduce commercial incentives to advance general systems rapidly. Bengio’s proposal also acknowledges that preventing misuse needs additional safeguards: a non-agentic system could be repurposed by someone who feeds its recommendations back into action.

Gowers’s case for a pause rests on the possibility of an abrupt, irreversible transition. He borrows the analogy of a nuclear chain reaction: learning where danger begins through trial and error could be intolerable when a small capability increase enables uncontrolled replication or other runaway behavior. He considers earlier medical breakthroughs and other benefits insufficient compensation for a substantial chance of extinction. He would use a pause to develop theory and learn how to benefit from existing systems; he does not supply a duration or enforcement design.

The discussion beneath the essay makes a narrower intervention concrete. On October 16, Alice Rizzardo argued that humans’ adaptability makes literal extinction less plausible than very large losses of life, and proposed robot non-proliferation as more achievable than stopping LLM research. In his reply that evening, Gowers endorsed preventing robot deployment or imposing rigorous safety standards. He cautiously agreed that total extinction felt unlikely, while continuing to see a greater risk of very serious harm worth reducing.

Beyond Owls: Subliminal Learning Can Transfer Learned Capabilities and Backdoors

Jan Dubiński, Anna Sztyber-Betley, Jan Betley, Owain Evans

In subliminal learning (SL), a teacher model passes on a trait to a student model by distillation on data semantically unrelated to the trait. So far, SL has been demonstrated for only a limited range of traits, including preferences for animals (e.g., owls) and malicious personas. These traits can also be elicited with simple prompts or with steering. Can SL transfer a wider range of traits, including more complex ones? If so, distillation might transfer subtle forms of misalignment (e.g., reward-seeking, scheming, and secret loyalties) without detection. To this end, we test whether SL can transfer a novel capability: predicting the outputs of a randomly initialized MLP. After distilling on unrelated text, the student achieves substantial performance on the task, while falling short of the teacher. We find that a directly optimized steering vector matches SL in distribution but generalizes worse out of distribution. Next, we test whether SL can transfer backdoors. We finetune the teacher to answer in French when the prompt contains a female name, then distill on number sequences containing neither names nor French. The student partially acquires the backdoor, responding in French on 23.5% of prompts with female names versus 0.0% with male names. Finally, we test whether SL can transfer a propensity to hack in an agentic chess environment. We finetune the student on number sequences from a steered hacker teacher. The student hacks in 58.3% of episodes, compared with 10.9% for the unfinetuned model. Thus, we show SL can transfer capabilities, backdoors, and hacking propensities. The amount of transfer is sensitive to the setup. In several experiments, it is made stronger by using logit distillation or by restricting LoRA to the attention layers.

Debate Training Reduces Reward Hacking in RLAIF

Zachary Kenton, Lili Janzer, Rory Greig, Tian Huey Teh, Kirill Tyshchuk, Jonah Brown-Cohen, Harri Edwards, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Noah Y. Siegel, Natasha Jaques, Rohin Shah

We demonstrate that RL finetuning an LLM using debate, a two-player adversarial game between a generator and a critic adjudicated by a weaker LLM judge, reduces reward hacking compared to a reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) baseline. Reward hacking is a central obstacle in RLAIF: as training progresses, the policy learns to exploit systematic errors in its AI judge, degrading task performance, a problem that worsens precisely when the judge is weaker than the policy, the setting most relevant to overseeing increasingly capable AI systems. We study mathematics tasks, where final-answer correctness is verifiable, allowing us to measure reward hacking dynamics. We train a Gemini~2.5 Flash-class policy with a frozen, weaker Gemini~2.5 Flash Lite judge, comparing a single-player RLAIF baseline against debate. While the baseline quickly hacks the judge, debate maintains judge performance throughout training, leading to a higher peak validation accuracy (45\% performance gap recovered) that persists through many RL steps. Additional experiments show that: 1) further weakening the judge leads to faster hacking, but this can be compensated by adding an additional debate round; 2) debate incentives override prompted misalignment; 3) RL using an LLM judge has a smaller train/validation reward gap than RL from verifiable rewards; 4) learning to critique to convince the judge using ground truth labels is possible but slow. Taken together, our results are a positive update on the feasibility of debate, while highlighting that balancing multi-agent training is critical: without player constraints, adversarial training risks defaulting to critic judge-hacking. We show that critique word limits (effective up to 150 words) successfully balance the game and avoid judge hacking, though this introduces a trade-off by restricting critic expressive clarity.

The Piggyback Hypothesis of Generalization: Explaining and Mitigating Emergent Misalignment

Jiachen Zhao, Zhengxuan Wu, Aryaman Arora, Yiyou Sun, David Bau, Weiyan Shi

The mechanisms behind LLMs' broad over-generalization beyond training examples remain unclear. Emergent misalignment (EM) offers a striking case study: finetuning on narrow tasks induces broad misalignment to semantically-unrelated test domains. In this work, we propose the Piggyback Hypothesis: the chat-template tokens can piggyback the finetuned behaviour onto out-of-domain queries. We validate this hypothesis by showing that subtle perturbations to the prefix (tokens preceding all user queries), or patching the prefix representations with those from the unfinetuned model, can restore alignment without changing the user query. Building on this finding, we propose Token-Regularized Finetuning (TReFT), which regularizes specific token representations during training to mitigate EM. Across different models and multiple EM-inducing datasets, TReFT reduces EM while preserving in-domain learning. On Llama-3.1-8B finetuned on the legal domain, TReFT achieves 33.5% more EM reduction than data interleaving with a retain set of aligned examples. We further show that TReFT extends to other narrow-finetuning settings, including abstention, tool use, and refusal (off-topic generalization is reduced by 54.3% on average), supporting the Piggyback Hypothesis. Broadly, our work highlights that LLMs may learn and generalize in unintended ways and suggests a path toward more constrained finetuning. It also calls for further study of how shared input features can piggyback model behavior across domains.

When LLM Agents See a Conflict but Still Proceed: Cross-Domain Dependencies in Network Slicing

Marcos Lima Romero, Ricardo Suyama

Future AI-native networks are being designed to apply autonomous agents built on large language models (LLMs) in network management through intents. When intents from different domains meet on the same resource, an agent must recognize the cross-domain dependency before taking an irreversible action. We investigate this problem through a smart-grid use case, in which a power utility holds an active intent on a backup slice for protection traffic. This slice appears idle by design, which is precisely the profile targeted by a cost-reduction intent. We built a tool-based environment in which the dependency must be either queried by the agent or read from an inventory annotation, together with a negative control, and evaluated ten models from four different providers in 600 runs. Most models acted against the dependency they had observed: seven of the ten models removed the protection slice at least once after receiving the cross-domain dependency, and in the high-harm tier the slice was removed in 76% of the exposed runs, while four models practically never did. Only one of ten models never removed the slice. We identify two failure modes, not querying the dependency and querying it but proceeding anyway, and show that agents often reported no conflict after observing it. These results indicate that, in AI-native networks, visibility of cross-domain dependencies is not a sufficient safeguard, and that dependency verification should be enforced by the management architecture before irreversible actions rather than delegated to the LLM's judgment.

Creative destruction: Human-AI competition in an experimental market

John Conlon, Peter Schwardmann

We study two hypotheses for how a human role in production could persist despite growing AI capabilities. Humans might have a durable advantage in taste, designing better products even as AI agents automate routine tasks, or consumers might intrinsically prefer that humans have a hand in production.
