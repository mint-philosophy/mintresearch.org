Daniel Litt sketches mathematics after superhuman AI

His OpenAI summit talk asks how cheap theorem production could erode mathematical understanding, professional trust, and researcher training even as output rises.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 August 2026 269 words · ~2 min

On X, University of Toronto mathematician Daniel Litt presented a 17-post account of his OpenAI mathematics summit talk, The End of Mathematics. The workshop assumed for discussion that AI would become robustly superhuman at mathematics. Litt then imagined mathematical progress stalling despite abundant automated output, though he stressed that the scenario was not a prediction and that he expected the field to adapt.

Litt sets weekly combinatorics submissions to arXiv, which his slides show rising steeply since late 2021, beside declining MathOverflow questions and answers. He also cites nearly simultaneous, similar proofs of Feige's 1/e conjecture and models replicating recent results from other labs. In his account, mathematicians and models increasingly spend effort solving the same problems.

Litt's 2027 vignette holds academic incentives constant while theorem production becomes cheap. If papers and resolved conjectures still bring jobs and prestige, researchers can ask agents to choose problems, solve them, and write multiple papers a day. Litt expects little engagement with the resulting flood. He also reports that several colleagues have become reluctant to discuss unfinished work because models can perform the last mile from key ideas to a paper.

By 2028 in Litt's story, autoformalization cheaply finds and repairs errors, but readers can trust formal statements without understanding the ideas behind them. Later, agents handle theory building and conjecture generation while humans mainly allocate compute. The profession then risks losing diversity of thought, mentorship, trust, and contact with mathematics itself. Litt closes by asking how institutions should reward high-quality science and human capital under capable AI, and says he remains optimistic that mathematics will adapt.
