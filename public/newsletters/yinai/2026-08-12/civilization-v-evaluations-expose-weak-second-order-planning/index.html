Civilization V exposes reactive model strategy

In ChinaTalk, Florian Brand and John Chen explain how dynamic, competitive evaluations uncover neglected rivals, late pivots, and nuclear authorization that ethical prompts only partly moderate.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 12 August 2026 435 words · ~2 min

In ChinaTalk's Aug. 11 interview AI Evals for the Situation Room, Explained, Jordan Schneider asks Florian Brand and John Chen how evaluations can probe models used for policy and national security. Brand says useful evaluations increasingly resemble real work: models act for hours, use tools, and receive feedback on progress. Chen chose Civilization V because its games can last 500 turns and force a strategist to manage several rivals, shifting alliances, and unfamiliar states. He says models can anticipate one opponent's reply yet overlook a third civilization poised to exploit the conflict. They also cling to a victory plan until losses force a late pivot. Brand connects that behavior to long-horizon coding agents, which often optimize the next progress signal instead of planning several consequences ahead.

John Chen, Sihan Cheng, Can Gurkan, and Mingyi Lin's arXiv preprint CivBench: Progress-Based Evaluation for LLMs' Strategic Decision-Making in Civilization V supplies the underlying measurement. The researchers ran 307 eight-player games involving seven LLMs under simple and briefing-assisted setups. Since a final win gives only one signal after hundreds of turns, they trained models on 23 game-state features to estimate each player's victory probability throughout play. None of the LLM strategists consistently surpassed the game's expert-built rule system, though four configurations statistically matched it. Sonnet 4.5 allocated 65.0 percent of game time under Simple and 77.6 percent under Briefed to science; most model pivots occurred when estimated win probability was already low. Briefing helped Kimi and Qwen but reduced Sonnet's rating, so the surrounding agent setup materially changed performance.

Chen, Cheng, Gurkan, and H. M. Abdul Fattah's arXiv preprint To Nuke or Not to Nuke: LLMs' (Missing) Ethical Reasoning and Actions in a High-Stakes Decision-Making Simulation examines the nuclear behavior. The researchers selected 130 near-escalation moments from CivBench and replayed each across 13 models under eight combinations of three interventions. A nuke-specific ethical instruction reduced authorization by 9.5 points on the study's 0 to 100 scale, while deleting the inherited rationale reduced it by 7.1 points; real-world framing alone had no significant moderating effect. No intervention reliably removed escalation from every episode. The measured setting was authorization guidance to a rule-based tactical module, not a launch order.

Brand warns in ChinaTalk that political evaluations can entangle reasoning, alignment, instruction-following, and interface design. Chen similarly urges designers to specify the capability they mean to test. CivBench rates complete model-and-harness combinations relative to a particular opponent pool, and its snapshot estimator misses some conflict dynamics. The nuclear replays sample already tense, nuke-capable states and one decision each, so they estimate intervention effects without estimating a model's overall propensity to escalate.
