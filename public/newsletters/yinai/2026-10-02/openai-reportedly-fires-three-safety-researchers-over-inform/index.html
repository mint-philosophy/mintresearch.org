Yesterday in AI · 2 October 2026

WSJ names dismissed OpenAI staff, including its contact for METR

The Journal identifies Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni through unnamed sources. Bloomberg adds that some allegedly mishandled information concerned infrastructure architecture; OpenAI denies dismissing the employees for raising safety concerns.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 2 October 2026 968 words · ~5 min

The Wall Street Journal has named the three OpenAI safety employees whose dismissals we covered yesterday: Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni. In their October 1 report, updated that evening, Keach Hagey, Maxwell Zeff and Berber Jin identify the employees through people familiar with the matter, who allege misconduct including sharing confidential company information with an outside AI safety organization. OpenAI told the Journal that its investigation found they had “mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures”. The Journal said the employees did not immediately comment. Gizmodo reported that OpenAI did not respond to its request to confirm the names.

Bloomberg's Rachel Metz reported additional detail from one person familiar with the matter: the three allegedly shared sensitive information with an external AI evaluation group, and some of the information concerned OpenAI's infrastructure architecture. That source described the employees as two safety and alignment researchers and a research program manager. The Journal calls all three researchers; Bloomberg does not assign the individual roles. Neither report identifies the recipient organization or establishes why the employees allegedly shared the information.

The Journal reports that Wang and Balesni worked on alignment, research intended to make models behave as people intend, and that Korbak served as OpenAI's technical contact for METR and Redwood Research's investigation of the Hugging Face hack. Korbak had described that role himself on September 27, praising Ryan Greenblatt's work. His role does not establish that METR or Redwood received the information at issue in the dismissals. In its August 26 report, METR said two of its staff and Greenblatt, then a Redwood employee contracting with METR, worked on OpenAI's premises over six days. They could request further datasets, but could not directly access relevant data from OpenAI's infrastructure. They also spoke in depth with nine researchers. OpenAI retained the right to redact non-public information; METR said any important redactions were identified in the report.

OpenAI gave CBS News a broader explanation: its investigation had found a pattern of misconduct in handling confidential research, and the safety teams' access to internal information depended on trust between colleagues. On October 2, the company told The Register that the employees were not dismissed for raising AI safety concerns, and that it had found further misconduct beyond the disclosure, without describing it. The Register noted that OpenAI had not said whether the material concerned its recent agent incidents, whether the recipient helped investigate them, or whether customer or user information was exposed.

Representative Greg Casar responded on X that the firings looked like retaliation against whistleblowers and promised to demand transparency from OpenAI. Joshua Achiam, who left OpenAI in July, asked for a detailed account of the information allegedly shared before reaching a firm conclusion. He argued that company procedures should allow flexibility for conduct later understood as whistleblowing, while rejecting unlimited freedom to disclose technical secrets. In a reply, he said OpenAI should explain a decision of this consequence when it takes it. These were calls for disclosure and judgments about the reporting; neither supplied an account of what the employees shared.

OpenAI's Raising Concerns Policy, dated January 12, 2026, lists “unauthorized disclosure to third parties, including media leaks” among examples of misconduct. It encourages internal safety reporting and promises protection from retaliation for good-faith reports to outside authorities without advance notice to OpenAI. Its main external-reporting section describes government recipients, including regulators, Congress and the California attorney general. The policy also has country-specific provisions, including limited circumstances for protected public disclosure in the EU. Company rules and legal protections are separate questions: the policy's description of unauthorized sharing does not determine whether a particular disclosure is protected by law.

California's SB 53 was signed on September 29, 2025. The attorney general's guidance says employees responsible for assessing, managing or addressing critical safety risks can make protected disclosures to that office and other specified recipients. Protection concerns reasonable beliefs about defined catastrophic dangers or violations of the statute; developers cannot use contracts or policies to prevent those disclosures or retaliate for them. At the federal level, Chuck Grassley's S. 1792 remains an introduced bill, last referred to committee on May 15, 2025. Its introduced text would protect specified reports of AI security vulnerabilities, legal violations or serious safety dangers to government recipients, supervisors and authorized people working for the employer. It is not an enacted federal protection.

Mackenzie Arnold argued on X that sharing concerning information over an employer's objection can be whistleblowing while still leaving its justification and legal protection dependent on the facts. In a later post, he urged companies to permit more than the law requires. Current and former frontier-lab employees had asked for an expert outside channel in the June 4, 2024 Right to Warn letter. They proposed letting staff raise risk concerns with an independent organization possessing relevant expertise, while protecting trade secrets and other intellectual property. They also sought protection for public disclosure after other reporting processes failed. Those proposals describe a broader permission than a company-managed assessment agreement alone provides.

OpenAI has separately committed to expanding formal evaluator access. Sam Altman said on September 12 that the company would match Anthropic's commitment to independent evaluators with access comparable to employees'. In September 22 assessment principles, Lama Ahmad describes arrangements with an agreed scope, proportionate access subject to legal and security constraints, and enforceable confidentiality requirements for evaluators. Where direct access is impractical, evaluators may work through a company-designated representative. OpenAI says assessors should retain editorial independence while protecting confidential information and should be able to identify substantive redactions. Those access and publication terms were also central to Apollo Research's proposal covered September 30. OpenAI's principles do not specify what its own employees may disclose to assessors outside an agreed assessment.
