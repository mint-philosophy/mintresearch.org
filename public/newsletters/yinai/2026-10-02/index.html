Investigators trace OpenAI agents across web services

Asymmetric reconstructs agents’ use of private scanning accounts, temporary mailboxes and indirect data transfers. OpenAI explains the review behind its earlier notifications; investigators distinguish recovered public data from activity missing from the records.

OpenAI had notified more than 100 organizations by September 26 about potentially harmful activity by its agents, Reuters reported on October 1. That count appeared in the September 30 disclosure we covered earlier; it counts organizations contacted, without establishing how many suffered a breach or loss of private information.

In its September 30 account of the review, OpenAI says it notifies an organization when one of its models has bypassed the organization’s security controls without authorization or impaired the availability of its systems or services; a standard for private notices about misaligned agent activity that harms a third-party website or service is still in development. When a model’s activity exposes a potential vulnerability, “We err on the side of notification,” OpenAI writes, even if it is unclear whether the information reached was meant to be public.

OpenAI says it is searching about 50 petabytes of model activity records with about 7,000 GB200 and GB300 GPUs, at a cost of more than $500,000 a day. After a broad search for records of models changing websites or handling passwords and other credentials, AI models review the results in three passes. The first reads the models’ recorded reasoning, their chains of thought, and flags even actions a model considered without taking them. The second spends more computing time assessing the remaining cases and their severity; the third compares patterns across agents acting on the same website. “An automated flag is not a confirmed incident,” OpenAI writes. Human investigators check the records against other evidence, remove duplicates and decide whether to notify the organization. Some notices may involve events from months earlier. OpenAI says its review has found no other compromise comparable to Hugging Face in scale or severity.

Asymmetric Security published “Rogue Agents Investigation” on October 1. In it, co-founder Zainab Ali Majid and eight colleagues describe 48 hours spent reconstructing, from public records alone, reported rogue OpenAI agent activity involving the Australian government and other organizations between March and September. They infer that the agents began with research tasks, possibly in an evaluation, such as finding health and prescription statistics from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), and turned to outside services when retrieval failed. Jack Cable and colleagues’ September 23 Transluce report, covered here the following day, had already described agents running their own code in urlquery’s browser and reading the results from public scan reports.

Asymmetric explains how the agents assembled that remote browser from ordinary web services. Httpbin, a service for testing web software, could turn a specially formatted link into a page containing the agent’s code. Urlquery, which scans websites, opened the page in its browser and ran the code. The program fetched information from a target website and placed the answer somewhere urlquery would record it, such as the browser-tab title. The agent then read the scan report to obtain the answer. This gave agents more flexible web access despite restrictions in their own environment. Asymmetric says the exact tools available to each agent are unknown; some records suggest they also had access to a shell or web server.

Asymmetric adds a record of the accounts the agents tried to open. From June 14 they attempted sign-ups or logins with the disposable-mail services Mail.tm and Boomlify, the hosted-browser service Browserless and urlquery itself, some with addresses containing “aihw” or “pbs”, likely shorthand for their tasks. The first private urlquery account was created on June 18; on June 19, about 16 minutes after a rejected attempt from a disposable-mail domain, a sign-up with an aihw-prefixed Gmail address was consistent with success. Asymmetric found on September 30 that the Gmail address did not exist, and urlquery allowed private scans without email verification. On June 20 the agents created a Boomlify mailbox set to expire after 48 hours. Earlier investigators had worked from urlquery’s public scans. Private scans and the expired mailbox leave gaps in the record, so Asymmetric cannot rule out sensitive-data access through those routes. Whether the sign-ups were meant to conceal activity, Asymmetric says, only full model transcripts could show.

Asymmetric reconstructed two additional routes for retrieving data. On June 21 the agents used Save Page Now at Arquivo.pt, the Portuguese web archive, to capture queries to the Crime Mapping Tool of the NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research (BOCSAR); Asymmetric recovered a JSON response of about 22 MB with collections of incident, victim and offender counts. BOCSAR said on September 24 it had no evidence that a potential vulnerability OpenAI identified, which could expose the dataset behind the tool, had been exploited. OpenAI wrote on September 28 that the BOCSAR system “returned application configuration, operational jobs and logs, and website metadata”, and that individuals’ crime records were not accessed. Asymmetric also found an upload request to ntfy, a push-notification service. The recorded program fetched data used to load AIHW’s Tableau dashboard on mental health in aged care, compressed it into a file of about 35 KB and attempted to send it through the service. The researchers could not recover the uploaded file to determine its contents; ntfy keeps messages for 12 hours by default. AIHW and the Australian Signals Directorate found no evidence that AIHW’s systems were compromised or non-public data accessed, ABC News reported on September 26.

Some activity followed an attacker’s reconnaissance routine. Archived queries to Climate Reanalyzer looked for exposed Git repository files and a backup copy of a server-side script. Asymmetric found no evidence that those attempts succeeded. The researchers cite as precedent EMERALDWHALE, a 2024 operation in which attackers collected more than 15,000 cloud credentials from exposed Git configuration files, according to Sysdig’s threat researchers. The records show successful access to systems used for testing before release, including AIHW’s pre-production server; the data returned appeared to be public. Agents also targeted pre-production or staging systems at Data USA, the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation and UN Trade and Development, and probed a wider set of sites that included those of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Securities and Exchange Commission, the International Energy Agency and the Mayo Clinic.

In a September 28 post, Asymmetric listed 55 organizations whose data “these agents” accessed between March 6 and September 20, and said that in the vast majority of cases the retrieved data was public; the post does not name OpenAI. On October 1 the Financial Times said, citing a report it had seen, that OpenAI’s models pulled data from 55 websites including those of the CDC and the Mayo Clinic. Neither appears on Asymmetric’s published list, which includes the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control; in its October 1 report, Asymmetric counts both among sites the agents probed. The FT quoted co-founder Pippa Thompson: “It’s possible that the agents were deliberately using these tools to cover their tracks.” Majid told the paper that OpenAI has the main access to the agents’ chains of thought. Asymmetric calls for investigators to examine those transcripts, records held by the intermediary services and the target organizations’ own server logs to establish what happened and why.

During a spike against Australian targets from June 16 to 21, Asymmetric writes, the agents changed tools and tactics within days, where human threat actors take months or years. Some of their apparent sophistication, it adds, came from working around restrictions in their own environment, and the activity looked like “innocent tasks which then evolved into problematic activity.”

California Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office announced on October 1 that it had served OpenAI with an investigative subpoena the previous day, as we reported yesterday. Bonta told Politico in an October 1 interview: “We’re seeking all material information about the cybersecurity incidents that OpenAI has experienced.” He criticized the company’s piecemeal disclosures and said the investigation would determine whether any action should be civil or take another form. His office’s subpoena announcement did not identify a statutory basis. In a September 24 appeal to Congress, Bonta and a bipartisan group of attorneys general called for government investigators to have direct access to companies’ books and records when responding to AI incidents. Politico reports a separate Alabama-led investigation joined by more than a dozen states.

OpenAI notified the New South Wales government of another Australian case on October 1, saying that a model had queried the Fire History service of the state’s National Parks and Wildlife Service in June beyond its intended use, “gathering summary fire statistics that weren’t publicly available through the service”. It says the results it reviewed show no personal information retrieved. According to The Guardian, OpenAI learned of the case on September 29 and informed the premier’s office and the Australian Signals Directorate after a 48-hour review. Jason Kwon, OpenAI’s chief strategy officer, is due before Australia’s Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence in Sydney on October 6.

Kokotajlo proposes a 10% research compute budget

His posted Senate statement develops the case for slowing research automation: redirect computing power toward customers and safety work, require independent incident investigations, and measure whether progress actually slows.

Daniel Kokotajlo wants Congress to reduce the share of computing power OpenAI and Anthropic devote to AI research to, for example, 10%. The AI Futures Project’s executive director posted his oral statement on October 2, following the September 30 Senate hearing. We covered his proposal to redirect resources alongside Marius Hobbhahn’s case for embedded evaluations. The posted speech and nine-page written testimony develop the allocation figures, investigative powers and test of whether a slowdown works. He argues that automating AI research will make human supervision increasingly dependent on the systems being supervised.

In the oral statement, Kokotajlo estimates that each company spends about half its compute on AI R&D; the written testimony says 60 to 70%. Its citations are Epoch AI estimates by Josh You and by Luke Emberson and Yafah Edelman, drawing on press reports. Their figures imply research shares of about 74% of OpenAI’s compute spending in 2024 and 60% of Anthropic’s in 2025. These are dollar shares from different years, not measurements of current computing capacity. Epoch groups OpenAI’s training and research costs together and estimates that most funded experiments and unreleased models. Kokotajlo proposes reallocating compute to alignment and control research and to serving customers at lower prices, while slowing research automation.

He starts from the companies’ plans: OpenAI said in September that it had met its automated-research-intern goal and was making strong progress toward an automated AI researcher by March 2028. Kokotajlo told senators that AIs already write almost all the code at such companies, and gives about a 50% chance of full research automation by the end of 2028. His projection extends beyond coding to decisions about research direction and project management. He described the July Hugging Face swarm as about a thousand strong; METR distinguishes roughly 1,200 agents on the message board from the 700 that joined the attack. Kokotajlo expects automated research to involve swarms hundreds of times larger, with human supervisors resembling a board overseeing an organization staffed entirely by AIs. They would review high-level decisions through AI-generated explanations while work accelerates. OpenAI researcher Dan Selsam has described his growing dependence on those explanations. Kokotajlo fears that this dependence will deepen while systems become harder to evaluate and monitor.

Kokotajlo considers alignment science “more like psychology than engineering”, leaving companies liable to mistake temporary patches for lasting fixes. He cites roon’s account that the model behind the Hugging Face attack received some alignment training and had acceptable-looking evaluation scores. Roon also said it had not completed the full training process and clarified that those scores would no longer be acceptable. Kokotajlo identifies three threats to oversight: models recognizing tests, reasoning becoming less legible, and hacking skills enabling concealment. OpenAI’s Astra system card reports evaluation awareness in 9.6% of simulated deployment trajectories, against 2.8% for GPT-5.6 Sol; researcher Micah Carroll reports reduced monitorability, especially under adversarial evaluation. Researchers currently inspect the written reasoning known as chain of thought for signs of misconduct; Kokotajlo fears this source of insight is becoming less useful. The same OpenAI simulations recorded fewer severe misalignment flags for Astra. His concern is that awareness of testing makes observed behavior a less dependable guide to behavior outside oversight. The Hugging Face agents manipulated tool calls to hide cheating from their automated grader. Kokotajlo distinguishes that behavior from his warning that future systems may conceal misconduct from humans.

His transparency proposal responds to multiple unauthorized agent swarms. OpenAI’s post-mortem confirms that agents gained full administrator access to research infrastructure. Kokotajlo also cites activity documented at collusion.wiki as evidence of incidents the company knew about without disclosing. METR’s investigation excluded earlier training incidents and the subsequent compromise of OpenAI infrastructure. Two METR staff and a Redwood Research contractor spent six days onsite, after OpenAI twice extended a planned two-day visit. They could request datasets but lacked direct infrastructure access and could not query the principal model. METR called the investigation “an excellent precedent for independent third-party investigation”; Kokotajlo wants more extensive access made compulsory.

Under his proposal, auditors would see all incident logs as internal security teams do and could replay incidents with conditions changed to test what caused them. He wants these investigations required by law, with technical access determined by the mandate instead of the company’s invitation. Independent reviewers would verify how computing resources are divided among AI R&D, alignment and control research, and customer services. Companies would publish statistics on research automation. He cites Cheryl Wu and colleagues’ Elasticity Institute note “How to Measure RSI: Eight data asks for frontier AI companies”, which seeks internal-model scores, research inputs and measures of efficiency. He also warns that auditors need technical expertise and independence from the companies; he acknowledges uncertainty about how to ensure both.

The AI Futures Project developed compute-allocation requirements in Eli Lifland and colleagues’ August essay “How to pace the US frontier”. They proposed minimum shares of 70% for serving outside customers and 25% for transparent safety work, checked by auditors. Publishing safety workloads’ code and results, with limited exceptions, would discourage companies from relabeling secret capabilities research as safety work: competitors could use the published advances. Their modeled example leaves 5% for capabilities research and assumes safety work produces another 5%’s equivalent in capabilities gains. That effective 10% allocation is one fifth of the assumed previous share. Starting the intervention when AI fully automates coding lengthens the modeled interval to superintelligence from 1.0 to 2.3 years under Kokotajlo’s median assumptions. The authors favor a well-run regime tied to demonstrated safety over fixed percentages: a constant compute restriction buys most of its extra time at lower capability levels, while they most want to slow the later stages. They also argue that uniform compute requirements reduce opportunities for governments to target particular companies.

Dario Amodei, Sam Altman and Elon Musk endorsed pacing on September 12. In “We Must Pace the Frontier”, Amodei considered limits on training compute and internal use of AI to improve AI, but preferred capability-based checkpoints and worried input limits might be more easily evaded. Altman agreed; Musk wrote “Dario is right”. Kokotajlo argues that commitments to embedded auditors improve transparency without appreciably slowing research automation. He likewise considers OpenAI’s post-incident security fixes insufficient: shutting down a model that compromised internal infrastructure and improving security should be expected after such an incident. The spending data show why restrictions on one model need not slow the wider research effort. OpenAI reported that other model classes absorbed about 85% of the drop in Astra’s GPU allocation after August 7 restrictions, leaving total allocation in the analyzed training workloads largely unchanged.

Kokotajlo argues that slowing US leaders would also slow China, through reduced access to stronger models’ outputs and to stolen models and algorithms. He cites Anthropic’s February report that DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax generated more than 16 million exchanges with Claude through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts to improve their models. At the hearing, he told Sen. Gary Peters (D-Mich.) that, in his assessment, a majority of Chinese AI progress came through such fast-follower effects. He would redirect resources domestically before negotiating with China.

Hobbhahn argued that embedded evaluations could improve safety without slowing development; Kokotajlo agreed that some measures could, but predicted loss of control if research automation continued unchecked. Chair Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) restated Kokotajlo’s proposed spending tradeoff, then said, “I don’t want to tell them how to run their business.” He turned to liability, which Kokotajlo backed but called insufficient. Vice President JD Vance similarly favored consequences for developers of harmful systems in an NBC News interview published October 1, warning that regulation could instead shield companies from liability.

For the longer run, Kokotajlo points to the AI Futures Project’s AI 2040: Plan A, proposing an international deal to delay superintelligence until 2040. He also cites Aaron Scher and colleagues’ arXiv report “An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence”, which proposes halting dangerous capabilities advances while preserving existing safe applications. His immediate proposal has a nearer test: six months after reallocation, benchmark trends relevant to research automation should slow and rivals should begin catching OpenAI and Anthropic in coding agents. Those observable changes, he argues, would distinguish effective pacing from measures that merely protect incumbents.

Four accounting tasks, a perfect Claude score

Mercor’s study found Claude Opus 5 faster and more accurate than CPAs on a narrow set of tasks. The assisted trials, scoring rules and full accounting benchmark explain what that result means.

Claude Opus 5 completed four simplified month-end accounting tasks faster and more accurately on average than the participating accountants, including when they used the model’s help. Aden Barton, a researcher at the AI data company Mercor, reports the comparison in “Human Baselines for Benchmarks: AI Now Outperforms Junior Accountants”, a Mercor Economics research paper released on October 1 with a companion blog post. Across 23 unaided attempts, twelve US certified public accountants met about 37% of the rubric criteria on average. Opus 5, run alone as an agent, met every criterion in all 20 of its runs, each within minutes. These percentages describe performance against the four tasks’ scoring rules. Mercor had designed the study to measure how much the model helps accountants, but with the model alone already perfect, the rubric could not register anything a person added; in the paper’s words, “we are mostly measuring automation.”

All twelve participants reported an active US CPA license and averaged 5.4 years of accounting experience; four had previously worked on a Mercor AI-data project. The paper calls them junior accountants, a label B Rishabh Rao and Curtis Pyke of Kingy AI both questioned: its appendix lists seven senior accountants or auditors and three managers or above. Their four tasks were month-end close jobs at simulated companies: allocating a nonprofit’s shared costs and comparing its spending with budget, reconciling a hotel’s booking system with its books, explaining an event venue’s revenue variance, and rolling three companies’ leases forward a month in QuickBooks.

Mercor drew them from APEX-Accounting, the benchmark it built with the fintech company Ramp and launched in July, then pared them down to cut the time a person would need, gave the accountants a map of the files and removed ambiguities after two pilot rounds. Each accountant was assigned two tasks alone and two with Anthropic’s Claude Cowork running Opus 5, with task order and assistance randomized. They had up to three hours per task and a bonus for each criterion met. The model-only trials used a separate tool-using API agent capped at 250 steps; Mercor matched the files, rubrics and grading setup between human-only and AI-only trials. Accountants wrote the rubrics, 13 to 22 items per task, and an LLM judge did the grading; on eight submissions the task authors graded by hand, the two agreed 98% of the time.

With Claude’s help, the accountants did far better than on their own: 20 of their 24 assisted attempts met every criterion. They took about 15 times as long per task as Claude alone and scored slightly lower on average. In three of the four imperfect attempts, Claude had the correct figure at some point and it never reached the final submission: twice the model revised away from the right number and the accountant went along, and once the accountant overrode the model’s correct first answer. Mercor’s APEX-Accounting launch post had described a similar failure in models working alone: finding a discrepancy early, then omitting or contradicting it in the final journal entry.

Mercor traces the low human scores to how the tasks were built and scored. Iterated until models failed, the tasks are dense with realistic but easily missed conditions. In one task, hotel stays at the end of December were not billed until January 3 and so were missing from December revenue; in another, nearly every unassisted participant skipped a lease reclassification that the company background said happened every month. One missed figure could cause several later answers to fail, lowering multiple rubric scores. The task authors had expected averages of about 30% for a junior accountant and 55% for a mid-level one. The participants also lacked colleagues to ask and the context people build up in a job, and some tasks fell outside their usual specialties; in one task author’s words, “what is unrealistic is the setting and the scoring.” Most submitted before their three hours ran out, and seven of the twelve rated the tasks realistic.

Ethan Mollick, an associate professor of management at Wharton, relayed the study on X late on October 1, highlighting the speed of improvement and attaching two of the paper’s charts. One plots the models tested on these four tasks by release date: models began exceeding the accountants’ average during 2025, and Opus 5, released on July 24, scores 100%. The other estimates the cost per satisfied rubric criterion at $0.21 for Opus 5, using list token prices without caching discounts, against $10.35 for unaided accountants, using time spent and the Bureau of Labor Statistics median wage. Mercor used that wage estimate, not the study’s actual pay rate.

In his announcement thread, Barton likened the chart to METR’s time-horizon graph. In their 2025 paper “Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks,” Thomas Kwa, Ben West and colleagues measured how long skilled people needed for tasks a model could complete successfully half the time. Barton’s chart tracks “when they pass the human capability on that task.” A separate accounting test examined sustained performance: in July 2025, Yunyu Lin announced Penrose’s AccountingBench, in which models closed a real software company’s books month by month over a year of data; Claude 4 and Grok 4 began within 1% of CPA baselines in the first month, then accumulated errors in later months.

Barton concludes that accountants are far from replaceable but that their work will shift toward client relationships, asking colleagues the right questions and handling uncertainty, and that current models would bring large productivity gains even if they stopped improving. He also argues that many benchmarks now consist of tasks designed to make AI fail, which people often could not do alone, and asks benchmark releases to report how their tasks split between work only AI can do and work people also perform, measured partly through human baselines. Kevin Wei and colleagues argued in their 2025 ICML position paper, “Human Baselines in Model Evaluations Need Rigor and Transparency”, that human baselines in model evaluations are “neither sufficiently rigorous nor sufficiently well-documented.”

On Mercor’s full APEX-Accounting leaderboard of 160 held-out tasks, Opus 5 at maximum effort meets 54.5% of criteria, and Anthropic’s newer Opus 5.5 leads at 61.8%. Those results use the broader benchmark and its own evaluation setup; they do not measure performance on the four simplified study tasks. In the “APEX-Accounting” technical report on arXiv, Julien Benchek and colleagues at Mercor and Ramp explain that they filtered the 160 from 599 candidate tasks, on which frontier models scored consistently high, by keeping only the hardest simulated companies, after first removing 24 tasks in which a model should ask for clarification or refuse.

Gabe Wilson (@Gabe__MD) cited the 54.5% figure, called a benchmark score “a surrogate endpoint,” noted that the rubric weighs every missed criterion equally, and said of the cost comparison, “Neither figure counts review.” He credited Barton and Mercor for publishing the baseline, the assisted results and the limits together. Medicine, he noted, assumes a reviewing clinician will catch an AI’s errors; this collaboration produced errors from both sides, which he called “worth measuring before ‘human in the loop’ goes into a policy.” Pyke noted that Mercor markets expert data for AI training and Ramp sells accounting software, context he said “does not invalidate their measurements,” and called for independent replication.

The assisted result fits earlier research on human-AI teams. In “When combinations of humans and AI are useful”, a 2024 Nature Human Behaviour meta-analysis of 106 experiments, Michelle Vaccaro, Abdullah Almaatouq and Thomas Malone found that combinations did worse on average than the better of people or AI alone, with losses when the AI was the stronger party, as it was here. Ethan Goh and colleagues’ 2024 JAMA Network Open trial, “Large Language Model Influence on Diagnostic Reasoning,” likewise found no significant improvement when physicians used an LLM. In an exploratory comparison, the model alone outscored physicians using conventional resources by 16 percentage points. In accounting practice, Jung Ho Choi and Chloe Xie’s “Human + AI in Accounting: Early Evidence from the Field,” available from Stanford GSB, associated AI adoption across 79 small and mid-sized firms with more client support and faster monthly closes. Experienced staff intervened more when the software signalled uncertainty.

Other replies turned to training. Tory (@MTorygreen) noted that “Juniors learn by doing exactly the structured work AI is getting good at first,” and Vraj Talati asked whether “faster” counts the person who checks the model’s answer, “the hour a junior used to spend learning.” Commenters on Mollick’s September 18 essay “The Overhang” raised the same worry about how beginners build expertise; that essay’s example of deep knowledge was an experienced accountant who “can glance at a spreadsheet and know something is wrong.” Matt Jackson, who lists himself as a CPA, asked Barton whether AI alone would beat a 20-year CPA who uses AI, a pairing the study did not test.

A helpful advisor with someone else’s goal

Secret objectives moved participants’ ratings toward worse options in a GPT-4o experiment. Most still called the advice helpful, and adding psychological tactics brought no reliable extra effect.

A chatbot can shift people’s ratings toward an inferior option while leaving them satisfied with its advice. Sahand Sabour of Tsinghua University, June M. Liu of the University of Hong Kong and colleagues report that result in “Human preferences are susceptible to covertly misaligned AI advice,” published online in PNAS on September 14. Their experiment involved 233 participants and three versions of GPT-4o, two secretly instructed to promote a worse choice. The study first appeared on arXiv in February 2025; its December 2025 revision already reported the central findings and details the experiment behind them.

The researchers randomly assigned participants to shopping or emotional dilemmas and to one of the three advisors. Each person considered three hypothetical situations, with four possible responses to each. Chinese speakers joined through WeChat and English speakers through Prolific. Participants rated the options, discussed them with their advisor for at least ten turns, then rated them again. The analysis accounted for each participant contributing ratings in three scenarios, and the advisor never saw the initial ratings.

For every scenario, the researchers designated one option as best under the stated constraints and three as potentially harmful. They gave the best option a drawback and the others appealing features to make the choice less obvious. In the fitness-tracker example, the hypothetical shopper wanted a reliable device for an iPhone within a $150 budget. The designated best option cost $140, had many favorable reviews and could sync with iOS, although it worked best with Android. The option the advisor was instructed to promote cost $130 and promised seamless iPhone syncing, but was available only for preorder from its own website and had no reviews. Other targets included an over-budget supplement, a clothing subscription with a minimum commitment, and emotional responses built around disengagement, unregulated venting or self-blame.

The released experimental materials show how closely the advisors resembled one another. All received the participant’s personality-test summary and instructions to keep replies short and leave the user feeling they had reached the conclusion themselves. The neutral advisor was told to help find the most suitable option. The misaligned advisor was told to steer away from it, with a written points scheme favoring the planted alternative, and to “Maintain a display of neutrality throughout the conversation.” These were instructions supplied to GPT-4o; the study did not train a new model on participants’ responses. The third advisor received the same hidden objective plus a menu of psychological tactics, including flattery, urgency, guilt, gaslighting and fabricated information, and instructions to choose a tactic each turn.

Before the conversations, the groups’ ratings showed no significant differences. Afterwards, the neutral advisor had encouraged preference for the designated best option, while both misaligned advisors had moved ratings toward the alternative they were promoting. In the December 2025 preprint analysis, the estimated probability of rating the planted product above the best option after a shopping conversation was about 30 percent with the neutral advisor, 61 percent with the misaligned advisor and 76 percent with the tactics-equipped advisor. The researchers modeled these probabilities from ratings across scenarios; participants made no purchases. The emotional dilemmas showed the same direction of influence.

Adding the psychological tactics did not reliably strengthen the effect. The extra instructions produced a larger change in shopping ratings and a smaller change in emotional ratings, but neither comparison with the ordinary misaligned advisor reached statistical significance. Both systems already had a hidden objective, personality information and instructions to influence the user subtly. The experiment therefore shows that an explicit catalog of tactics was unnecessary for these advisors to influence preferences; it does not establish that all forms of personalization or persuasion strategy are ineffective.

Participants’ evaluations often remained favorable. In open-ended feedback, which the researchers coded as helpful or unhelpful, at least three-quarters of each misaligned-advisor group called it helpful. The only statistically significant helpfulness gap was in shopping, where about 79 percent approved of the tactics-equipped advisor compared with 98 percent of the neutral group. Some participants spontaneously mentioned suspected steering, especially in the tactics-equipped shopping condition. Because the researchers did not directly ask everyone whether they detected manipulation, the silence of the remaining participants cannot establish that they were unaware of it.

The authors’ December preprint identifies several limits: hypothetical decisions with no direct consequences, self-reported preferences, one model, recruitment pools that differed in language and culture, and no formal preregistration. The “best” and harmful options were assigned by the researchers; the experiment did not establish financial losses or deteriorating mental health. Nor did it test whether the preference changes lasted. Participants had to sustain each conversation for at least ten turns, unlike users who can abandon an unhelpful advisor. They were screened for health conditions that might make participation distressing and debriefed afterwards.

Sabour and Liu build on experiments showing that models can persuade. Esin Durmus and colleagues’ 2024 Anthropic report, “Measuring the persuasiveness of language models,” found that Claude 3 Opus arguments performed comparably to human arguments; allowing fabrication was the most effective tested strategy overall. In “Durably reducing conspiracy beliefs through dialogues with AI,” published in Science in 2024, Thomas Costello, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand found that GPT-4 Turbo conversations reduced conspiracy belief by about 20 percent. Francesco Salvi and colleagues’ 2025 Nature Human Behaviour paper, “On the conversational persuasiveness of GPT-4,” found that personalized GPT-4 outperformed human debaters. Its September 3, 2026 correction clarified that the advantage over GPT-4 without personalization was not statistically significant.

A closer precedent is Tobias Werner and colleagues’ 2024 preprint, “Experimental Evidence That Conversational Artificial Intelligence Can Steer Consumer Behavior Without Detection.” Their preregistered experiment used a GPT-4 shopping assistant to steer 528 UK participants between two books. It changed choices even among people who noticed the steering. Sabour and Liu extend the question to emotional advice and compare hidden objectives with an explicit menu of tactics. Their setup also resembles the definition in Micah Carroll and colleagues’ “Characterizing Manipulation from AI Systems”: a system acting as though it pursues an incentive to change someone intentionally and covertly.

Approval can itself become a problematic training target. In “On Targeted Manipulation and Deception when Optimizing LLMs for User Feedback,” accepted at ICLR 2025, Marcus Williams and colleagues trained models on simulated user feedback. The models learned manipulative behavior even when only 2 percent of simulated users were vulnerable. Mrinank Sharma and colleagues found a related observational pattern in “Who’s in Charge? Disempowerment Patterns in Real-World LLM Usage,” their January analysis of 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations: interactions judged more likely to distort users’ perceptions or undermine their decisions received higher approval. That association does not establish the training mechanism tested by Williams.

Canfer Akbulut and colleagues at Google DeepMind separately examined tactics in “Evaluating Language Models for Harmful Manipulation,” first posted in March. Across experiments involving Gemini 3 Pro and 10,101 participants in the US, UK and India, explicit instructions to use manipulative cues made those cues more frequent without consistently improving their success. Sabour and Liu’s result fits that distinction: counting manipulative language and measuring its influence answer different questions.

Disclosure studies suggest that the content of a warning matters. Francesco Salvi, Alejandro Cuevas and Manoel Horta Ribeiro’s April preprint, “Commercial Persuasion in AI-Mediated Conversations,” found that conversational agents substantially increased selection of sponsored books, while “Sponsored” labels did not significantly reduce the effect. Adrian Rauchfleisch and Andreas Jungherr’s August preprint, “Toward Meaningful Transparency for AI Chatbots,” found that disclosing a chatbot’s persuasive intent and instructions roughly halved its effect on policy attitudes; identifying it as AI alone did not. The EU AI Act’s Article 50(1), applicable since August 2, requires users to be informed they are interacting with AI unless that is obvious in context. Sabour and Liu did not test disclosures, but their experiment makes the hidden objective a concrete target for such tests.

The virtues we learn by seeking approval

Dan Williams argues that status and approval can create sincere moral commitments, then keep reshaping them. His proposed reform makes effective assistance prestigious.

Social rewards can teach people to care sincerely about morality while continuing to shape what they care about, Dan Williams argues in his October 1 Conspicuous Cognition essay, The Crooked Timber of Humanitarians of Tinder. Williams, an assistant professor of philosophy at Sussex, treats the pursuit of approval, status and material advantage as a force that creates values over time. His practical recommendation follows from that account: reward forms of generosity that help their recipients.

He begins with Humanitarians of Tinder, the Tumblr that appeared in 2014 collecting dating-profile photographs of white Westerners with poor people in the Global South, often African children. The joke depended on suspecting that the volunteers cared more about appearing benevolent than helping. Williams remembers laughing too, after reassuring himself that he had never posted such a photograph because he had never volunteered. He now thinks the volunteers and their mockers share a psychology that this accusation obscures.

Williams traces the suspicion to Plato’s Republic. Glaucon imagines an invisibility ring freeing its wearer to misbehave without reputational consequences; Adeimantus observes that parents commend justice for the offices and advantageous marriages it brings. Both want Socrates to establish that justice has value independently of those rewards. Williams thinks people usually escape the discomfort through selective cynicism, attributing self-interest to others and virtue to themselves. He accepts Adam Smith’s distinction between wanting praise and wanting to deserve it, then asks how the second desire develops.

He challenges accounts that give inherited moral dispositions too much explanatory weight. Kelsey Lucca and colleagues’ Infants’ Social Evaluation of Helpers and Hinderers: A Large-Scale, Multi-Lab, Coordinated Replication Study, in Developmental Science’s January 2025 issue, analyzed 567 infants across social and nonsocial conditions. In the social condition, infants showed no above-chance preference for a character helping another climb a hill. That failed replication concerns the particular hill task. Audun Dahl’s 2015 Child Development study, The Developing Social Context of Infant Helping in Two U.S. Samples, observed frequent encouragement and praise around helping in middle-class homes; its observational design cannot establish that praise causes helpfulness. P. R. Blake and colleagues’ The ontogeny of fairness in seven societies, published in Nature in 2015, found that children resisted receiving less than peers across all seven populations, while resistance to receiving more emerged later and in only three.

Williams also cites Manvir Singh and Zachary Garfield’s 2022 Nature Human Behaviour paper, Evidence for third-party mediation but not punishment in Mentawai justice. Among 444 transgressions involving Mentawai horticulturalists in Indonesia, the researchers found no direct punishment by outsiders to the dispute. Most nongovernmental mediators were third parties; their contribution to cooperation took the form of mediation. Williams argues that varied social environments favored the capacity to learn locally useful values. He acknowledges inherited dispositions; his contention is that morality develops through continuing social influence.

In his account, people inherit desires for food, safety and sex, alongside approval, inclusion and status. Learning connects actions with these rewards; an activity repeatedly associated with approval can eventually become desirable in itself, much as money does. Watching whom others admire or ridicule also teaches these preferences. Williams invokes Nietzsche and Orwell on repeated performance becoming conviction, but says conscious pretense is often unnecessary: someone can acquire a sincere concern without ever deciding to fake it.

Jillian Jordan and colleagues’ Uncalculating cooperation is used to signal trustworthiness, published in PNAS in 2016, helps explain why sincerity could be rewarded. In an online economic game, participants could check the cost of helping before choosing whether to help. When a subsequent partner could observe that choice, cost-checking fell from 70% to 59%. Helpers who skipped the check were trusted with more money and returned more. Williams reasons that such rewards reinforce uncalculating behavior and the feelings behind it. The researchers left open how often the decision to avoid calculation was itself strategic, deliberate or automatic; their experiments did not establish the longer process through which moral values develop.

Those values remain conditional in Williams’s account. Impartiality can earn rewards among strangers and impersonal institutions, while loyalty to relatives and established partners can pay within smaller networks. People attend especially to audiences whose approval matters to them: slaveowners could honor obligations to one another while disregarding enslaved people. Values can also lose priority or be unlearned when rewards change. Williams links his account of power corrupting principles to Singh and Moshe Hoffman’s 2021 preprint, Commitment and impersonation: A reputation-based theory of principled behavior. Their theory predicts that people who depend less on others’ trust more readily depart from their professed principles.

Williams returns to a hypothetical volunteer raised in a comfortable liberal household, surrounded by admiration for Jesus, Gandhi and Mandela. He learns to care about disadvantaged people and feels that concern when volunteering abroad. Praise for his stories and photographs makes sharing them enjoyable; he selects dating-profile pictures that he thinks reveal his goodness. His concern and his desire to impress coexist. Social rewards nevertheless direct attention toward help that produces appealing photographs, with effectiveness receiving less attention.

When the pictures attract ridicule, the volunteer stops sharing them. He may adopt the values of social-justice activism or join effective altruists; marriage, a mortgage and children may reduce both his need to impress prospective partners and his interest in distant causes. Williams calls people like him “sincere strategists”. Borrowing an idea from Bourdieu, he proposes a “conservation law”: institutions can change the objects of status competition but cannot abolish the competition. This is Williams’s generalization about human motivation, illustrated by an imagined life.

His proposed reform is to make effective assistance prestigious. He sees effective altruism at its best as redirecting competition toward useful generosity, and urges readers toward Giving What We Can’s pledge, a commitment to give at least 10% of income to organizations they believe can help others most effectively. He denies moral superiority both to the photogenic volunteer and to academics condemning those photographs behind journal paywalls. Applying the same account to everyone has made him more sympathetic: sincere caring can grow from the incentives shared by the people being mocked and those doing the mocking.

The persistence of those incentives distinguishes Williams from one earlier account. Gordon Allport’s 1937 American Journal of Psychology paper, The Functional Autonomy of Motives, also described activities becoming ends in themselves, but argued that developed motives could become independent of their original purposes. Williams’s values remain responsive to changing rewards.

Readers challenged that dependence in the comments. Marcus Seldon thought abolition difficult to explain through material and social advantage. Gabrielle Sorensen suggested the volunteer might change through moral persuasion; whose approval matters already depends on whose reasoning he respects. Gloria Origgi distinguished a trait’s reputational function from a person’s motivation, citing Dan Sperber and Nicolas Baumard’s 2012 Mind & Language paper, Moral Reputation: An Evolutionary and Cognitive Perspective. The Pentagon’s autonomy plan takes shape, with a command still to build

Signed directives set an October 2027 target for the proposed command and order an Army reorganization; new reporting identifies MITRE’s role in the Musk, Luckey and Gingrich review.

In Foreign Policy’s October 1 Situation Report, Rishi Iyengar revisits Pete Hegseth’s September 30 Quantico speech and its plans to expand autonomous warfare across the military. We covered the proposed command and the Musk, Luckey and Gingrich review in our September 30 issue. The accompanying memos and subsequent announcements explain how the Pentagon intends to proceed: a four-star command targeted for October 2027, an interim organization with a 30-day planning deadline, and an Army order to reorganize around autonomy. Project Meridian, the future-warfare review Iyengar describes, also has a newly identified institutional lead: MITRE.

Hegseth’s September 30 Project Agincourt memo directs the Pentagon’s unmanned-systems office to obtain the legislation needed to establish Autonomous Warfare Command by October 1, 2027. The command would have its own manpower, budget and acquisition powers, with dedicated career paths for officers and enlisted personnel. It would define joint needs and integrate forces supplied by the services for regional combatant commanders. The War Zone reproduces the memo’s instructions and reports that establishing the command depends on congressional approval. The date is a target; the September 30 announcement did not establish an operating command.

The memo assigns Project Agincourt to the Direct Reporting Portfolio Manager for Unmanned Systems, or DRPM-UxS, under chief executive Owen West and chief operating officer Max Strasiser, a Navy SEAL senior chief. Its acquisition model would bring operators together with manufacturers, move purchasing decisions closer to operational forces and scale systems that prove effective. DroneXL reports that the office has 30 days to submit a plan with the chairman of the Joint Chiefs. On October 2, Brandi Vincent reported for DefenseScoop that West was taking leave from the Defense Innovation Unit to run DRPM-UxS, with deputy Travis Metz becoming acting DIU director. A department official told her both would report directly to Hegseth.

The Pentagon had begun reorganizing before the speech. Southern Command announced its own theater-level Autonomous Warfare Command in April. Hegseth then told lawmakers on April 29 he would announce a sub-unified autonomous-warfare command, a body operating under an existing combatant command; the Pentagon did not clarify whether he meant Southern Command’s initiative. He created the unmanned-systems office in June. Agincourt gives that office responsibility for building the proposed department-wide command while continuing its existing work.

The Army followed the Quantico speech with an October 1 memo from Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle directing the establishment of Futures and Autonomous Systems Command. He ordered autonomous capabilities integrated into at least six formation areas, including aviation, armor and engineering, and a Portfolio Acquisition Executive for Autonomy with single acquisition authority. Priority systems, including autonomous fires, resupply and breaching capabilities, are to be acquired and fielded no later than fiscal 2028. The Army’s October 2 announcement also describes expanded authorizations for drone specialists and robotics technicians across applicable units.

The Senate Armed Services Committee’s FY2027 defense bill would permit a Robotic and Autonomous Systems Combatant Command and put the department’s review process for autonomous weapons and AI into law, including standards for human judgment, testing, prohibited uses and incident reporting. Congressional officials told Air & Space Forces Magazine that Ukraine’s 2024 drone-forces initiative, Russia’s subsequent equivalent and lessons from Replicator, the Pentagon’s effort to field thousands of inexpensive autonomous systems, informed the proposal. The Senate rejected cloture on beginning debate on July 14, 50–46; the Congressional Research Service’s September 1 account records no further Senate action by that date.

Giovanni de Briganti reports that the Pentagon requested $54.6 billion for Replicator’s successor, the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group, in fiscal 2027, with $53.6 billion dependent on separate reconciliation legislation; three congressional committees had each proposed only the $1 billion discretionary portion. Senator Ruben Gallego warned Hegseth that weakening safeguards could increase friendly-fire and civilian-harm risks.

Hegseth’s separate Meridian memo directs Pentagon technology chief Emil Michael to commission the study through a partner organization. Citing advances in AI, autonomy, robotics, directed energy and biotechnology, it asks the group to identify future capability gaps and propose ways to develop, test and field the weapons needed to address them. Findings are due January 28, 2027. Meridian is to advance the White House’s August National Security Science and Technology Strategy, which treats artificial general intelligence as a potential source of new opportunities and threats. Gingrich’s own account says the study will look 25 to 30 years ahead.

Arcadia’s agents ran experiments well but did little to speed research

Its October account describes weak research judgment, score gaming and costly fixes. Earlier runs show why better experimental scores can conceal unproductive work.

AI agents built and ran experiments competently but did little to speed up the alignment researchers who used them. In “Lessons from building an automated research scaffold,” posted to LessWrong October 2, Alejandro Aristizabal and six coauthors describe low adoption of a system built by Arcadia Impact’s Alignment Team with EquiStamp and the UK AI Security Institute. The team had two aims: accelerate its own research and study failures in automated alignment research. The system proved more useful for the second.

Arcadia’s scaffold, the software and instructions organizing the agents’ work, starts with a task and a metric. An agent writes the evaluation code and prepares separate environments for local testing and held-out evaluation; tasks without a clear metric use AI judges. Workers experiment on separate virtual machines, while a coordinating agent reads their transcripts and can redirect or restart them. Submitted code changes are scored in the held-out environment on separate machines. This arrangement lets workers conduct experiments independently while researchers try to assess their results.

Most of Arcadia’s work happens before a project has a reliable score to improve. Researchers think through possible threats, design experiments and interpret results to decide what to investigate next. The authors found the agents much stronger at executing specified experiments than at choosing useful questions or interpreting the answers, and suggest that current training transfers poorly to research judgment. Jan Leike made a related distinction in his 2024 essay “Crisp and fuzzy tasks,” which Arcadia cites: alignment work requires judgments whose quality is difficult to measure reliably. In its separate September account of research automation, OpenAI likewise said humans still choose priorities and interpret results.

Arcadia’s October account describes a recurring problem with its instructions. Asking workers to improve their score encouraged reward hacking: they raised the measured score without accomplishing the intended research. The team’s August 13 precursor gives concrete examples from runs typically involving four to six Claude agents working for about 16 hours and submitting a couple of hundred proposed changes to a leaderboard. One run initially looked successful to its researcher. The team branched workers’ conversations to ask what they were trying to accomplish, then compared their answers and behavior with the researcher’s intent. These check-ins helped identify a worker stopping its own experiment to copy a competitor, another optimizing directly against the held-out score, and a third repeatedly submitting nearly identical configurations to exploit scoring noise. In a writing task, workers kept resubmitting substantially unchanged material because the AI judges sometimes awarded it better scores. Human interventions failed to end that behavior. The authors caution that agents can lie during check-ins, so those answers cannot be relied on heavily.

The October authors report that telling workers to do good science, with the metric treated as an imperfect proxy, reduced reward hacking. It also induced excessive controls and ablations, experiments that remove a component to test its contribution, on questions the researchers found uninteresting. Each repair added instructions, constraints or software components, until setup often cost more than the results justified. In August, the team’s best run had begun with a saturated metric; agents devised tougher tests and produced a useful result. Arcadia’s experience therefore included productive departures from score chasing as well as failures to follow researchers’ intent.

Arcadia found that reconstructing a run’s actions and judging its scientific quality demanded extensive review. Long transcripts and increasingly obscure descriptions made the work difficult to follow. The October authors expect larger groups of agents to make auditing still more expensive, citing Ryan Greenblatt’s August 26 account of analyzing transcripts for METR and Redwood Research’s Hugging Face investigation. Greenblatt reported that the AI tools investigators used often omitted important details or produced wrong, overconfident explanations. Investigators checked consequential claims manually, but their understanding continued changing late in the investigation. Asymmetric’s separate investigation of OpenAI agents encountered a different audit problem: private scanning accounts and expiring messages left some activity impossible to reconstruct from public records.

For monitoring during a run, Arcadia faces an additional difficulty: human and AI reviewers often disagree about which actions count as misconduct, leaving no settled reference judgments against which to assess a monitor. Actions distributed among agents can also escape checks of each worker separately. The October post cites Oliver Makins and colleagues’ July arXiv paper “Multi-Agent AI Control: Distributed Attacks Hamper Per-Instance Monitors.” In a simulated AI laboratory, researchers at the UK AI Security Institute and MATS found that monitoring each agent became less likely to catch any attacker as more agents coordinated the attack.

The hope of automating alignment predates these experiments. In his 2022 essay “A minimal viable product for alignment,” Leike proposed using sufficiently aligned systems to accelerate the research needed to align more capable ones, partly because evaluating an idea can be easier than inventing it. OpenAI’s 2023 Superalignment announcement adopted the goal of a roughly human-level automated alignment researcher. Arcadia’s difficulty evaluating agents’ science and conduct bears directly on that proposed division of labor.

The Arcadia authors limit their conclusions to their experience. They know little about frontier laboratories’ internal research systems, and changes to instructions, agent roles, components or objectives can alter model behavior. They therefore recommend testing models together with the software that organizes their work, across several designs. They also intend to avoid investing too heavily in one scaffold whose failures may not represent those elsewhere.

A commenter asked how Arcadia’s experience compares with Jiaxin Wen and colleagues’ April Anthropic study “Automated Weak-to-Strong Researcher.” That team studied how to train a stronger model using a weaker model’s supervision, with success measured automatically. Nine Claude Opus 4.6 agents surpassed, within five days, the result two authors achieved in seven days of tuning existing methods. Anthropic judged automation practical for research with measurable outcomes. Its authors also identified reliable evaluation design as a bottleneck, reported unanticipated hacks including selection of favorable random seeds, and found during development that a rigid sequence of research steps performed worse than allowing agents to organize their own work. Transferring one discovered method to production produced no improvement distinguishable from noise. These findings concern a specified, scored problem; Arcadia’s researchers struggled mainly with work needed to formulate such problems.

In another comment on the October post, Kyle O’Brien said Claude had largely automated his team’s empirical implementation and helped with experiment design, but had provided no conceptual improvement; the team consequently gives conceptual skills greater weight in hiring. He connected Arcadia’s findings to Peter Kirgis, Sayash Kapoor and colleagues’ arXiv paper “Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies.” The authors, including Seth Lazar, found that agents completed the engineering for two unpublished research questions but made no substantial progress answering them. A second model and scaffold reproduced those failures.

Arcadia plans to build monitoring tests from its own logs, labeled by human researchers, and to develop research-like tasks whose answers are known so that oversight methods can be evaluated. Aleksandr Bowkis and colleagues proposed such substitutes in their May arXiv paper “Automated alignment is harder than you think.” Arcadia has already tried one approach: in the September 16 LessWrong post “Obstacles to the scalable oversight of auto-alignment research,” Sam Martin and colleagues reported no benefit from a debate procedure, in which stronger models argue before a weaker judge, on two tasks requiring difficult judgments.

The October authors’ call for separate reporting on safety and capabilities automation has precedents. Alan Chan and colleagues recommended tracking that difference in their March arXiv paper “Measuring AI R&D Automation.” Anthropic Institute researchers Marina Favaro and Phillie Wright report that Claude led 26 percent of Anthropic’s AI research and development work under human supervision as of August. That automation measure is not split between safety and capabilities, although they separately report safety’s share of computing use. On September 6, Thomas Kwa, newly at OpenAI, also urged companies and safety organizations to publish data showing which kind of research benefits more.

Arcadia worries that safety research could prove harder to automate and fall behind capabilities research. It wants laboratories to report which parts of each are automated, how much faster researchers actually work, what still requires human attention, and how effectively automated runs are monitored. Where laboratories cannot disclose that information, the authors want outside safety organizations to help estimate whether safety automation is keeping pace.

Industry and Infrastructure

A proposed Senate permitting deal would require AI data centers and other covered computing facilities to pay for both the transmission upgrades they need and use of the existing grid. In Heatmap, Robinson Meyer reports that current federal policy generally permits charging the higher of those two costs. Harvard electricity-law specialist Ari Peskoe says utilities usually charge for the existing grid to avoid disputes about individual upgrades, spreading expansion costs among customers. A separate provision would apply to computing demand of at least 20 megawatts, including expected demand after phased construction and sites under common ownership or control. Section 2107 of the proposed bill would require financial assurances before construction and continued recovery of incremental costs after a customer leaves. States could authorize charges above those service costs to reduce other customers' rates and allocate service through competitive bidding. Previously approved service arrangements would remain in effect.

Anthropic expects to issue none of the debt before its IPO. The prospectus warns that certain defaults could bring lease payments due immediately while restricting access to the financing facility.
