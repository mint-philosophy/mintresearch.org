Narayanan argues AI’s benefits depend on institutional change

In conversation with Dan Williams and Henry Shevlin, Narayanan connects unreliable agents and research bottlenecks to choices about human authority, persuasion and institutional reform.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 16 November 2026 1140 words · ~6 min

In a November 16 Conspicuous Cognition interview with Dan Williams and Henry Shevlin, Arvind Narayanan argues that society must reorganize work and institutions to benefit from increasingly capable AI. He expects effects comparable in scale to the Industrial Revolution, but thinks reliability problems, the need for evidence from the world and the pace of organizational change will shape their arrival. His argument extends from research automation to democratic authority: people retain consequential choices about which systems to deploy and how much power to give them.

Narayanan starts with factories adapting to electricity. Replacing a steam engine with an electric motor left much of the opportunity unused; factories needed different layouts and divisions of labor. Shevlin objects that AI can spread through existing digital infrastructure without comparable reconstruction. Narayanan accepts that difference and treats the analogy as a way to identify questions about adoption. The organizational argument has an economic precedent in Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson’s The Productivity J-Curve, an NBER working paper first issued in 2018: new general-purpose technologies require complementary investment in processes and skills, whose benefits can take time to appear in productivity statistics.

Narayanan’s empirical example is agent reliability. A system that sometimes completes a task may still need close supervision if its failures are unpredictable. He describes improvements of only five or ten percentage points across roughly two years of model releases. Stephan Rabanser and colleagues at Princeton examined these dimensions in Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability, published at ICML 2026. Their tests examine repeated performance, responses to disruptions, estimates of likely success and the severity of mistakes. The published study finds uneven progress: customer-service simulations improved more than open-ended assistant tasks. The evaluation covers two benchmarks with one agent setup for each; Narayanan uses the results to motivate separate work on dependable deployment.

Narayanan also expects capabilities to develop unevenly. When a task has a checkable answer, an expensive system that solves it can help train cheaper successors. Coding and mathematics benefit from this process; judging an essay or choosing a research direction can be harder to automate. He thinks these differences may widen even as models improve. Over a decade or longer, he expects many reliability problems to diminish, but rejects the expectation that automating AI development will immediately remove constraints across the economy.

Williams presses him on AI research: if models become excellent at coding and mathematics, could they build successors that overcome today’s weaknesses? Narayanan distinguishes executing specified tasks from deciding which questions deserve investigation and what evidence would answer them. An earlier arXiv paper by Peter Kirgis and colleagues, Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies, gave agents six days to tackle questions from unpublished papers. The original authors rejected both resulting papers despite the agents completing the engineering. The cases support investigating research judgment separately from code production.

Narayanan makes a further argument about the knowledge those successors could acquire. Medical and economic understanding depends partly on experiments and observation outside a computer. Faster computation and more elaborate simulation can help, but he doubts they can eliminate that dependence. In his November 16 interview, Paul Christiano argues that improved systems could gather such evidence faster by coordinating experiments and collecting data in parallel. Narayanan nevertheless supports greater transparency around research automation and is cautiously receptive to restrictions on systems approaching complete automation of the research process.

Scott Alexander’s July 2025 response to the normal-technology argument challenges both constraints. AI laboratories and powerful public institutions might adopt systems quickly even while other sectors move slowly; improved learning efficiency might also reduce the amount of external evidence required. Alexander explicitly says his account does not require a mathematical discontinuity: continuous growth can become extremely fast. Alexander and Narayanan disagree over how strongly these constraints will bind as capabilities improve. Narayanan’s interview develops the position he and Sayash Kapoor set out in their April 2025 Knight First Amendment Institute essay; Alexander was responding to that earlier work.

On authority, Narayanan asks why exceptional coding ability should entail putting a machine in charge. Organizations can assign people responsibility for choosing goals and reviewing work even when systems outperform them at particular tasks. Williams challenges the generalization: advocates of superintelligence imagine systems superior across domains, potentially undermining our ability to retain control.

Narayanan wants to avoid creating AI systems with moral status so they can remain tools. Shevlin objects that improving general intelligence could also increase the likelihood of consciousness, making that boundary difficult to enforce. He separately questions whether consciousness necessarily entails moral status: a system might have experiences without anything feeling good or bad to it. Williams adds that even systems with pleasure and suffering could have interests very different from ours, potentially including enjoyment of serving people. Narayanan acknowledges that he has not worked through these questions; the discussion leaves open whether useful general systems can be built without moral obligations toward them.

On persuasion, Narayanan separates patient, evidence-based conversation from influence built through dependence. He welcomes systems that help people reconsider false beliefs, but worries that trusted companions or assistants could gradually steer millions of users toward a developer’s interests. He distinguishes both from an unfamiliar adversarial system persuading someone to act against their interests, for which he says he has not seen convincing evidence of the catastrophic scenario. His proposed defenses against dependence include greater transparency and designs that preserve users’ judgment. He also accepts that social applications can spread quickly even when industrial adoption faces substantial constraints.

Williams asks whether a small probability of an intelligence explosion should nevertheless dominate policy. Narayanan answers in terms of democratic justification. A successful record of predicting benchmark improvements does not, he argues, establish comparable authority over forecasts of social catastrophe. Policies imposing unequal costs require reasons that can convince a public with different beliefs and standards of evidence. His example is a restriction on open-weight models justified by a small estimated probability of disaster while sacrificing benefits to research and transparency. He accepts that his own worldview could be wrong and favors empirical investigation of its vulnerable assumptions.

Narayanan ends with the practical work of adaptation. An independent researcher may welcome continually redefining their job; an employee instructed to use each new tool may experience exhaustion and loss of professional identity. STABL, the Societal Transformation and AI Bottlenecks Lab, which he announced on November 10 with Sayash Kapoor and Rishi Bommasani, aims to study these changes profession by profession. Its agenda includes reforming institutions where they obstruct useful applications and strengthening defenses where dangerous capabilities will be widely available. In the interview, Narayanan makes the latter concrete through hospital cybersecurity and helping young people make better decisions about digital products. He would preserve institutional constraints that protect people while changing those that prevent useful applications.
