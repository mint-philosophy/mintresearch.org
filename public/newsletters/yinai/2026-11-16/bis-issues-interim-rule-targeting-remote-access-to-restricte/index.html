BIS extends chip controls to overseas computing access

The interim rule connects chip transfers to their remote users, with a compute threshold, customer checks and a limited recommender exception. It is scheduled to take effect November 19.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 16 November 2026 862 words · ~4 min

On November 16, the Bureau of Industry and Security announced an interim final rule that will require licenses for covered chip transfers to overseas data centers supplying substantial computing capacity to Chinese companies and other restricted customers. Following the debate over overseas computing restrictions reported yesterday, Commerce has issued regulatory text covering remote access, additional shipment destinations and chip manufacturing. BIS says Treasury and the National Security Council wrote and directed the rule. Its Federal Register publication is scheduled for November 19, with effect upon publication.

Under the new section 744.24, a license is required to export or reexport covered items, or transfer them within a foreign country, with knowledge that they are destined for data centers providing more than 12 million aggregate total processing performance to or for a company headquartered in Macau or the restricted Country Group D:5, including China. The test also reaches companies whose ultimate parent is headquartered there. Aggregate TPP adds the processing performance of covered chips and equipment; the controlled items also include specified software and technology. Tim Fist estimates the threshold at about 760 Nvidia H100s. The threshold concerns access supplied to the restricted customer. The preamble expressly includes intermediaries that train, help train or provide models for restricted companies.

BIS aggregates capacity across linked data centers, including facilities connected digitally to share resources, balance workloads and replicate data as one system. Dividing the same operation between buildings therefore need not put it below the threshold. Operators also cannot rely solely on the circumstances of the original chip shipment: changing equipment from an unrestricted use to a covered remote-computing use counts as a transfer requiring authorization. Data centers already providing the covered services must obtain a license within 90 days of the rule taking effect to continue. BIS will review applications under this new end-use control case by case. A separate transition allows qualifying shipments already en route when the rule takes effect to proceed under their previous authorization if completed within 30 days.

The rule requires suppliers to obtain enough information about a foreign data center's customer checks to establish compliance. Those checks must examine direct and indirect ownership or control by people or companies in restricted jurisdictions. A data center's refusal or inability to provide sufficient information becomes a red flag that presumptively establishes knowledge unless the supplier resolves it before proceeding. This makes customer diligence part of the hardware supply transaction, with responsibilities extending beyond the original exporter to subsequent operators and transferors.

BIS exempts access used exclusively for commercial recommendation systems from the new section, subject to reporting. The definition covers tools designed and used only to rank, filter or suggest commercial products, services or content; it excludes systems that generate new content or incorporate generative models. Providers must report the restricted company's identity, the amount of computing capacity and their diligence establishing the qualifying use. Other export controls remain applicable. In its preamble, BIS says existing restrictions already reach arrangements where a restricted company purchases the equipment or becomes the primary remote user, consuming more than half a facility's capacity. Those obligations continue under section 744.23. For qualifying existing recommender arrangements covered by that section, the preamble provides a 90-day transition before a license is needed.

Fist supports the rule but identifies several enforcement problems. The headquarters and ultimate-parent tests may invite corporate restructuring even though the diligence requirement examines ownership more broadly. Providers receive no prescribed method for verifying customer identities or requirement to identify every individual user, he argues. He also questions their ability to verify that compute serves only recommendation systems and notes that the new end-use restriction leaves US data centers outside its overseas scope. Jason Baker endorses Fist's assessment.

The rule separately adds licensing requirements for covered advanced-computing shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which BIS identifies as presenting elevated diversion risks. Applications receive individual review, including consideration of the destination's export controls and cooperation with BIS. Shipments to recipients headquartered in the US, or with US-headquartered ultimate parents, can qualify for an exclusion from this destination requirement if exporters submit monthly reports identifying the parties, products and transactions. That exclusion concerns this shipment requirement; it does not remove the separate end-use restrictions.

Commerce also formally rescinds the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule, whose non-enforcement BIS announced in May 2025. The changes remove its country tiers, controls on advanced model weights and data-center Validated End User authorizations. Alongside that repeal, Commerce tightens the checks through which overseas foundries and packaging companies determine whether chips designed for customers in restricted destinations fall under advanced-chip controls.

Fist, Georgia Adamson and Konstantin Pilz had proposed a new remote-access end-use control in an October 19 Institute for Progress report, alongside alternative approaches using chip-license conditions or model-weight controls. Earlier, Hanna Dohmen and colleagues at CSET and CNAS, including Fist, argued in June 2023 that targeted cloud restrictions would need customer-identification requirements to work. The new rule connects that longstanding concern to suppliers' diligence obligations and operators' continuing use of exported hardware. BIS will accept comments for 60 days after publication, including on the reporting requirements through which it intends to monitor compliance.
