Obedient superintelligence could help rulers escape accountability

Stefan Torges compares state takeovers and elite coalitions, with a subjective forecast conditional on superintelligence arriving this century and following its controllers’ intentions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 16 November 2026 957 words · ~5 min

Stefan Torges argues that governments are central to the risk of a few people permanently controlling humanity through obedient superintelligence. In “Paths to extreme power concentration,” published November 16 in Forethought’s ForeWord, he estimates roughly a 23% chance that fewer than 25 people gain permanent control over civilization’s future, conditional on superintelligence arriving this century and reliably following its controllers’ intentions. He expects governments to participate in most of the scenarios because they already command coercive institutions and can restrain companies that threaten their power.

Torges combines personal judgments with a simple model whose inputs are also subjective, adding the midpoints of broad ranges for nine routes. He defines control as being able to determine civilization’s long-run direction without anyone else being able to remove the rulers; they could set AI systems’ goals and then step back. The threshold of 25 applies to an entire coalition. He excludes gradual disempowerment without deliberate seizure and leaves less likely actors outside the calculation.

His principal precedents are Tom Davidson, Lukas Finnveden and Rose Hadshar’s April 2025 report, AI-Enabled Coups, and Hadshar’s profile of extreme power concentration. They explain how replacing workers, officials and soldiers with obedient systems could remove people whose cooperation rulers currently need. Torges adds route-specific estimates and a scenario involving an agreement between state leaders. His analysis of deliberate political takeover differs from Michael Barr’s analysis of concentrated AI ownership, covered November 14, which focused on who receives productivity gains and access to useful technology.

Torges assigns one of his two largest ranges, 2% to 10%, to an autocracy overtaking democratic countries in AI. He considers theft and sabotage, alternative technical approaches, faster deployment and state-directed industrial expansion. He expects industrial capacity to matter more with longer development timelines. An autocrat could then replace even loyal supporters with AI, reducing the number of people needed to sustain the regime. A separate route involves foreign intelligence secretly redirecting a rival’s leading systems. He rates that much lower because it requires several difficult steps to succeed while avoiding detection by a technically stronger opponent.

For democratic leaders, Torges distinguishes an automated military self-coup from personal capture of frontier AI development. The military route requires a force that can carry out illegal orders despite opposition from other institutions. Visible procurement, difficult integration of autonomous weapons and resistance from officers would give opponents opportunities to intervene. In the development route, a leader favors or creates one dominant project, restricts rivals’ access and secures the loyalty of its executives. Courts, legislators, researchers and investors could resist. Torges expects state intervention to be plausible during rapid AI progress, but considers unchecked personal control substantially harder.

Institutional deterioration would make both democratic routes much easier. Surveillance and political manipulation could weaken opposition; loyal AI could replace officials who would otherwise refuse orders. Torges also stresses the remaining dependence on human intermediaries. Unless AI can fully automate its own improvement, a leader trying to control development personally must still rely on people who might obstruct or betray the plan. He gives each democratic route a 1% to 5% range.

Company leaders have more direct access to the technology, but Torges expects their governments to intervene before they become independent political rulers. He considers secretly loyal systems deployed in government, an independent coercive force, and economic growth that makes the company too powerful to restrain. Hidden loyalties benefit from legitimate access to training; building military hardware creates conspicuous infrastructure and supply chains. Each route requires a sufficiently large technological lead, and rapid self-improvement could give a company time to act before government responds.

He considers economic dominance especially unlikely, assigning it less than 0.5%. In “Could one country outgrow the rest of the world?”, Davidson describes how a company might use a temporary AI monopoly to build rapidly expanding physical capital while resisting intervention. Davidson considers this unlikely but plausible. Torges places more weight on the host state’s incentive and ability to stop a firm that could disempower it. His disagreement concerns the speed of technological change and the effectiveness of government reaction.

Torges’s additional scenario concerns leaders of powerful states agreeing to share control or divide the world under AI enforcement. He compares it with proposals such as Fin Moorhouse’s argument for early agreements about post-AGI resources. Moorhouse argues that uncertainty about who will prevail can make sharing attractive to both sides. Torges’s dangerous variant requires leaders to escape domestic accountability: a democratic participant would already need to have defeated or seriously weakened institutional checks. Distrust and the difficulty of verifying compliance remain substantial obstacles.

His other largest range, also 2% to 10%, concerns a democratic head of government and a dominant company leader cooperating. The company contributes advanced systems; the political leader contributes control of law enforcement, intelligence and armed forces. Cooperation removes the mutual restraint Torges relies on elsewhere, while early steps could appear to be ordinary national-security partnerships. Employees could still leak or leave, and officials could refuse participation until AI replaces them. Each leader also has reasons to exclude the other, especially when neither can verify whose orders the systems ultimately follow.

Torges counts a coalition route even if its members later turn against one another and one prevails. Routes involving governments contribute about 21 percentage points to his overall estimate; those involving companies contribute about eight, with overlap through the government-company coalition. His total also depends on a roughly 70% judgment that a country could achieve enough technological advantage to dominate others; he treats the transition from national to global control as a background assumption. Faster AI improvement, weaker democratic checks or easier concealment of loyalties would change the assessment. So would stronger audits or difficulty turning national control into permanent global power.
