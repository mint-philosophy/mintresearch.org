Eighty-four cases map AI-driven flooding across government services

Across 11 jurisdictions, most documented cases involve people filing LLM-written text themselves, and autonomous portal navigation does not appear; officials usually answer with small fixes, while fast barriers fall hardest on poorer applicants.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 29 August 2026 918 words · ~5 min

In a paper accepted to the Ninth AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society, Characterizing Agentic Flooding of Government Services, Chris Schmitz of the Hertie School's Centre for Digital Governance, with Lewis Hammond and Alan Chan, put a name to something officials in several countries have started complaining about. They define agentic flooding as a surge in the volume or complexity of requests reaching a government service, caused by AI agents and severe enough to strain the service, and they count 84 cases across 11 jurisdictions where a government body or a credible outlet has publicly blamed AI for a change in demand.

They built the dataset by scanning the same 13 service domains in 12 countries chosen to vary by administrative tradition and OECD digital-government maturity, from Estonia and Denmark to Brazil and Japan. A semi-automated search using Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview for discovery and review and Flash-Lite for revision, at a token cost of about $200, surfaced 2,288 candidate services; fewer than one in twenty cleared the three inclusion criteria. A case needed a specific mechanism by which AI could cut the cost of submitting, evidence of a matching change in demand, and an external attribution of that change to AI by a government source or a reputable secondary one. Attribution eliminated the most candidates. In 58 of the 84 surviving cases, government officials made the claim themselves.

One mechanism recurs across 87% of the cases: a person uses an LLM to produce legally sophisticated text, then files it by hand. Agents navigating portals on their own do not appear yet, which the authors attribute partly to text generation being the mature capability and partly to sampling: officials can spot AI-shaped prose, not an ordinary-looking form filled in by software. Justice and legal services supply the largest share of cases at 19, ahead of regulatory complaints at 10 and benefits and social protection at 9. Seventy-six cases show filings growing longer or more complex, 50 show more filings arriving, and 42 show both. Some German social-court letters ran past 4,000 pages. A minority of cases involve bad faith, including AI-generated medical certificates submitted for Brazil's temporary disability benefit and organized voter-roll challenge campaigns in the United States.

Legal Tribune Online reported on August 17 that Jens Blüggel, president of the Landessozialgericht in Essen, said urgent proceedings across North Rhine-Westphalia's eight social courts climbed from roughly 1,400 in the first quarter of 2025 to 3,600 in the same quarter of 2026, with urgent appeals to his own court nearly doubling. Submissions often run 20 pages or more and cite hallucinated or irrelevant judgments, and because urgent matters go first, everyone else's case waits longer. The state decides roughly a quarter of all German social-court matters. Blüggel asked claimants for the essential facts on two pages, handwritten if there is no other way, and declined to turn the problem into a complaint about AI, describing subsistence benefits as often the welfare state's last line of defense.

The paper argues that near-term risk concentrates where two properties meet: a submission is financially worth making, and demand has historically been held down by the difficulty of making it. Tax administration and court systems fit both. The authors' risk matrix separates likelihood factors, such as how much effort a submission takes, from severity factors, such as how much processing each submission legally compels and how far a service's budget assumes take-up below full entitlement. Both properties can be assessed today, with no forecast of AI capability required.

Governments respond in 56% of the dataset's cases, usually with something small: guidance on AI use, or a bounded tool covering one processing step. The paper sorts the wider menu into suppressing demand and adding capacity. Fees, in-person appointments, rate caps and closed digital channels deploy fastest and have precedent; they also deter poorer and less digitally literate applicants, and in many jurisdictions charging for a welfare application is illegal. Structured interfaces, identity verification and proactive delivery carry no such equity cost, but they take years and cross-department coordination, which puts them out of reach once a surge is under way. Schmitz, Hammond and Chan expect demand suppression to win by default, and file that expectation under Charles Lindblom's "muddling through".

The authors are direct about what their dataset cannot support. The search collected only positive instances, so no share of services was assessed and no prevalence figure follows; the attribution requirement almost certainly undercounts; only 25 of the 84 cases carry time-series volume data, which rules out even correlational quantitative claims; and while every case passed human review, they ran no inter-rater coding and did not benchmark the model against human coders. They conclude that flooding looks widespread while acute operational collapse does not, and that the likelier costs are budget adjustments and worse service where friction returns. Their recommendations need no new technology: audit services against the risk factors, integrate digital identity into the most exposed ones, and establish through legal review which resilience measures are lawful before a surge makes the question urgent.

David Guzman Piedrahita and colleagues, including Yoshua Bengio and Stuart Russell, anticipated the mechanism in a March working draft on congested bureaucracy: friction had served as the implicit filter keeping public input within what human staff could read, and general-purpose AI dissolves it. Google's usage study of Gemini supplies the demand-side context, finding government services and civic obligations over-represented in AI conversations by a factor of almost twenty against the share of time Americans spend on them.
