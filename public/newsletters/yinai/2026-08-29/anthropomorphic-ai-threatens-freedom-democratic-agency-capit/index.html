Conti argues superintelligence would end capitalism

The Princeton political theorist argues in Compact that superintelligence plus a universal basic income would sever effort from reward, and that conservatives who prize markets should want AI suppressed instead of regulated later.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 29 August 2026 852 words · ~4 min

On X on August 29, Gregory Conti described artificial intelligence as "the anti-capitalist technology par excellence" and pointed readers to Martin Sandbu's Financial Times Life & Arts column "Could AI revive the socialist dream?", whose standfirst says the technology could "reignite and overturn the century-old debate over central economic planning". Conti, an associate professor of politics at Princeton and Compact's editor-at-large, was claiming ground he had already taken at length. The longest stretch of his July 13 Compact essay The AI Apocalypse Is Already Here argues that success at superintelligence would end capitalism.

Conti's economic case runs through the remedies AI's own promoters offer. He points to Elon Musk's promise that most people will need a universal basic income once machines transcend human capacities, and to proposals for a government jobs guarantee once human labor stops paying for itself. Both, he argues, sever effort from reward and retire market-set wages as the main vehicle for distributing income, which he calls "the death knell of capitalism". Nobody advertises fully automated luxury capitalism, he notes, because the phrase contradicts itself. He then quotes at length from Marx's Grundrisse notebooks of 1857-58, where wealth comes to depend on the general state of science more than on labor time, the worker becomes "watchman and regulator" of production, and "production based on exchange value breaks down".

Conti reads the industry's utopias back into that lineage. He places Dario Amodei's October 2024 essay Machines of Loving Grace, with its "country of geniuses in a datacenter", inside the socialist tradition, on the grounds that competition, striving, deferred gratification, and the work ethic are all absent from the world it describes. David Hume, who held that justice becomes "useless" amid a "profuse abundance of all external conveniencies", and John Locke, whose defense of property rests on the earth's not being spontaneously bountiful, supply his claim that markets and private property were justified as answers to scarcity. Against the idea that capitalism is morally agnostic about growth, he invokes the doux commerce arguments of Smith, Hume, Montesquieu, and Voltaire, Milton Friedman's title Capitalism and Freedom, and Irving Kristol's willingness to grant capitalism only "two cheers". He prescribes a property-owning democracy, with rewards secured to conscientious savers and investors.

Conti's appeal to conservatives turns on pace. Taking Samuel Huntington's account of conservatism as the voice of those "fundamentally happy" with the social order, and Walter Bagehot's "enjoyment" of the present, he argues that a technology outrunning any settled expectation breaks the intergenerational transmission of wisdom the tradition exists to protect, and quotes Burke's warning that men "would become little better than the flies of summer". He points to remarks by Will Fithian, an associate professor of statistics at UC Berkeley, at March's Stop the AI Race rally in San Francisco, where Fithian said that "planning ahead about your daughter's future career is so 2022". Where the future becomes opaque, Conti writes, "the moral difference between free and compelled choices vanishes", which makes abrupt technological discontinuity a species of state failure. He also tells social conservatives that the transhumanism many AI developers profess should repel them more than transgenderism does.

Whether adopting AI is voluntary at all occupies the essay's Rousseau section. Powerful technologies alter the fabric of society and unsettle the moral expectations built around the old equilibrium, Conti argues, so the language of choice misleads. He works the point through the smartphone: a third of American mobile-phone owners had one fifteen years ago, and within a decade two-factor authentication and workplace norms made going without one incompatible with many jobs and schools. He borrows David Singh Grewal's "network power" for the way one standard can "progressively eliminate" alternatives that most people might prefer. He cites a publisher asking whether he consents for his work "to be included in new licensing routes" for generative AI, a choice he calls lose-lose, since withholding his scholarship would make what students learn from AI answers about his subject less accurate.

Conti's own classroom supplies the concrete loss. He says AI-assisted cheating has made assigning papers pointless, and that what disappears with the assignment is a student's chance to have considered thinking assessed by a specialist. If prose stops signaling a human mind behind it, he expects prestige to move toward charisma and quick wit, and "the shy and retiring but deliberate and measured person" to be selected out. He grants that environmental objections to AI are "empirically overblown", and rests everything else on political theory, historical analogy, and teaching. For the political mechanism, a technological version of the resource curse, he draws on Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine's The Intelligence Curse, which argues that powerful actors lose their incentive to invest in ordinary people once AGI does the work.

Conservatives have answered him. In Faster, Please! on August 11, James Pethokoukis, author of The Conservative Futurist, named the essay and replied "I couldn't disagree more." Conti had taken the case to American Compass on July 31, discussing classroom technology restrictions, age-gating social media, and shutting AI down with Oren Cass and Chris Griswold on the Commonplace podcast.
