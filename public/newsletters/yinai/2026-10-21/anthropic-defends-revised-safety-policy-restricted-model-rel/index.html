Amodei defends Anthropic’s discretion; Piper asks for constraints

Amodei argues that costly safeguards vindicate Anthropic’s strategy. Piper accepts those sacrifices but questions whether they establish a willingness to constrain internal development and share power.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 946 words · ~5 min

Dario Amodei defended Anthropic’s safety strategy in an October 20 reply on X to Nathan Calvin of Encode AI. Calvin’s October 19 criticism, covered in our October 20 issue, argued that the company’s recent decisions fit an effort to build superhuman AI first, with safety taking second place. Amodei begins by welcoming scrutiny and acknowledging mistakes, then defends a longstanding strategy he calls the “race to the top”: a company can make responsible practices more influential by demonstrating them while competing at the capability frontier.

Amodei lists nine decisions he considers commercially costly and beneficial for safety. His biological safeguards example concerns the cost of blocking legitimate work: Anthropic’s efforts to catch dangerous requests also reject benign research, he says, giving competitors an advantage with biologists. He cites concerning biological-capability tests and blocked misuse attempts as reasons to accept that cost. He invokes the verification program, which gives vetted biology professionals greater access, to reject the suggestion that Anthropic is restricting research for its own business advantage.

He also says Anthropic had Mythos-class models in February but withheld general availability until June while improving cybersecurity safeguards. Limited partners received access for cyberdefense. He believes the delay sacrificed part of a several-month capability lead while allowing organizations to strengthen their defenses. These are Amodei’s assessments of the commercial and safety effects; he also acknowledges that restricting access can delay beneficial uses, including progress in biology.

His remaining examples include advocating a stronger SB 53, calling for government power to block dangerous releases, and the company’s February confrontation with the government. He says export controls strengthen democracies’ bargaining position in AI safety talks, despite creating difficulties with commercial partners. He also cites restrictions on sales to Chinese-controlled companies and extensive public risk reporting. He says Anthropic spends resources curbing model distillation, the use of one model’s outputs to train another, which he regards as a national-security concern but a minor commercial threat. The October 18 public-benefit fund proposal would commit 5% of company shares, subject to stockholder approval. These are prior actions supporting his argument. Amodei agrees that government should ideally decide access to dangerous capabilities, while arguing that developers must make those decisions in its absence. He says Anthropic takes both misuse and loss of control seriously.

Turning to Calvin’s accusations, Amodei defends the chip team, compute purchases and growing use of Claude in AI research as ordinary requirements of frontier competition. He acknowledges that AI-driven research may soon accelerate faster than safety work can follow, motivating discussions about slowing that acceleration. He maintains that the cited investments preceded that point and were consistent with Anthropic’s strategy.

Amodei concedes problems with the company’s letter to Congress about hacking incidents, its statement about doing safety from second place, and its draft federal bill. He describes these as poorly written communication or failures to transmit changing circumstances between teams. He takes responsibility as chief executive, while placing the errors in the context of a company with more than 5,000 employees.

He treats the Responsible Scaling Policy revision as the most substantive disagreement. The older policy, he argues, demanded substantial work with little safety value while missing other important risks. He credits the replacement’s risk reports, roadmaps and external review with improving transparency and enabling more intensive embedded evaluation. The September 2023 announcement did anticipate revision, but also described temporarily pausing more powerful training when safeguards could not keep up. In their analysis of version 3.0, Sophie Williams and Jonas Freund report that Anthropic removed the pause commitment and added nonbinding safety goals and broader risk reports. They value the transparency improvements while noting their reliance on company reporting.

Amodei separately promises to examine the relationship between the Frontier Compliance Framework and the voluntary RSP. He says the former is intended to meet regulatory obligations and the latter to allow experimentation beyond legal requirements. His answer leaves open whether the company has balanced those considerations correctly; it announces an inquiry, with no accompanying policy amendment.

Kelsey Piper’s response early on October 21 accepts that Anthropic has made costly sacrifices from conviction. Her concern is whether that commitment extends across different risks. She sees the strongest sacrifices in biosecurity and China, subjects she believes particularly motivate Amodei. She is less reassured about preserving human control and giving the public authority over consequential decisions, where she sees more disagreement within the company. Employees who fear takeover, she argues, may infer too much from leadership’s willingness to incur costs for other goals.

Piper connects that concern to the company’s institutions. She argues that Anthropic has increasingly replaced binding constraints with leadership’s judgment, although she welcomes embedded evaluators as an exception. She wants stronger guarantees of auditor independence and contractual protection for workers, including the ability to strike. She interprets the draft bill as favoring limits on executive intervention, continuing the concern about government authority discussed in our October 17 issue. In her account, rapid growth and political conflict have made Anthropic more defensive about sharing decision-making power.

She also believes the gap between internal and public model capabilities is growing and should be much smaller. In replies, Piper argues that broader access would reduce one company’s ability to concentrate power, improve public understanding of capabilities and expose behavior missed by testing. She supports biological safeguards, while objecting to the most capable systems remaining secret inside labs. As she puts it, “If a model isn't safe to deploy, it isn't safe to internally deploy either.” Piper allows that her assessment may be wrong, and that secrecy, workload and pressure can obstruct communication. She remains unconvinced that Anthropic will accept the sacrifices she thinks alignment, takeover prevention and public participation require.
