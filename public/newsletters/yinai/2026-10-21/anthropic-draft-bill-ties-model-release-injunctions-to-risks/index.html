The same harm threshold, different powers to suspend AI

Critics question using SB 53’s catastrophic-risk definition to limit model suspensions. The FRONTIER Act uses the same numerical threshold, with a different decision-maker and broader categories of harm.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 680 words · ~3 min

The debate over Anthropic’s draft federal safety bill now concerns the purpose of its harm threshold. Lucas Ropek’s October 20 TechCrunch article recaps Politico’s earlier account of when a model could be blocked, covered in our October 16 issue. Owen Dahlkamp’s October 21 Politico follow-up examines a further dispute: a definition used for California’s risk assessments and disclosures would determine when a federal court could suspend AI operations.

California’s SB 53, signed in September 2025, defines catastrophic risk using foreseeable, material contributions to death or serious injury affecting more than 50 people, or property damage or loss exceeding $1 billion, from a single incident involving specified conduct. That definition shapes developers’ safety frameworks and risk reporting. The statute also defines reportable critical safety incidents separately, including some loss-of-control events. The numerical bar is not a general exemption for every lesser harm.

The publicly circulating September 29 discussion draft attributed to Anthropic uses those numerical thresholds in a narrower definition governing suspension orders. Its specified pathways are biological-weapons assistance, evasion of developer or user control, offensive cyber operations, and automated AI research that would substantially amplify those dangers. Ropek’s summary lists the first three. Section 12 would allow a federal judge in Washington to suspend development, deployment or internal use when the defined risk is imminent. The test concerns anticipated harm; it does not require the deaths, injuries or losses to have occurred.

Scott Wiener, SB 53’s author, objected on October 19 that repurposing the disclosure definition would severely restrict emergency intervention. He described SB 53 as an initial transparency measure and argued that the proposal also lacked minimum standards for safety plans and auditors. He accepted the danger of officials abusing shutdown powers and credited provisions that exceeded other congressional proposals. His objection concerned the protections needed when moving from disclosure to powers over dangerous operations.

Dahlkamp reports disagreement about how high the bar should be. Nathan Calvin of Encode AI considers it dangerously demanding. Charlie Bullock of the Institute for Law and AI defends a threshold that focuses courts on widespread harm, distinguishing catastrophic-risk legislation from measures addressing injuries to smaller groups of chatbot users. ITIF’s Michelle Lopes Maldonado questions whether the threshold remains appropriate, noting how quickly losses could reach $1 billion. Adam Kovacevich of Chamber of Progress interprets the borrowing as a political argument for preemption: Anthropic could present federal rules as equivalent to existing state protections.

Politico reports that Bullock, Kovacevich and others regard the draft as weaker than the FRONTIER Act. The introduced text of H.R. 9925, sponsored by Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan in July, uses the same more-than-50-person and more-than-$1-billion thresholds. Its emergency-order power depends on catastrophic risk being present or impending. The comparison therefore turns on additional features of the proposals. FRONTIER’s definition includes chemical, radiological and nuclear weapons and specified autonomous crimes as well as cyber operations and control evasion. Anthropic’s suspension definition focuses on its narrower set of pathways, including automated research amplifying them.

The procedures also differ. FRONTIER lets the Commerce Secretary issue emergency orders, followed by administrative and judicial review. Under the circulating Anthropic draft, Commerce must obtain a court order and submit an independent assessment, with the judge deciding the grounds afresh. Commerce must establish an imminent risk, the necessity of suspension, the inadequacy of less restrictive measures and an order limited to what is necessary. The bracketed 45-day duration is renewable on fresh findings. These procedures were already examined in our October 17 coverage; they explain why matching numerical thresholds need not produce equivalent intervention powers.

Ropek’s statement that agencies could not independently halt a model also needs a qualification from the text: the Anthropic draft’s exclusive court procedure applies under that proposed Act and expressly preserves authority under other laws. FRONTIER contains its own broader exclusivity clause for federal action substantially based on imminent catastrophic risk. Anthropic’s Anthony Cimino had already said the California-derived threshold might need reconsideration in his October 18 explanation, covered in our October 20 issue. The October 21 reporting develops the dispute over that existing threshold.
