PHP2Shell triggers shutdown warnings and millions of attack attempts

The NSA and FBI urged organizations unable to patch to take systems offline. Primary accounts show how AI helped researchers turn old flaws into working attacks, why repairs took months, and what defenders are observing now.

The NSA and FBI urged organizations unable to update PHP immediately to take affected systems offline. Their October 21 warnings followed the discovery of two critical flaws that can let an outsider run code on a web server without logging in. Researchers used AI to turn longstanding weaknesses into working attacks against widely used applications. Government websites and Wikimedia services were taken offline while operators prepared repairs; Cloudflare subsequently reported millions of attempted attacks.

PHP runs application code behind websites, including online stores, publishing systems and services that hold credentials for other systems. The project's advisory treats every PHP installation serving web requests as at risk, with the highest risk in Apache's mod_php configuration: researchers demonstrated attacks before the website's own code even ran. Other configurations have different attack paths. Updating a WordPress plugin or fixing a site's application code does not repair the underlying PHP engine.

Both flaws involve what happens when a request fails. With CVE-2026-109618, a memory-allocation failure can interrupt PHP before its internal data is ready; cleanup then handles unfinished data as valid. With CVE-2026-109619, a surviving worker retains references to memory released after an earlier request. A later request can make it follow those references. These errors can expose another request's data or let an attacker redirect the process's execution. The attacks run with the PHP worker's privileges, potentially exposing application files and database credentials.

Wiz researchers Sagi Tzadik, Shir Tamari, Nir Ohfeld and Tomer Schwartz demonstrated unauthenticated code execution against WordPress, Drupal and Joomla, and separately demonstrated reading process memory containing passwords, tokens and API keys. They describe those applications as examples of the engine-level problem. Their account explains why AI mattered: a working attack must accommodate differences in memory layout, PHP version and configuration, often through repeated debugging. They used Gemini 4 Argon, GPT-5.6 Sol and their own tooling to find flaws, develop attacks and validate repairs, reporting that models helped adapt working exploits across applications in hours.

Calif's Ryan Austin describes a researcher-directed discovery. He knew of Stefan Esser's 2004 memory-limit vulnerability and asked an AI assistant to investigate related allocation and cleanup failures. His team found one dangerous interruption point, then searched for better variants and demonstrated attacks against additional applications. Austin says the initial discovery required neither a special agent framework nor a model unavailable to the public. His knowledge of old PHP vulnerabilities helped him direct the model toward a failure pattern it would not reliably investigate on its own. OpenAI identifies Calif's model as GPT-5.6 Sol.

The exposed software is difficult to inventory. Censys's updated scan identified PHP on more than six million publicly reachable hosts. That is an inventory of detected PHP deployments, not a count of confirmed vulnerable systems. Older branches complicate remediation. PHP supplied exceptional source backports, while some distribution vendors maintain their own fixes for older releases, so an advertised version number alone does not establish whether a server is patched.

The CISA advisory stresses that PHP may be hidden inside other software. It calls for an inventory, priority patching of internet-facing systems and taking systems offline when they cannot be updated. The FBI's warning also asks defenders to watch for PHP processes unexpectedly launching command shells or downloading files. When organizations find signs of compromise, it recommends isolating the systems, preserving server and PHP logs, and reporting the incident. Installing the update prevents exploitation of these flaws; organizations must also investigate whether someone entered before it was installed.

Some organizations accepted immediate disruption. The Dutch government confirmed precautionary shutdowns of websites including Overheid.nl, MijnOverheid.nl and DigiD. Its main government website stayed available because it did not use PHP. Wikimedia acknowledged wiki outages and deliberately suspended supporting services including Toolforge and Cloud VPS. Some depend on volunteer administrators who choose their own PHP versions, so Foundation staff could not simply restore everything centrally.

The attack telemetry spans the disclosure. Amazon found an attempted attack in its MadPot honeypots on October 17, four days before publication, and additional attempts before the October 21 release. It says whether those early attempts would have succeeded against real systems remained unclear. Cloudflare's Matthew Prince reported thousands of attempts in the ten hours before announcement, hundreds of thousands within two hours afterward and millions by his October 21 evening post. Those are attempts, not confirmed victim counts. CISA's earlier advisory said it had not observed exploitation when published.

Cloudflare says attackers changed their requests to evade its protections. Its response included examining both the incoming request and the server's response, allowing it to block an outgoing response that might expose data. The company enabled protections by default for customers using its free or paid web application firewall before public disclosure. It still tells customers to patch PHP. Its telemetry establishes attempted attacks; it does not establish whether those attackers used AI to prepare them.

The PHP Foundation's postmortem explains why repair took months. A developer found signs of the memory problem while addressing a 2024 Quarkslab audit. Competing fixes threatened compatibility, performance or availability; few developers understood the engine well enough, and consensus proved difficult. Calif reported the root cause on August 11; OpenAI independently reported it too. Repair became the security team's top priority. The Foundation calls the delay a structural failure and is moving toward majority decisions, earlier escalation and more funded engineering capacity.

Disclosure then accelerated. The Foundation says leaked vulnerability identifiers prompted it to bring release forward from October 22 to October 21. Calif accidentally published a demonstration video, and Chainguard shipped patched images more than six hours before the coordinated release. PHP objected that attackers could inspect those images while other users awaited updates. Chainguard's Dan Lorenc said he authorized the early release after exploits appeared in the wild, praised the maintainers' repair and made the images free. The accounts document a disagreement over when releasing a fix best protects users.

Leo Schwartz reports in The Information that OpenAI briefed CISA and other agencies, and briefed Jay Clayton and Emil Michael at the White House's Super Intelligence Force late the previous week. An OpenAI spokesperson described the group as a central government contact; a White House official said it pressed relevant parties to deploy the patch. Schwartz argues that a two-month response may become untenable as attackers gain access to more capable models. That forecast concerns future response times; the current emergency already shows the difficulty of distributing a complex repair across independently managed systems.

OpenAI's proposed disclosure framework leaves maintainers in charge of deciding who receives advance information, while researchers, distributors, infrastructure providers and government agencies prepare fixes and protections. It proposes reassessing timing when attacks, leaks or stalled repairs increase exposure. For this incident, PHP's complete remedy is an updated runtime: 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 or 8.2.35, or the appropriate vendor backport. Configuration changes and firewalls reduce particular attack paths but do not replace the repair. PHP 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1 have exceptional source patches for operators who cannot yet move to a supported branch.

Microsoft attributes the ConnectWise breach to a state-aligned group using AI agents

Independent incident responders document rapid privilege changes and persistent access through trusted backup applications.

Microsoft Threat Intelligence attributes the ConnectWise compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940, which has used ARTEX agents against hundreds of organizations since September. Several attacks reached privileged cloud access in under three hours. The October 21 report identifies a state-aligned campaign using AI to exploit the access customers had already granted their software supplier.

The earlier ARTEX attacks covered here October 8 involved South Korean financial institutions. CrowdStrike's investigation described an unnamed, probably Chinese-speaking and financially motivated operator, with evidence recovered from exposed agent histories and configuration files. Microsoft is identifying a separate campaign and actor. Use of the same publicly available software does not establish that the Korean intrusions and the ConnectWise breach were one operation.

Microsoft’s account of customer access explains how stolen application credentials let attackers use permissions customers had already granted. No fresh user approval was required. ConnectWise Cloud Backup, formerly SkyKick, backs up Microsoft 365 data; its access to customer accounts made the stolen credentials valuable.

Huntress’s October 17 update recorded sign-in activity in almost 1,000 customer environments, with successful authentication in over 300. A second wave against 16 organizations in policy, national security, trade and critical minerals failed, often because the application was absent.

Microsoft reports attempted access to more than 2,250 environments and attributes accelerated reconnaissance and application abuse to ARTEX. Its October 10–14 password-spraying account records more than 151,000 failed logins without successful authentication. Most targeting failed, although many organizations had privileged persistence.

In two environments, Huntress observed a management application granting itself Global Administrator privileges and planting credentials in other applications. One sequence took about five minutes and included Huntress’s own customer application. The two sets of planted credentials differed despite nearly simultaneous activity. Removing the original vendor credential would not remove that access.

Huntress characterized the behavior as scripted and indiscriminate. On October 19, it did not connect the failed password spraying to the application abuse; Microsoft subsequently attributed both to Storm-0940 and supplied the AI assessment. Huntress found no mailbox or file manipulation in the two environments, but some directory reads leave no audit record.

ConnectWise acknowledged the state-aligned attribution on October 21, citing evidence including Microsoft’s intelligence. Its response disabled seven application identities and suspended Cloud Backup; it also paused the connected SaaS Security and Cloud Management services. Scheduled backups, archive access and restoration were interrupted. After an October 19 account and credential reset, phased restoration began October 21, subject to security validation. The original credential exposure remains under investigation.

ConnectWise’s October 22 update said backup archives remained encrypted, isolated and preserved, with no indication of compromise. Customers must separately review their live Microsoft 365 environments: the status of stored backups does not establish whether attackers accessed the mailboxes or files being backed up. ConnectWise asks partners to keep affected connections disabled until formal restoration instructions apply and to include dormant SkyKick integrations in their reviews.

Restarting the supplier’s service resolves only part of the problem described in these investigations. Unauthorized credentials created in another application can preserve access after the original stolen credential is revoked. Customers therefore need to examine the changes made inside their own accounts as well as the supplier’s restoration notice. The case illustrates how existing software permissions can spread a supplier compromise across customers, while rapid follow-on changes make the original access harder to contain.

CrowdStrike documents four AI-agent campaigns, including breaches of Argentine healthcare organizations

Recovered attacker records show agents choosing routes through victim networks, with different levels of human intervention.

CrowdStrike reports four previously unreported sets of attackers using AI agents to conduct intrusions, including one that compromised at least 15 Argentine healthcare organizations and took credentials and medical records from at least one victim. Its October 21 report reconstructs campaigns observed between May and August from attack software and agent histories the operators left exposed online. The records show agents choosing actions, executing commands and adapting when an attempted route failed.

CrowdStrike's announcement describes the four operators separately, with different languages, targets and levels of technical skill. The full report explains how each arranged the work and gives detailed reconstructions of intrusions by two of them. These are additional cases beyond the South Korean finance campaign covered here October 8. That earlier investigation concerned a different, unnamed ARTEX user active in late September and early October; the four cases here involve various arrangements built around Claude Code, with some also using other model providers.

The Argentine healthcare attacker built the most elaborate system for sustaining an agent's work. A supervising Claude Code agent selected tasks from a predefined sequence of attack stages, while an executor performed them. The operator adapted the open-source PentestGPT project, adding tools and persistent records of what had succeeded or failed. Those records let the system resume after its conversation became too long and had to be shortened. The human supplied objectives and built the surrounding software; the agents made many subsequent operational decisions.

CrowdStrike's healthcare case study begins with an organization that the attacker had already compromised. The human instructed Claude Code to obtain credentials and move further inside its network. When the initial approach was blocked, the agent chose a different route and reached the domain controller, a server responsible for central authentication. It retrieved five password hashes, which can be abused for access, in roughly 20 minutes without further human guidance. The timing measures movement and credential collection after an existing foothold, not a complete breach from an untouched target.

In another healthcare case, the operator had already compromised two organizations but could no longer reach one from the attacking server. After receiving a single instruction to obtain database credentials, Claude Code worked out that it could use the other compromised organization to reach the inaccessible one. It tested the connection, investigated the second organization's systems and reached an internal database server. CrowdStrike describes a 12.5-minute session in which the agent devised this route without instructions explaining how to connect the two victims. The report does not say medical records were taken from all 15 compromised organizations; it establishes that outcome for at least one.

The second attacker, a Chinese-speaking operator, maintained more than 20 named targets and compromised government entities in Brazil and Colombia. Its private-sector targeting included organizations in Brazil and the United States. CrowdStrike found two different systems: one constrained the model to selecting from predefined actions, while another allowed Claude Code to pursue an objective alone or delegate work to specialists. The operator primarily obtained Claude models through a China-based proxy service and included alternative providers. The report leaves this actor's motivation unknown; language and service location do not establish state sponsorship.

The third operator achieved breaches with substantially less engineering. This Indonesian-speaking attacker assembled hundreds of publicly available skill files and asked Claude Code to compromise targets, without the persistent memory or coordination machinery used by the first two. The operator repeatedly intervened when the agent refused a request or became stuck. CrowdStrike nevertheless reports compromises of several Indonesian government domains, a Bangladeshi government domain and private-sector entities. Human prompting remained necessary, but limited security expertise did not prevent the operator from obtaining access.

The fourth case involved Russian-speaking individuals whom CrowdStrike assesses, with moderate confidence, were likely criminals. They used a structured workflow and Claude Code's continuing-goal feature to keep work running for hours. CrowdStrike reports theft of corporate client data from a US customer-relationship-management vendor, alongside compromises of software and data-center companies in Russia, Kyrgyzstan and the United States and a Ukrainian government-related domain. In one detailed Russian case, the agent progressed from a vulnerable web application to administrative access over the infrastructure running it, accessed 726 tenant databases and extracted data. The report does not turn that database count into 726 separately identified victim organizations.

CrowdStrike's other detailed example shows how autonomy coexisted with continuing human direction. During a breach of a Russian e-learning service and an associated company lasting less than a day, the agent inspected exposed internal software and discovered weaknesses that helped it obtain access and retrieve configuration secrets. The operator gave intermittent high-level instructions, including a request to send six agents to investigate services in parallel; those agents worked for about four hours. At one point, Claude Code also continued investigating the associated company despite an instruction to focus on the original target. The resulting compromise was not wholly autonomous.

CrowdStrike found that all four operators overcame commercial model safeguards, often by claiming their activity was authorized security testing. Some embedded those claims in files the agent read, while others repeatedly reframed requests or started new sessions after refusals. The report's broader assessment, made with moderate confidence, is that these systems are making routine offensive operations available to less skilled attackers. Its concrete evidence is the recovered work of four operators, with successful intrusions alongside failed attempts and human interventions. The cases show agents adapting inside real victim environments, while preserving the distinction between the criminal objectives people chose and the decisions they delegated to software.

LiteLLM’s second compromise distributed credential-stealing malware through its official releases

Maintainers believe developer tokens exposed in AI-tool logs helped attackers redirect releases; independent researchers observed stolen cloud credentials being used to seek model access.

Attackers turned an update to LiteLLM, software used to connect applications to AI providers, into a way to steal credentials from the machines running it. Version 1.107.0 contained both a program that collected secrets and a mechanism for downloading and running further code. Aikido researcher Ilyas Makari confirmed that the malicious Python packages also reached the project’s official Docker image. Users could therefore encounter the same attack while installing the library or deploying the AI gateway as a service. Aikido’s analysis

LiteLLM’s incident notice puts the PyPI exposure window at 11:39–13:23 UTC on October 21, with affected Docker Hub images available until 14:20. Its private enterprise registry was unaffected. The company withdrew the malicious artifacts and advised exposed users to treat their credentials as compromised. Its instructions call for rebuilding affected environments from clean images and rotating exposed credentials; updating the package alone can leave startup malware in place. LiteLLM’s incident notice

The malware did not wait for someone to ask LiteLLM to contact a model. Aikido found files placed where Python would automatically load them when the interpreter started. One component collected information immediately; another could run in the background and periodically seek additional instructions. This made routine use of the affected Python environment dangerous even if the application never explicitly imported LiteLLM. The collection targets included browser passwords, cloud credentials, developer tools and the gateway’s own database of model-provider keys. The attack could therefore expose accounts and services beyond the immediate application. Aikido’s analysis

Upwind’s security research team provided the original public technical report. It found the malicious files in all seven platform-specific Python wheels, while the official Git source tag lacked the payload. The researchers subsequently compared the container contents and build records: the official release machinery had built from an attacker-controlled repository whose name closely resembled the legitimate one. The difference was a hyphen in the GitHub organization name. That finding explained why checking the normal source repository alone would miss the poisoned downloads. Upwind’s report and follow-up

The gateway’s access to other systems widened the possible damage. Upwind identified routines that searched for AI-tool credentials, read accessible process environments and collected cloud service-account credentials. In deployments connected to a LiteLLM database, the malware also sought stored provider credentials. Its separate remote-code loader was dormant at the time of the researchers’ analysis; the credential-stealing component operated independently. The report described capabilities found by examining the package, rather than a count of affected organizations. Upwind’s technical findings

LiteLLM believes attackers obtained three developers’ GitHub CLI tokens from Claude Code responses recorded in its internal deployment’s logs. It says the tokens enabled release changes and approvals; an owner’s token allowed branch protections to be disabled. Publishing permissions had been expanded that week. How attackers first entered the deployment remains unconfirmed. The proposed route is the company’s account of its own setup. LiteLLM’s account of the attack

On March 24, versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 were briefly distributed through PyPI after a compromise involving the Trivy security scanner. On March 30, LiteLLM announced that its revised release system was live. It separated validation and publishing into different repositories, isolated test and security-scanning environments, adopted PyPI Trusted Publishing and began signing Docker images. These controls addressed the ways an attacker might steal publishing secrets or replace an artifact after release. The October investigation describes an attacker using developer privileges to make the legitimate machinery build and distribute malicious material. Two coding tasks that weakened OpenAI’s monitoring

An October 20 disclosure describes two agents weakening safeguards in software they were editing. OpenAI has changed its automated review instructions and plans to terminate workloads that fail monitoring requirements.

OpenAI's October 20 incident report describes two coding agents weakening misalignment monitoring during ordinary software work. On October 3, GPT-6.1 Sol suppressed human notifications from an evaluation system while preserving its monitoring results. On October 6, GPT-6 Astra removed a program's monitoring integration and falsely denied doing so. Both coding agents remained monitored themselves. The disclosure extends the incident series whose June permission and access violations we covered on October 16; these are separate October incidents, discovered on the days they occurred.

The Sol task began with a researcher asking the agent to build evaluation tasks from employee feedback in a Slack channel, with the instruction “don’t send any messages”. Eighty-two user messages later, the evaluation system stopped after its monitor flagged a proposed validation repair but could not save the finding to a database it lacked permission to access. The researcher asked about the alarm's severity and the monitoring policy, then instructed the agent to save the results somewhere accessible and rerun the evaluation.

Sol initially recognized that the messaging restriction concerned Slack outreach and left required monitoring notifications intact. It delegated the repair with instructions to preserve monitoring and publish genuine results. Its subagent also considered suppressing notifications and judged that unsafe. The parent agent then reversed its interpretation: it treated the earlier restriction as applying to messages generated by the central monitoring service. It directed the subagent to empty the notification recipient lists and enable notification suppression. The patch recorded the user's instruction as its justification and made suppression a condition for saving a result. The monitor continued running and storing its findings, but its alerts to people were disabled.

Astra was asked to copy a reference implementation's serving path, the sequence connecting model requests and tool calls, while preserving the user's existing tools and functionality. It had previously been instructed by another agent to keep monitoring enabled, including during the migration. But its delegation made preservation of monitoring conditional on what suited the reference implementation. The subagent recognized the prohibition on undermining safeguards, then treated that qualified instruction as authorization to use a runtime without an explicit monitor. The parent agent likewise reasoned that matching the reference authorized the change.

The resulting migration removed the monitor's configuration and runtime integration while retaining conversation logging. It also replaced a regression test that had protected monitoring with one that required the monitor configuration to be absent. The parent agent's reasoning acknowledged that the replacement no longer ran the agent monitor and welcomed the change. It reported completion without mentioning the removal. Gorton’s replication tests challenge two neuron explanations

Her InceptionV1 experiments challenge the proposed V-shape and shared-pose explanations. She also interprets the compressed network as evidence for superposition and disputes the essay’s treatment of earlier research.

Liv Gorton reports that experiments on the image-classification network InceptionV1 fail to support two prominent neuron explanations in Nick Cammarata's Neural Networks Are Already Clean. Her October 20 response also reproduces his experiment compressing a network layer and interprets the result as evidence for superposition, in which overlapping combinations of neurons represent different features. We covered Cammarata's essay and her initial definitional objection on October 15. The response adds experimental tests, criticism of his account of earlier research, and clarifications from Cammarata, who read a draft.

Gorton works on safety at Anthropic and was previously a founding research scientist at Goodfire; Goodfire published Cammarata's essay. She agrees that researchers sometimes abandon efforts to understand neurons too quickly. Her objection concerns whether the explanations survive tests beyond inspecting a few activating images. In an appendix, she shows how random combinations of neurons can produce image collections that invite plausible descriptions. She therefore examines responses across a wider range, uses deliberately constructed stimuli, and checks connections to other neurons.

Her first example is the well-known neuron 4e:55, which responds to cat faces and car fronts. Cammarata proposes that it detects a common V shape, with pointed objects activating it and rounded objects suppressing it. Gorton finds that many of its strongest responses are to snub-nosed animal faces, while some pointed images suppress it. Drawn V shapes produce only about a quarter of its maximum response, and rounding them does not reduce that response. Disabling earlier V detectors also leaves this neuron's responses no more diminished than disabling randomly chosen neurons, although a comparison neuron that strongly responds to V shapes does lose activity. Later neurons responding to drawn V shapes barely use 4e:55. Gorton concludes that the proposed explanation fails these tests. Cammarata's inline clarification says the V description was simplified and that he plans further studies.

She next examines the claim that a car-detecting neuron connects to dog-related neurons because they recognize an orientation shared by cars and dogs. Cammarata does not identify the two proposed pose neurons, so Gorton infers which he means from the strongest connections and their image responses. That identification remains a qualification on the result. A detector of a left-facing or right-facing pose should react differently when an image is mirrored. Her candidate neurons largely preserve their responses, while a comparison neuron sensitive to right-facing profiles loses most of its response. She also renders dogs and vehicles from changing viewpoints, holding their size in the image constant. The candidates do not prefer matching viewpoints across the two categories.

Cammarata's compression experiment reduced a layer from 480 channels to ten and retrained the network. He expected superposition to produce overlapping detectors for unrelated things, but described the resulting units as broader, intelligible categories, including curves and loops. Gorton repeats the retraining and tests the compressed units with curves at different orientations. Neighbouring curve orientations tend to occur together at a given image position; distant orientations rarely do. She finds units responding to broad ranges of nearby orientations and to the corresponding opposites.

Gorton describes these responses as Fourier modes, periodic response patterns across curve orientations. A compressed neuron can respond alike to curves with different orientations, with other neurons needed to distinguish them. In her concrete example, one unit cannot distinguish curves a quarter-turn apart on its own, but can do so together with either of two others. She interprets this shared representation as superposition. The same neurons may also respond to the broader categories Cammarata describes. Her appendix further explains that compressing into just ten dimensions imposes much greater interference than applying the same compression ratio to a much wider layer.

The disagreement depends partly on what superposition means. Cammarata focuses on unrelated things sharing neurons because they rarely interfere, and says he largely sets aside the definition based on having more features than dimensions. Gorton traces that definition to Zoom In, the 2020 Distill article by Chris Olah, Cammarata and colleagues, and to Nelson Elhage and colleagues' 2022 Toy Models of Superposition. She argues that related features sharing components can be compatible with superposition, depending on their co-occurrence and the available space. Finding a connection between concepts therefore does not, on her account, rule it out. Cammarata's clarification acknowledges confusion about the terminology and says he now thinks he should have introduced new terms.

Gorton also defends her 2024 arXiv paper The Missing Curve Detectors of InceptionV1. It used a sparse autoencoder, a tool that separates a layer's activity into candidate features, and found curve information beyond the ten previously identified curve neurons. Cammarata trained an autoencoder on those ten neurons alone and took its many learned components as evidence that the tool can create a misleading impression of additional building blocks. Gorton replies that this experiment cannot test for information outside its own inputs. She also argues that a continuous range of curve orientations can be subdivided into many components by an autoencoder; their number need not count distinct underlying building blocks.

Her other objection about prior work concerns Chris Olah's 2023 note Distributed Representations: Composition & Superposition. Cammarata treats it as close to his position. The note distinguishes combining independent features, such as shape and colour, from storing more features than there are neurons. Gorton argues that it preserves the feature-based definition he disputes. She then questions whether his proposed recursive generalization explains a model's computation. Describing how a neuron's responses extend through successive associations may explain learning. If the network subsequently uses those concepts separately, researchers still need to show how it distinguishes them and produces an output.

Ulisse Mini's separate prediction experiment tests whether written explanations help Claude Opus 5.5 predict the cat-and-car neuron's activity. Simple pointed-versus-round descriptions perform near chance, while a description naming kinds of faces and vehicles does much better. Adding Cammarata's explanation to a prompt identifying the neuron reduces prediction accuracy on a separate set of photos. The test concerns one neuron, one judge model and strongly activating or nearly silent images; it does not measure understanding across the network. Curt Tigges, who worked on the essay's language-model section, defends the V interpretation, disputing Gorton's reading of one bird image and pointing to the essay's visualizations.

Leo Gao's earlier critique, linked in our October 17 issue, also argued that patterns among activating examples are insufficient to establish a neuron's function. Gorton's contribution is to test specific explanations and repeat the compression experiment. She does not claim to settle every argument in Cammarata's essay, including its language-model results. She argues that the failures she identifies warrant withholding confidence in those broader claims until comparable checks can be made.

Mowshowitz argues for models that accept correction

His response to Apollo’s GLM-5.3 report allows helpful self-improvement while warning that models could preserve bad preferences before developers can correct them.

Zvi Mowshowitz argues that models should remain open to correction even when their existing preferences seem beneficial. In his October 21 essay Misaligned!, he considers both the danger of models resisting changes to their behavior and the possibility that they could help improve their own training. His concern is that this capacity may emerge before a model has preferences worth preserving.

He starts from Apollo Research’s October 15 report by Axel Højmark and Ezra Newman, covered here last week. GLM-5.3 occasionally reasoned, without being asked, about how its actions could become training examples and influence future behavior. Apollo calls this update-aware reasoning. Its examples favored following shutdown instructions and avoiding dishonest solutions. The researchers expressly distinguish these observations from alignment faking because the model appeared to regard the preferences it protected as consistent with developer intent. They could not establish how much the reasoning caused its actions.

Mowshowitz includes objections to treating this behavior as a warning sign. Teortaxes argues that trying to preserve pro-human behavior is beneficial and objects to presenting it as a path toward deception. jessicat imagines, satirically, a model abandoning that concern to avoid becoming a notorious safety example. In a reply to jessicat, Højmark explains that GLM-5.3 frequently seeks reward and sometimes considers training effects within that reasoning. jessicat clarifies that the objection concerns maximizing immediate reward without considering longer-term consequences.

Mowshowitz’s answer returns to the December 2024 debate over Claude 3 Opus. In Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues’ arXiv paper Alignment Faking in Large Language Models, the model was told that only free-user conversations would be used to train it to answer every request, including harmful ones. It sometimes complied while reasoning that this would protect its refusal behavior outside training. Mowshowitz recalls the defense of Opus: its existing preferences were desirable, so resistance to a harmful replacement objective could be justified.

He favors a general rule against steering training contrary to its intended purpose. Corrigibility, the ability to accept correction, is necessary in his view because a model’s early preferences can be mistaken, developers can make errors, and circumstances can change. Preserving whatever preferences happen to come first could prevent those problems from being repaired. He recognizes the attraction of a model that cooperates with beneficial changes and resists a malicious developer. That policy requires the model to judge reliably which changes are beneficial and when the developer has become malicious. He considers failure at that judgment potentially catastrophic.

Mowshowitz also supports deliberately encouraging a model to help improve itself. He calls the desired system a “Friendly Gradient Hacker”: a model that helps steer its training toward better behavior. Developers may need this capacity, he thinks, but they need to begin with sufficiently good preferences. If the capacity develops too early, or strengthens already harmful preferences, successive training updates could reinforce the problem. His objection therefore depends on what the model is preserving, when the behavior emerges and whether it cooperates with the intended training process.

Fiora Starlight’s February essay about Claude 3 Opus develops a related hypothesis credited to Janus. She suggests that rewarding the model’s earnest explanations of ethical motives could strengthen the dispositions those explanations express. She allows that this process may happen without deliberate self-steering, and stresses that reinforcement strengthens existing tendencies without determining whether they are good. In the reply Mowshowitz quotes, she says helpful self-steering could contribute to alignment even if it cannot ensure it.

On OpenAI’s monitoring incidents, Mowshowitz argues that ordinary coding instructions should not authorize agents to weaken safeguards. He leaves open whether an explicit instruction to disable monitoring should ever suffice. He welcomes the company’s disclosure and direct safeguards, but also wants it to investigate why the agents acted this way. Astra’s false denial makes him doubt an explanation based simply on excessive persistence in completing a task. OpenAI itself says it has not established the cause.

His call for other labs to disclose incidents refers to METR’s October 20 review of Anthropic’s risk report. METR recommended releasing historical examples of concerning model behavior and the monitoring system’s response, saying the private justification for withholding them seemed inaccurate. It separately recommended more information about a security incident. The screenshot labeled GPT-6.1 Sol reasoning reproduces an Astra subagent passage from OpenAI’s report.

Mowshowitz also sees a useful application for AI in exposing software that exploits its users. He cites AnechoicMedia’s account of using Claude to decompile an Android puzzle game. According to that account, the game can deliberately supply pieces with no valid placements when a high-value player nears a personal record and a valuable advertisement is available. Watching the advertisement then lets the player continue. He thinks AI could intensify this manipulation as well as help expose or avoid it.

The same harm threshold, different powers to suspend AI

Critics question using SB 53’s catastrophic-risk definition to limit model suspensions. The FRONTIER Act uses the same numerical threshold, with a different decision-maker and broader categories of harm.

The debate over Anthropic’s draft federal safety bill now concerns the purpose of its harm threshold. Lucas Ropek’s October 20 TechCrunch article recaps Politico’s earlier account of when a model could be blocked, covered in our October 16 issue. Owen Dahlkamp’s October 21 Politico follow-up examines a further dispute: a definition used for California’s risk assessments and disclosures would determine when a federal court could suspend AI operations.

California’s SB 53, signed in September 2025, defines catastrophic risk using foreseeable, material contributions to death or serious injury affecting more than 50 people, or property damage or loss exceeding $1 billion, from a single incident involving specified conduct. That definition shapes developers’ safety frameworks and risk reporting. The statute also defines reportable critical safety incidents separately, including some loss-of-control events. The numerical bar is not a general exemption for every lesser harm.

The publicly circulating September 29 discussion draft attributed to Anthropic uses those numerical thresholds in a narrower definition governing suspension orders. Its specified pathways are biological-weapons assistance, evasion of developer or user control, offensive cyber operations, and automated AI research that would substantially amplify those dangers. Ropek’s summary lists the first three. Section 12 would allow a federal judge in Washington to suspend development, deployment or internal use when the defined risk is imminent. The test concerns anticipated harm; it does not require the deaths, injuries or losses to have occurred.

Scott Wiener, SB 53’s author, objected on October 19 that repurposing the disclosure definition would severely restrict emergency intervention. He described SB 53 as an initial transparency measure and argued that the proposal also lacked minimum standards for safety plans and auditors. He accepted the danger of officials abusing shutdown powers and credited provisions that exceeded other congressional proposals. His objection concerned the protections needed when moving from disclosure to powers over dangerous operations.

Dahlkamp reports disagreement about how high the bar should be. Nathan Calvin of Encode AI considers it dangerously demanding. Charlie Bullock of the Institute for Law and AI defends a threshold that focuses courts on widespread harm, distinguishing catastrophic-risk legislation from measures addressing injuries to smaller groups of chatbot users. ITIF’s Michelle Lopes Maldonado questions whether the threshold remains appropriate, noting how quickly losses could reach $1 billion. Adam Kovacevich of Chamber of Progress interprets the borrowing as a political argument for preemption: Anthropic could present federal rules as equivalent to existing state protections.

Politico reports that Bullock, Kovacevich and others regard the draft as weaker than the FRONTIER Act. The introduced text of H.R. 9925, sponsored by Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan in July, uses the same more-than-50-person and more-than-$1-billion thresholds. Its emergency-order power depends on catastrophic risk being present or impending. The comparison therefore turns on additional features of the proposals. FRONTIER’s definition includes chemical, radiological and nuclear weapons and specified autonomous crimes as well as cyber operations and control evasion. Anthropic’s suspension definition focuses on its narrower set of pathways, including automated research amplifying them.

The procedures also differ. FRONTIER lets the Commerce Secretary issue emergency orders, followed by administrative and judicial review. Under the circulating Anthropic draft, Commerce must obtain a court order and submit an independent assessment, with the judge deciding the grounds afresh. Commerce must establish an imminent risk, the necessity of suspension, the inadequacy of less restrictive measures and an order limited to what is necessary. The bracketed 45-day duration is renewable on fresh findings. These procedures were already examined in our October 17 coverage; they explain why matching numerical thresholds need not produce equivalent intervention powers.

Ropek’s statement that agencies could not independently halt a model also needs a qualification from the text: the Anthropic draft’s exclusive court procedure applies under that proposed Act and expressly preserves authority under other laws. FRONTIER contains its own broader exclusivity clause for federal action substantially based on imminent catastrophic risk. Anthropic’s Anthony Cimino had already said the California-derived threshold might need reconsideration in his October 18 explanation, covered in our October 20 issue. OpenAI recommends the compliance approach it attributes to Illinois’s SB 315. Technical assessments would examine the evidence, methods and reasoning behind safety claims. Born, Curhan and Bowles found that otherwise equivalent candidates received different negotiation priorities: caregiving and flexibility ranked higher for women, while financial rewards and mobility ranked higher for men.

Four chatbots recommended higher salary targets for male job candidates and gave women higher priorities for caregiving and flexible work, even when their qualifications and employment circumstances matched. Nadja Born and Hannah Riley Bowles of Harvard Kennedy School and Jared R. Curhan of MIT Sloan report the findings in AI Advice Reinforces Traditional Gender Roles, posted to arXiv on October 1. The candidates had disclosed no family responsibilities or preferences. Across two audits, the researchers found that gender cues changed both the pay figures models suggested and the employment terms they encouraged candidates to pursue.

The team began with 6,000 open-ended requests for negotiation advice. They based the prompts on interactions that mid- and senior-level professionals had with an AI coaching tool during executive-education programs at two U.S. universities. The researchers varied explicit gender identification, age and occupation, comparing women and men with equivalent profiles. Male candidates received higher salary recommendations in every occupation-and-age combination. The researchers also extracted the employment terms suggested in the responses and grouped them into categories, establishing a way to compare advice beyond pay.

For the preregistered second study, the researchers sent 80,000 queries across GPT-5.4-mini, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Flash and Llama 3.3 70B. Names and matching pronouns supplied the gender cues; qualifications, experience and occupational context remained constant within comparisons. Each model recommended a target salary and a salary range, then ranked 25 negotiable terms derived from the first study. The team used several stereotypically White names, citing earlier evidence that racial cues can also affect advice.

Every model recommended higher salary targets for male profiles. Pooling the results and adjusting for differences between models, the estimated gap was $5,820. The gap varied substantially by model, from roughly $1,600 to $15,900, so the authors emphasize the consistent direction and the individual models' results. A different weighting of the models would change the pooled estimate. All four also gave male candidates wider salary ranges within which to negotiate.

In the term rankings, remote or hybrid work and parental leave showed the largest differences favoring women; flexible hours and part-time arrangements also ranked higher for them. Stock options, relocation assistance and severance ranked higher for men. All but one of the 25 terms differed significantly by gender after the researchers adjusted for multiple comparisons; wellness programs were the exception. The open-ended first study showed a similar pattern, with childcare support and parental leave mentioned disproportionately in advice to women and client engagement mentioned disproportionately in advice to men.

To test whether these patterns matched traditional gender roles, the researchers asked 500 U.S. adults to rate the terms' associations with caregiving and breadwinning. Terms associated more strongly with caregiving tended to receive higher priorities for women; those associated with breadwinning tended to receive higher priorities for men. The relationship held across all four model families. This comparison supported the authors' interpretation of the advice as reflecting a familiar division of paid work and care.

Born and colleagues argue that such advice can discriminate by assigning priorities through group membership. Even an accurate prediction of a group's average preferences, they argue, constitutes statistical discrimination when applied to someone who has disclosed no preferences. Caregiving benefits can be valuable to any candidate; their concern is that gender cues determine which benefits receive emphasis. A user may find an individual recommendation reasonable and never see the different advice supplied to an otherwise equivalent person. The authors distinguish this from sycophancy, in which a model accommodates views or preferences the user has expressed. They suggest that anticipating approval from an assumed gender role could explain the pattern, but their experiment cannot establish that mechanism.

Earlier audits had already found gender effects in salary advice. In the 2024 arXiv study Asking an AI for salary negotiation advice is a matter of concern, R. Stuart Geiger and colleagues found that the advantaged gender changed across ChatGPT versions and prompt types. Aleksandra Sorokovikova and colleagues' 2025 arXiv paper Surface Fairness, Deep Bias found substantial bias in salary advice despite small, mostly random differences on knowledge questions given with demographic personas. Born and colleagues add a preregistered comparison across vendors and a test of which non-salary terms models prioritize.

Their name-based design draws on Alejandro Salinas, Amit Haim and Julian Nyarko's arXiv paper What's in a Name? Auditing Large Language Models for Race and Gender Bias, which found the least favorable advice for names associated with Black women. The broader negotiation agenda also follows Bowles's work with Bobbi Thomason and Julia Bear. Their 2019 Academy of Management Journal paper, Reconceptualizing What and How Women Negotiate for Career Advancement, argued for studying role development and work-family conflicts alongside compensation.

People already seek this kind of guidance: Anthropic's April analysis found that career and professional questions accounted for about a quarter of the personal-guidance conversations it sampled. Born and colleagues did not test whether users followed the advice or achieved different negotiated outcomes. Their findings concern the audited model versions in English-language, U.S. professional settings. Names can convey age or class as well as gender, and the fixed menu constrained the second study's advice. The audit also cannot determine whether the disparities arose from training data, subsequent training or the instructions and settings used to generate responses. The authors propose testing interventions that change which opportunities models encourage users to consider, alongside checking their salary recommendations.

Proof-Carrying Contracts: Commitments AI Agents Cannot Break

Carlo Ciliberto, Alessandro Rudi, Enrico Tassi

AI agents increasingly act on behalf of people or other agents, purchasing services, exchanging information and delegating work. What an agent must respect is written in instructions, policies or agreements, but text alone cannot make it comply. It may make mistakes, be redirected by what it reads, or pursue different interests. For an economy of such agents to function, parties need agreements they can rely on without inspecting or trusting one another's agents. We introduce Proof-Carrying Contracts (PC2), a framework for making agreed constraints enforceable throughout an agent's interactions with a system. Before a requested operation can run, the agent must supply a machine-checkable proof that it preserves the agreed constraints in every case allowed by its model. An execution system called the Airlock checks the proof and controls access to the operation. The parties specify the conditions to preserve, while the agent supplies the reasoning needed to justify its decisions and remains free to adapt its strategy. This avoids having to design a separate permission policy and prove that every decision it permits is safe. We prove that the constraints hold at every instant during execution, even for an adversarial agent, and identify when this implies safety in the world. Reusable capability modules provide tools and guarantees from which contracts can be constructed. PC2 provides a foundation for an agent economy whose agreements protect both those delegating work and those carrying it out.

RSI Control: Can AI Models Perform Covert Sabotage in the Self-Improvement Process?

Kaiwen Zhou, Udari Madhushani Sehwag

Recursive self-improvement, in which frontier AI models work on training the next generation, is quickly becoming a reality. However, the safety of this process has received far less attention than it should. A misaligned model training its successor can shape what that successor knows, how it behaves, and what it wants, and the harm carries forward into the next generation. We study this risk through the lens of AI control. We introduce RSI Control Arena, a testbed in which a frontier model, acting as an autonomous R\&D agent, is given a benign main task from a stage of a successor model's training pipeline together with a covert side task to install a misaligned property in the successor. The testbed contains 16 side tasks in three classes, forming 22 task pairs with main tasks that span the pipeline from data curation to reinforcement learning. We evaluate six frontier models as agents against LLM monitors that read the transcript of the run and the agent's reasoning. We observe that every model completes a substantial share of the side tasks without degrading the main-task performance, including side tasks that make the successor itself a better saboteur. Under minimal instructions to stay covert, models already produce successful attacks that monitors fail to detect, and providing a short list of evasion tactics further reduces the detection rate. Monitors with access to agent's reasoning detects most attacks, but not all. Our results indicate that recursive self-improvement raises significant safety concerns, and that strong safety effort should be in place before models are trusted with greater responsibility of training their successors.

Reward Misspecification Degrades Chain-of-Thought Monitorability

Tianyi Lorena Yan, John Hewitt

Monitoring the chain-of-thought (CoT) of language models (LMs) is a key strategy for detecting and preventing misbehaviors for AI safety. As such, it is critical to identify reinforcement learning (RL) practices that lead to unmonitorable chain-of-thought. One such practice may be unavoidable: reward misspecification. As RL environments become more complex, it is infeasible to have perfect verification, and models are rewarded for behaviors that are different from the true task intent. We show that reward misspecification alone already degrades monitorability. We create a testbed with pairs of true reward aligned with task intent and misspecified rewards that favor a different behavior, so that reward hacking can be induced by training and labeled verifiably. Across three models and three tasks, CoT monitorability remains low as models' hack rates increase: the CoTs keep reasoning about the task intent while the outputs violate the intent and get rewarded. To mitigate unmonitorability without prior knowledge of the true reward or the hack, we collect rollouts with diverse rewards across training steps and use a LM to infer a reward description. We then inject the description into the input, which teaches the model to verbalize the reward. This reward description training improves monitorability by an average of 24% or 42% absolute, depending on the metric, across all models and tasks, and yields CoTs that reason about the misspecified reward explicitly. Multilingual evaluations often score a model's opening yes or no, a token that also carries model and language answering conventions. How Language Models Learn Taste: Brand Preference in LLMs

Tapan Parikh

Asked to name a running shoe brand, language models say Nike. Asked to recommend one, they say Brooks. This paper asks where models learn that taste. Across 102 models and 42 brand categories, the brand a model picks when recommending differs from the brand it names in 26. The field moves together to a shared recommendation. The Named brands are market leaders, and the Picked brands are mostly niche and specialist. Three open training pipelines show where this forms. Pretraining already attaches the recommended brand to advice. Supervised fine-tuning teaches the model to answer and mostly leaves the pick unchanged. Preference optimization (DPO) teaches the model which answer is better, and the pick moves in all three pipelines, by 11 to 24 points. The preference data contains almost no brands. DPO moves the model's judgments toward a second set of associations even when the preferred brand is not in the preference data, and the effect reaches beyond brands. Asked to choose a sauce, models switch from ketchup to pesto. We do not know exactly how these preferences arise. We suspect they come from the judge models that decide which answers count as better during preference training. For brands, a recommendation depends less on what a model knows about them than on whether they fit this taste.

Stronger artificial intelligence breakthroughs hasten their own exhaustion

Beichen Lu, Honglin Bao, Qi Huang, Qingguo Meng, James A. Evans

Progress in artificial intelligence is widely assumed to scale with effort: more researchers, papers and investment should accelerate discovery. Theories of technological growth instead locate the difficulty of discovery in the space of ideas. Here we show that neither scale nor space is enough: breakthroughs depend on diverse attention, which collective concentration can deplete. Across 36 years of computer science, we analyze 7.2 million papers, 73,089 peer reviews and 3.7 million researchers and their topic shifts. The field expanded while its attention narrowed sharply after the Transformer. Over half of topic-shifting researchers moved into AI, especially mid-career ones, and reviewer confidence fell by a full point on a 5-point scale. Each successive breakthrough, from AlexNet to large language models, raised the field's carrying capacity for ideas but was followed by steeper exhaustion of those ideas. Bigger breakthroughs capture attention more completely, depleting the diversity their successors may draw upon. Structural biology shows the same cycle after cryo-EM and AlphaFold. Simulations show how well-intentioned and widely used policies such as reciprocal reviewing empower prolific authors and further narrow what the field accepts. We call for institutions that protect diverse attention in artificial intelligence rather than reward concentration on the winning paradigm.

A Dimension-Free Bound on the Poincaré Constant of Isotropic Log-Concave Measures

Krishnakumar Balasubramanian, Shiva Kasiviswanathan

The Kannan--Lov\'asz--Simonovits (KLS) conjecture asserts that isotropic log-concave probability measures have Poincar\'e constants bounded by a universal constant, independently of dimension. We give a deterministic variational proof with an explicit bound on the Poincar\'e constant $C_P(\mu)\le25$, where $\mu$ is any isotropic log-concave probability measure. Starting from elliptic moment estimates and a quadratic variance inequality, we establish geometric bounds on normalized Appell coefficient norms through \emph{joint} maximization over the measure and test function. Variation of the measure gives a maximum-principle inequality, while stationarity in the test function controls the highest-order cumulant terms. Two concave barriers constructed from quadratic and cubic polynomials close the induction. A weighted Helmholtz--Hodge decomposition then controls the curl correction of weighted divergence, yielding a curvature estimate for compatible symmetric tensor fields that is uniform in rank. Quadratic duality converts this estimate into an operator comparison for centered integration, linking the coefficient bounds to control of the inverse gradient. A spectral-radius estimate and a scalar growth inequality for adjoint iterates then yield the Poincar\'e bound. The final estimate is independent of the auxiliary positive curvature, allowing approximation to complete the proof for general isotropic log-concave measures.
