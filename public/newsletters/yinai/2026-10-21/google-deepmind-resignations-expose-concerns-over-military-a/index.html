Military AI dissent under Google’s speech restrictions

Sofia Liguori and Andreas Kirsch describe their objections to military AI, while Kirsch publishes a Google UK clause restricting criticism during and after employment.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 870 words · ~4 min

Several Google DeepMind employees have resigned or are leaving over military AI contracts, Christopher Giles reported for BBC Eye Investigations on October 21. Sofia Liguori, a London-based research engineer, resigned that week after three years at the company. She said Google's growing work with the US and Israeli militaries had undermined her confidence in its ethics. Employees also told the BBC that contractual restrictions on public criticism made them afraid to speak. Google said it reviews public-sector contracts against its policies and AI Principles, provides channels for concerns and prohibits retaliation.

Andreas Kirsch had already left. In his October 1 departure announcement, he placed his exit at the end of the previous week, in September. He said Google lacked the binding, independent oversight he considered necessary for safely developing superintelligence. We covered his objections to the Pentagon contract in July. The BBC now reports his doubts about Google's ability to observe or veto military deployments. Its account of the agreement permits classified use for “any lawful government purpose”, with human-oversight conditions for domestic mass surveillance and autonomous weapons, including target selection.

Liguori described her attempts to challenge that direction in Sam Biddle's October 21 Intercept interview. She joined DeepMind to work on electricity-grid efficiency and learned about Project Nimbus as the Gaza war escalated. Nimbus is Google and Amazon's $1.2 billion cloud and AI contract with Israel's government and military; Google previously said it was not directed at sensitive military or intelligence workloads. Liguori says she joined colleagues organizing through the union and No Tech for Apartheid, helped organize a February demonstration, and stopped work temporarily after April's classified Pentagon deal. She says internal posts containing the word “genocide” were deleted, managers and HR challenged her tone, and complaint tickets produced impersonal replies without substantive discussion.

Liguori opposes AI targeting even when people formally approve the results: she argues that automated classifications obscure how targets are defined and let participants evade responsibility. In his essay explaining the objections, Kirsch accepts that the US and its allies need military capabilities, while rejecting today's language models for targeting because of their unreliability. The BBC quotes Sarah Shoker, formerly head of OpenAI's Geopolitics team, explaining that hundreds of AI-selected targets and compressed decision times can make meaningful review impossible. Former Project Maven director Jack Shanahan similarly warns that rapid deployment can displace rigorous testing. The Pentagon argues that adopting AI quickly is essential to military success.

Kirsch's October 21 thread responding to the BBC adds documentary evidence about restrictions on criticism. He posted a screenshot of clause 4.2.5 from the Google UK Confidentiality and Proprietary Information Agreement he says he signed in 2024, before starting work. It prohibits actions harming the reputation of Google, its parent or group companies, and statements “criticising or disparaging” them to any person or organization, during and after employment. Kirsch argues that its breadth can deter truthful criticism. He also says it lacks protected-disclosure exemptions. The published excerpt does not show whether the agreement provides exceptions elsewhere. Google's reported response does not address that clause.

UK law already limits contractual restrictions on whistleblowing. Section 43J of the Employment Rights Act 1996 makes provisions void insofar as they prevent a worker from making a protected disclosure. Under section 43B, qualifying information must, in the worker's reasonable belief, be disclosed in the public interest and tend to show specified wrongdoing or dangers, including legal breaches and threats to health or safety. Protection depends on the information disclosed and the statutory conditions. Kirsch's claim is that the wording discourages staff from raising concerns, irrespective of protections they may ultimately be able to invoke.

Current and former frontier-lab employees made a related demand in the June 2024 Right to Warn letter, signed by people including Ramana Kumar and Neel Nanda. They asked companies to stop using agreements that prohibit criticism about risks, establish anonymous reporting to boards and independent bodies, and protect public disclosures when other processes fail. Their concern was that laws addressing existing wrongdoing leave employees exposed when they warn about risks that governments have not yet regulated. The letter also acknowledged the need to protect trade secrets and avoid unnecessary disclosure of confidential information.

Google's current AI Principles, invoked in its response, promise human oversight, testing and safeguards consistent with international law and human rights. They no longer list the prohibited applications in Sundar Pichai's 2018 statement, which excluded weapons designed to cause injury and surveillance violating international norms. Shannon Vallor, describing her own past involvement in developing the principles, criticized Google for invoking commitments she believes it has weakened. She added that the original principles had themselves been insufficient.

The CWU reported in May that 98% of DeepMind employees belonging to its UTAW branch voted to seek voluntary union recognition. Management rejected voluntary recognition on May 19 but agreed to further talks through ACAS. Workers' demands included the ability to refuse work on moral grounds and an independent ethics body. In the BBC investigation, two current employees speaking anonymously described colleagues leaving over the company's changed ethical direction; one anticipated younger recruits replacing them. The BBC reports several departures or planned departures without giving a total.
