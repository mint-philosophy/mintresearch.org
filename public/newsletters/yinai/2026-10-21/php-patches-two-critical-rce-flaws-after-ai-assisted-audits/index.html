Why PHP’s critical security repairs took months

The PHP Foundation explains how difficult engineering choices and a small team delayed repairs, then leaks disrupted their release. Leo Schwartz reports on the government response and argues that defenders may have less time for the next vulnerability.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 1072 words · ~5 min

PHP released security updates on October 21 for two flaws that can let attackers execute code on web servers without logging in. In its account of the release, the PHP Foundation acknowledges that developers saw signs of one problem in 2024 but struggled to agree on a repair. AI-assisted audits later demonstrated the danger, prompting full-time work on a fix. Leaks then forced the project to advance its release, and early publication of patched software divided participants over how to protect users.

The PHP advisory identifies the fixed versions as 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35. Both vulnerabilities have a severity rating of 9.2 out of 10. In CVE-2026-109618, reaching the memory limit can interrupt PHP before internal data is fully initialized; cleanup then treats unfinished data as valid. The repair lets PHP stop at a safe point, with immediate process termination at a higher memory threshold. In CVE-2026-109619, a worker process retains references to memory from an earlier request after that memory has been freed. A later request can make it use those references. The advisory identifies Apache's mod_php as the highest-risk configuration, with demonstrated attacks before application code runs. Other configurations have different exploitable paths.

The Foundation traces its investigation to a 2024 Quarkslab audit, organized by OSTIF with Sovereign Tech Agency funding. A developer fixing an audit finding noticed the memory-exhaustion problem. Developers could imagine an exploit but had not established a concrete path to code execution. Proposed repairs risked breaking existing applications or extensions, or introducing denial-of-service problems. The security team required consensus, and the few developers who understood the engine had other responsibilities. Work stalled and restarted for months. The Foundation calls the delay “a structural failure” and says the team is moving toward majority decisions and earlier escalation to its leadership when difficult choices remain unresolved.

Calif researcher Ryan Austin's technical account explains how older research guided the AI-assisted investigation. Stefan Esser had shown in 2004 that interrupting PHP at its memory limit could enable remote code execution. Austin asked an AI assistant to examine allocation and cleanup code for similar weaknesses. After identifying one unsafe sequence, the researchers generalized it into instructions for finding related failures. Calif says the initial discovery required neither a special agent framework nor models unavailable to the public; Austin credits his knowledge of PHP's vulnerability history with directing the search. OpenAI's account identifies the model Calif used as GPT-5.6 Sol.

According to the Foundation, Calif began auditing on July 30 through OpenAI's Patch the Planet program. Calif and OpenAI independently reported the same root cause on August 11; Wiz supplied further attack paths on August 24 and OpenAI on September 18. The Foundation made repairs its security staff's top priority from August 11. In Wiz's account, Sagi Tzadik and colleagues found their first vulnerability on August 19 and confirmed unauthenticated code execution against Drupal and WordPress over the next two days. They used Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-5.6 Sol alongside their own tools to investigate, develop exploits and validate repairs.

The Foundation's disclosure timeline begins around 15:00 UTC on October 20, when the vulnerability identifiers circulated with an incorrect release time and severity score. PHP published an advance notice at 19:30 UTC and moved the coordinated release from October 22 to October 21 at 14:00 UTC. Distributors still needed time to prepare packages that users could install. Around 20:00 UTC, Calif mistakenly published a demonstration video. Copies continued circulating after its deletion; the Foundation says the video helped others develop exploits.

At 07:41 UTC on October 21, Chainguard published software images containing the fixes, more than six hours before the coordinated release. The Foundation argues that this exposed the repairs to attackers while other users still awaited updates. In his original Hacker News response, Chainguard's Dan Lorenc says he authorized early publication after exploits appeared in the wild. He praises the maintainers, says Chainguard tested the complex patch extensively, and says the images are free and will remain so. The Foundation says it found no working exploits among the public examples it inspected before disclosure.

Amazon's telemetry records an earlier attack attempt against its MadPot honeypots on October 17, from anonymized infrastructure. Amazon also observed further attempts between 02:00 and 08:00 UTC on October 21, before the coordinated release. It says technical analysis was continuing and whether those attempts would have succeeded against real systems was unclear. Amazon's observations establish attempted exploitation before disclosure; they do not establish successful compromises of production sites or what information Chainguard had when it acted.

Leo Schwartz's October 21 column in The Information describes the government response. An OpenAI spokesperson told him the company briefed CISA and other agencies, and briefed Jay Clayton and Emil Michael late the previous week, in its first major engagement with the White House's Super Intelligence Force. The spokesperson said the group centralized communication across government. A White House official told Schwartz it helped press relevant parties to deploy the patch.

Schwartz argues that this response may be too slow for future vulnerabilities as attackers gain access to stronger models. He cites Anthropic's estimate that Z.ai's GLM-5.3 lagged US frontier models by about four months, and a former White House official's concern that July's Gold Eagle vulnerability clearinghouse might already be outdated. His forecast is that future defenders may have less than the two months available here to prepare repairs.

OpenAI's own disclosure names the Super Intelligence Force, the Office of the National Cyber Director and CISA among its government contacts. It credits PHP's maintainers with leading the fixes and release. Its proposed coordination framework leaves maintainers responsible for deciding which organizations receive advance information, weighing their protective role and the time they need against the danger of leaks. The company proposes reassessing disclosure timing when attacks, leaks or stalled repairs increase users' exposure.

The PHP advisory says updating is the only complete remedy. For unsupported PHP 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1, the team supplied minimal source patches for users building their own versions. The Foundation is also asking for sustained funding for engineers who understand the codebase. It created an Ecosystem Security Team in May and added staff for triage and hardening in July and September. Since July 1, the security team has published 15 vulnerability notices and handled more than 100 invalid, duplicate or unreproducible reports. AI tools help assess and group reports, but people still choose repairs, weigh compatibility costs and review patches.
