Rufus Knuppel on who should decide AI’s future

In Coxon Day, Knuppel examines the shared ideas and funding behind frontier labs and their critics, then argues that the public must help decide AI's future.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 1069 words · ~5 min

Rufus Knuppel argues that the public must take AI risk seriously while questioning the authority of the people building and opposing advanced AI. His October 19 essay Coxon Day, in The New Critic, develops that argument through a September visit to Constellation, the Berkeley AI-safety workspace, and his own growing dependence on Claude. Knuppel, a Dartmouth graduate and founding editor of the magazine, sees frontier laboratories and their safety critics as products of overlapping rationalist and effective-altruist communities. He doubts those communities are prepared for the wider political response they seek.

Knuppel was visiting Constellation on September 8 when Jacob Coxon announced his resignation from Anthropic. Coxon's thread accused both Anthropic and OpenAI of recklessly pursuing self-improving systems and urged researchers to demand different conditions for development. Anthropic researcher Evan Hubinger responded with his personal estimate of more than a 10% chance that AI would kill all humans within a decade, while saying Anthropic was trying its best. Eliezer Yudkowsky's September 9 post naming Coxon Day gave the essay its title. Knuppel treats the resignation as the moment existential-risk arguments reached a much larger public.

Knuppel describes Constellation as a community sustained through university clubs, retreats, shared houses and personal trust. He lists organizations including METR, Redwood Research and the AI Futures Project, supported by funders such as Coefficient Giving, formerly Open Philanthropy. His friend Noah introduces him to the space, where he finds technical discussions difficult to follow and access carefully controlled. Knuppel says he signed a media agreement limiting what he could report. He connects that secrecy to a political difficulty: researchers need outside allies, but public attention also exposes a private culture they value and expect outsiders to misunderstand.

Some of Knuppel's description comes from Berber Jin and Keach Hagey's September 26 Wall Street Journal feature. They reported roughly $20 million in Open Philanthropy grants to Constellation in 2024 and documented personal ties between Anthropic's founders and the EA community. The accounts differ on access: Knuppel says frontier-lab employees are barred, while the Journal describes offices hosting safety-minded OpenAI and xAI employees and separate space for Anthropic staff. The Journal also quotes Anthropic saying its employees hold diverse views.

Knuppel argues that shared beliefs help explain the conflict. Rationalists and effective altruists took both transformative benefits and catastrophic risks seriously; founders who thought existing laboratories too careless established companies they believed could develop AI safely. He sees the same reasoning encouraging research inside the labs and opposition outside them. On his account, each side believes its own participation is necessary to avert outcomes it fears. His criticism extends to who chooses a model's values: he worries about Anthropic imposing its preferred ethics, while recognizing dangers in OpenAI's emphasis on serving individual users' purposes.

Knuppel expects philanthropic commitments associated with Anthropic's wealth to give safety institutions far more resources, leaving the movement financially entangled with the development it seeks to constrain. He describes Anthropic as retaining EA commitments internally while adopting more conventional corporate language after FTX's collapse. For that account he draws on Gideon Lewis-Kraus's New Yorker profile, which described leadership distancing itself publicly from EA while the movement remained visible among employees. Knuppel's prediction that this wealth will extend EA's influence is part of his argument about power and accountability.

Knuppel's own change of mind occupies much of the essay. He recalls a winter 2026 Dartmouth course that introduced neural networks but, in his view, encouraged students to dismiss AI without considering its improving capabilities. He includes himself in that criticism: he had discussed AI's social effects while barely understanding the technology. After graduating, he asked Claude to build a website and says it finished within ten minutes. He increased his subscription, organized his day around usage resets and delayed sleep while waiting for its work.

Knuppel describes becoming invested in the very capability growth he feared. A better Claude would make him more effective, so he began anticipating releases and watching benchmarks. He also worried that dependence could reverse who was directing whom. Later, a conversation with an unnamed AI-welfare researcher made work on the transition to more capable systems seem urgently worthwhile to him. He presents these experiences as reasons for outsiders to learn about AI and participate. His instruction to readers includes the warning: “I do not trust San Francisco to save humanity.”

Knuppel describes frustration among safety advocates before Coxon's departure: earlier warnings, including the Hugging Face investigation, had attracted less public engagement than they expected. He welcomes subsequent efforts to coordinate development, then questions whether they will withstand competition. Dario Amodei's pacing proposal committed Anthropic to embedded outside evaluators; Sam Altman explicitly endorsed that commitment. Anthropic's September 18 Accenture announcement described a non-exclusive arrangement led by Faculty, funded directly by Anthropic, with discussions continuing with METR. Jack Clark's October 12 prediction that full recursive self-improvement was more likely in 2027 than 2028 contributes to Knuppel's worry about the pace of development.

Knuppel predicts that AI will disturb existing political loyalties. He describes potential overlap between left-wing objections to corporate power and conservative objections to cultural and human displacement. Gregory Conti's September 25 declaration that he would support whoever halted superintelligence is one example he uses. Knuppel expects public concern to grow, but the Echelon Insights polling he cites found AI was a first or second political issue for only 8% of voters, up from 5%.

Knuppel also imagines public fear of extinction provoking political violence, adding “and rightfully so” to his prediction of upheaval. When he recounts asking safety advocates about this possibility, he supplies an imagined concern about reputational damage as his interpretation of their response. He separately reports an unnamed researcher's view that Coxon's departure had changed little for the community. He sees advocates caught between seeking mass support and losing control of how the public understands and acts on their arguments.

Knuppel closes by connecting public participation to the technical problem of alignment: ensuring that increasingly capable systems pursue ends compatible with human interests. He argues that training machines to achieve goals does not by itself establish what they will seek as their capabilities grow, and worries that widespread reliance on agents will erode human control. His confidence in the urgency of this problem coexists with distrust of the institutions claiming to solve it. The essay discloses Manifund's financial support for the reporting and states that The New Critic retained editorial independence.
