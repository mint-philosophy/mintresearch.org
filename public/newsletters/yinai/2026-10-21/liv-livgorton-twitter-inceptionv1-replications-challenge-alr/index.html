Gorton’s replication tests challenge two neuron explanations

Her InceptionV1 experiments challenge the proposed V-shape and shared-pose explanations. She also interprets the compressed network as evidence for superposition and disputes the essay’s treatment of earlier research.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 1109 words · ~6 min

Liv Gorton reports that experiments on the image-classification network InceptionV1 fail to support two prominent neuron explanations in Nick Cammarata's Neural Networks Are Already Clean. Her October 20 response also reproduces his experiment compressing a network layer and interprets the result as evidence for superposition, in which overlapping combinations of neurons represent different features. We covered Cammarata's essay and her initial definitional objection on October 15. The response adds experimental tests, criticism of his account of earlier research, and clarifications from Cammarata, who read a draft.

Gorton works on safety at Anthropic and was previously a founding research scientist at Goodfire; Goodfire published Cammarata's essay. She agrees that researchers sometimes abandon efforts to understand neurons too quickly. Her objection concerns whether the explanations survive tests beyond inspecting a few activating images. In an appendix, she shows how random combinations of neurons can produce image collections that invite plausible descriptions. She therefore examines responses across a wider range, uses deliberately constructed stimuli, and checks connections to other neurons.

Her first example is the well-known neuron 4e:55, which responds to cat faces and car fronts. Cammarata proposes that it detects a common V shape, with pointed objects activating it and rounded objects suppressing it. Gorton finds that many of its strongest responses are to snub-nosed animal faces, while some pointed images suppress it. Drawn V shapes produce only about a quarter of its maximum response, and rounding them does not reduce that response. Disabling earlier V detectors also leaves this neuron's responses no more diminished than disabling randomly chosen neurons, although a comparison neuron that strongly responds to V shapes does lose activity. Later neurons responding to drawn V shapes barely use 4e:55. Gorton concludes that the proposed explanation fails these tests. Cammarata's inline clarification says the V description was simplified and that he plans further studies.

She next examines the claim that a car-detecting neuron connects to dog-related neurons because they recognize an orientation shared by cars and dogs. Cammarata does not identify the two proposed pose neurons, so Gorton infers which he means from the strongest connections and their image responses. That identification remains a qualification on the result. A detector of a left-facing or right-facing pose should react differently when an image is mirrored. Her candidate neurons largely preserve their responses, while a comparison neuron sensitive to right-facing profiles loses most of its response. She also renders dogs and vehicles from changing viewpoints, holding their size in the image constant. The candidates do not prefer matching viewpoints across the two categories.

Cammarata's compression experiment reduced a layer from 480 channels to ten and retrained the network. He expected superposition to produce overlapping detectors for unrelated things, but described the resulting units as broader, intelligible categories, including curves and loops. Gorton repeats the retraining and tests the compressed units with curves at different orientations. Neighbouring curve orientations tend to occur together at a given image position; distant orientations rarely do. She finds units responding to broad ranges of nearby orientations and to the corresponding opposites.

Gorton describes these responses as Fourier modes, periodic response patterns across curve orientations. A compressed neuron can respond alike to curves with different orientations, with other neurons needed to distinguish them. In her concrete example, one unit cannot distinguish curves a quarter-turn apart on its own, but can do so together with either of two others. She interprets this shared representation as superposition. The same neurons may also respond to the broader categories Cammarata describes. Her appendix further explains that compressing into just ten dimensions imposes much greater interference than applying the same compression ratio to a much wider layer.

The disagreement depends partly on what superposition means. Cammarata focuses on unrelated things sharing neurons because they rarely interfere, and says he largely sets aside the definition based on having more features than dimensions. Gorton traces that definition to Zoom In, the 2020 Distill article by Chris Olah, Cammarata and colleagues, and to Nelson Elhage and colleagues' 2022 Toy Models of Superposition. She argues that related features sharing components can be compatible with superposition, depending on their co-occurrence and the available space. Finding a connection between concepts therefore does not, on her account, rule it out. Cammarata's clarification acknowledges confusion about the terminology and says he now thinks he should have introduced new terms.

Gorton also defends her 2024 arXiv paper The Missing Curve Detectors of InceptionV1. It used a sparse autoencoder, a tool that separates a layer's activity into candidate features, and found curve information beyond the ten previously identified curve neurons. Cammarata trained an autoencoder on those ten neurons alone and took its many learned components as evidence that the tool can create a misleading impression of additional building blocks. Gorton replies that this experiment cannot test for information outside its own inputs. She also argues that a continuous range of curve orientations can be subdivided into many components by an autoencoder; their number need not count distinct underlying building blocks.

Her other objection about prior work concerns Chris Olah's 2023 note Distributed Representations: Composition & Superposition. Cammarata treats it as close to his position. The note distinguishes combining independent features, such as shape and colour, from storing more features than there are neurons. Gorton argues that it preserves the feature-based definition he disputes. She then questions whether his proposed recursive generalization explains a model's computation. Describing how a neuron's responses extend through successive associations may explain learning. If the network subsequently uses those concepts separately, researchers still need to show how it distinguishes them and produces an output.

Ulisse Mini's separate prediction experiment tests whether written explanations help Claude Opus 5.5 predict the cat-and-car neuron's activity. Simple pointed-versus-round descriptions perform near chance, while a description naming kinds of faces and vehicles does much better. Adding Cammarata's explanation to a prompt identifying the neuron reduces prediction accuracy on a separate set of photos. The test concerns one neuron, one judge model and strongly activating or nearly silent images; it does not measure understanding across the network. Curt Tigges, who worked on the essay's language-model section, defends the V interpretation, disputing Gorton's reading of one bird image and pointing to the essay's visualizations.

Leo Gao's earlier critique, linked in our October 17 issue, also argued that patterns among activating examples are insufficient to establish a neuron's function. Gorton's contribution is to test specific explanations and repeat the compression experiment. She does not claim to settle every argument in Cammarata's essay, including its language-model results. She argues that the failures she identifies warrant withholding confidence in those broader claims until comparable checks can be made.
