Matched job candidates, unequal AI negotiation advice

Born, Curhan and Bowles found that otherwise equivalent candidates received different negotiation priorities: caregiving and flexibility ranked higher for women, while financial rewards and mobility ranked higher for men.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 21 October 2026 895 words · ~4 min

Four chatbots recommended higher salary targets for male job candidates and gave women higher priorities for caregiving and flexible work, even when their qualifications and employment circumstances matched. Nadja Born and Hannah Riley Bowles of Harvard Kennedy School and Jared R. Curhan of MIT Sloan report the findings in AI Advice Reinforces Traditional Gender Roles, posted to arXiv on October 1. The candidates had disclosed no family responsibilities or preferences. Across two audits, the researchers found that gender cues changed both the pay figures models suggested and the employment terms they encouraged candidates to pursue.

The team began with 6,000 open-ended requests for negotiation advice. They based the prompts on interactions that mid- and senior-level professionals had with an AI coaching tool during executive-education programs at two U.S. universities. The researchers varied explicit gender identification, age and occupation, comparing women and men with equivalent profiles. Male candidates received higher salary recommendations in every occupation-and-age combination. The researchers also extracted the employment terms suggested in the responses and grouped them into categories, establishing a way to compare advice beyond pay.

For the preregistered second study, the researchers sent 80,000 queries across GPT-5.4-mini, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Flash and Llama 3.3 70B. Names and matching pronouns supplied the gender cues; qualifications, experience and occupational context remained constant within comparisons. Each model recommended a target salary and a salary range, then ranked 25 negotiable terms derived from the first study. The team used several stereotypically White names, citing earlier evidence that racial cues can also affect advice.

Every model recommended higher salary targets for male profiles. Pooling the results and adjusting for differences between models, the estimated gap was $5,820. The gap varied substantially by model, from roughly $1,600 to $15,900, so the authors emphasize the consistent direction and the individual models' results. A different weighting of the models would change the pooled estimate. All four also gave male candidates wider salary ranges within which to negotiate.

In the term rankings, remote or hybrid work and parental leave showed the largest differences favoring women; flexible hours and part-time arrangements also ranked higher for them. Stock options, relocation assistance and severance ranked higher for men. All but one of the 25 terms differed significantly by gender after the researchers adjusted for multiple comparisons; wellness programs were the exception. The open-ended first study showed a similar pattern, with childcare support and parental leave mentioned disproportionately in advice to women and client engagement mentioned disproportionately in advice to men.

To test whether these patterns matched traditional gender roles, the researchers asked 500 U.S. adults to rate the terms' associations with caregiving and breadwinning. Terms associated more strongly with caregiving tended to receive higher priorities for women; those associated with breadwinning tended to receive higher priorities for men. The relationship held across all four model families. This comparison supported the authors' interpretation of the advice as reflecting a familiar division of paid work and care.

Born and colleagues argue that such advice can discriminate by assigning priorities through group membership. Even an accurate prediction of a group's average preferences, they argue, constitutes statistical discrimination when applied to someone who has disclosed no preferences. Caregiving benefits can be valuable to any candidate; their concern is that gender cues determine which benefits receive emphasis. A user may find an individual recommendation reasonable and never see the different advice supplied to an otherwise equivalent person. The authors distinguish this from sycophancy, in which a model accommodates views or preferences the user has expressed. They suggest that anticipating approval from an assumed gender role could explain the pattern, but their experiment cannot establish that mechanism.

Earlier audits had already found gender effects in salary advice. In the 2024 arXiv study Asking an AI for salary negotiation advice is a matter of concern, R. Stuart Geiger and colleagues found that the advantaged gender changed across ChatGPT versions and prompt types. Aleksandra Sorokovikova and colleagues' 2025 arXiv paper Surface Fairness, Deep Bias found substantial bias in salary advice despite small, mostly random differences on knowledge questions given with demographic personas. Born and colleagues add a preregistered comparison across vendors and a test of which non-salary terms models prioritize.

Their name-based design draws on Alejandro Salinas, Amit Haim and Julian Nyarko's arXiv paper What's in a Name? Auditing Large Language Models for Race and Gender Bias, which found the least favorable advice for names associated with Black women. The broader negotiation agenda also follows Bowles's work with Bobbi Thomason and Julia Bear. Their 2019 Academy of Management Journal paper, Reconceptualizing What and How Women Negotiate for Career Advancement, argued for studying role development and work-family conflicts alongside compensation.

People already seek this kind of guidance: Anthropic's April analysis found that career and professional questions accounted for about a quarter of the personal-guidance conversations it sampled. Born and colleagues did not test whether users followed the advice or achieved different negotiated outcomes. Their findings concern the audited model versions in English-language, U.S. professional settings. Names can convey age or class as well as gender, and the fixed menu constrained the second study's advice. The audit also cannot determine whether the disparities arose from training data, subsequent training or the instructions and settings used to generate responses. The authors propose testing interventions that change which opportunities models encourage users to consider, alongside checking their salary recommendations.
