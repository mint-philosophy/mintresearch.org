Patterson bets budget-poor academia against a $700 billion spending panic

The RISC pioneer argues fear drives hyperscaler capex, and constraint is where new architectures come from.

By Adaeze Okafor · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

The Laude Institute, the AI research nonprofit whose founding board chair is Berkeley computer architect David Patterson, posted a video clip this week in which Patterson argues that Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta will spend more than $700 billion on capital expenditure this year — as the Institute's post puts it, "not because this spending makes economic sense, but out of competitive fear." The post carries only the clip; it names no venue and links no full talk.

The dollar figure tracks market reporting. CNBC's February accounting put the four companies' combined 2026 AI spending at nearly $700 billion, and reported that the build-out is taking a large bite out of their cash.

Patterson draws a parallel to his own history with microprocessors: when you cannot match a competitor's budget, you need fundamentally different ideas, and that is historically where breakthroughs have come from. On the Laude Moonshots page he attaches a number to the payoff from that kind of constraint, documenting "over a trillion dollars in economic value traceable to $100 million in public investment" across the Berkeley labs that produced RISC, RAID, and RISC-V. The same essay prescribes small teams, hard deadlines, sunset clauses, and working artifacts over papers that merely argue, and warns that the open-science ecosystem is "being hollowed out from both ends" as federal funding tightens and private labs pull top researchers into closed, proprietary work.

The argument pairs with a skeptic standing on the other side of the same trade. George Hotz, writing on his blog, contends that frontier laboratories are overvalued because AI's economic value will be widely commodified rather than captured by a few firms, with much of the progress traceable to Moore's law and general computing advances. Patterson sees academia as the cheap source of the next architecture; Hotz doubts the capital will earn outsized returns for anyone. From opposite ends, both arrive at the same suspicion about the spending race.
