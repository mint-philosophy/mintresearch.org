Cowen tells DeepMind the leisure dividend is running late

The economist's contrarian read: capable AI intensifies work before it frees anyone, and the payoff has no fixed date.

By Adaeze Okafor · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

Tyler Cowen posted a transcript of his talk at DeepMind on July 13, and his argument turns on timing. The near-term effect of capable AI, he told the lab, is more work and a kind of exhaustion. He sides with the moderate camp against mass-unemployment forecasts, accepts that “total leisure time will go up,” and then asks the question he leaves open: “across what time horizon?”

His case is a labor-supply story. Rapidly improving tools raise the payoff to mastering them now, so people work harder to bank future earnings or to avoid falling behind. Cowen, 64, says his own “relative wage gradient” for working harder today is “really quite extreme,” and that an 18-year-old would feel forced to work hard, since starting at 23 leaves you “behind by X number of years.” He calls it a substitution effect from a higher implicit wage on future earnings.

The exhaustion comes from cadence. New releases arrive faster than anyone can absorb them, and Cowen admits he now eyes his calendar and asks the labs, “could you all wait a week, please?” DeepMind staff, with access to systems the public has never seen, must be “drenched in meaning,” he tells them; the coming problem is an excess of it. Commenter Kevin H quotes a blunter admission from the fuller transcript, a line the post’s own excerpt omits: since GPT-4 arrived, Cowen has multiplied his in-person appearances by roughly 2.5, and “I write less, I’m writing fewer books, and I’m doing way more appearances.”

The published transcript shows no pushback from the room; Cowen notes that “anything from the audience (Q&A with comments) has been cut out.” The visible dissent sits in his comment section, where commenter Dismalist separates work intensity from work time, citing a fall from 60-hour weeks in 1900 to 34.5 today, a figure the thread offers without a source. The same labor tension is already concrete in South Korea, where unions are demanding “not a single robot” on factory floors without worker consent, per Tech Policy Press reporting by Lam Le flagged by Justin Hendrix.
