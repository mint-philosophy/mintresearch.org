Whose Half of AI? A Jacobin Essay Takes Sanders' Wealth Fund Global

Cecilia Rikap accepts a public stake in the AI giants as a first step, then argues it should span the planet and reach the clouds.

By Iris Calloway · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

Cecilia Rikap's essay in Jacobin starts from a claim about origins: generative models are trained on the world's collective output (social media data, artwork, software code, health records), so AI is a product of humanity and humanity should own it. From there she endorses Bernie Sanders' plan to take a public stake in the largest AI firms as reasonable compensation, then argues it stops short on two counts.

Sanders would levy a one-time 50 percent tax, paid in stock rather than cash, on AI companies with more than $200 million in annual sales, seeding a sovereign wealth fund he says would pay initial dividends of about $1,000 per person and give the government voting shares and equal representation on each company's board. Rikap's first objection is geographic: the training data and the globally distributed, often publicly funded researchers behind these models come from around the world, so the fund should belong to the world's population rather than to US taxpayers. Her second is structural. Every model runs as a service on the clouds of Amazon, Microsoft, and Google, whose data centers consume land, energy, and water, so any fund should reach past the AI labs to Nvidia and the cloud giants.

Ownership does not deliver control, she argues, and SpaceX supplies her most concrete evidence. At its debut, laypeople were allocated 20 to 25 percent of stock while Elon Musk's 42 percent stake, held in shares carrying ten times the voting power, works out to roughly 80 to 85 percent of the votes. A successful Sanders tax would still leave founders able to sell advanced models to the military or build data centers offshore. A stand-alone fund could backfire, Rikap warns, the way sovereign oil funds can blunt enthusiasm for a renewable transition.

The essay treats a public cut as a floor rather than a finish line. Sanders frames his bill as giving the public "a direct role in determining the future of this technology"; Rikap closes with a harder demand: "Control, thus decision-making power, and knowledge should be equally shared."
