The case for auditing the whole AI company, not just its models

A 48-author proposal defines four assurance levels and the deep, non-public access each demands. Then it reaches the question every auditing regime eventually faces: who pays the auditor.

By Iris Calloway · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1084 words

Nearly fifty researchers have put their names to the most institutionally detailed blueprint yet for scrutinizing the companies building the most capable AI systems, and its central move is to widen the lens: audits should cover a developer's safety and security practices “as a whole, not just individual models and systems.” First-authored by Miles Brundage and Noemi Dreksler, with Aidan Homewood, Sean McGregor and Patricia Paskov, and with co-authors including Yoshua Bengio and Rishi Bommasani (the paper cautions that authorship does not entail endorsement of every claim), it defines frontier AI auditing as rigorous independent evaluation of a company's systems and practices against relevant standards, grounded in secure access to non-public information. Paskov flagged the work while discussing frontier AI auditing at an ICML technical-governance workshop.

The whole-company framing follows from a failure mode the authors call abstraction error: judging an organization's risk by testing one component in isolation. A model that passes its evaluations can still ship inside a system whose safety classifiers, routing rules, or deployment settings materially alter misuse potential in ways a single test never sees. So the proposed scope covers four risk categories, intentional misuse, unintended system behavior, information security, and emergent social phenomena such as AI addiction or facilitation of self-harm, with auditors both verifying company claims and testing practices against stated policies. The stated aim is to end reliance on companies “grading their own homework,” an approach the authors say has a checkered record across industries.

A menu of assurance levels

The framework's spine is a graduated set of four AI Assurance Levels, sorted by how much confidence an audit can support. AAL-1, limited assurance, is a time-bounded audit of typically a few weeks, built on black-box API access to multiple model checkpoints, chain-of-thought outputs and logits, and the ability to enable or disable safety classifiers; the authors recommend it as a baseline for frontier AI generally. AAL-2, moderate assurance, is their near-term goal for the companies closest to the state of the art: months of work at minimum, gray-box access, unredacted safety cases, staff interviews across safety, security, policy and product, and statistical model signatures to confirm the audited models correspond to the deployed ones. The two top tiers stay aspirational. AAL-3 involves multiyear white-box engagement with the authority to examine any area of concern, and AAL-4 imagines continuous verification able to detect active deception attempts and provide “treaty-grade” confirmation of a company's risk profile. The authors concede that AAL-3 and AAL-4 are not yet technically or organizationally feasible, and they outline research directions to change that.

Who pays the auditor

This paper lands directly on the objection raised in this newsletter's July 11 coverage: that audits paid for by the audited reproduce the incentives that let credit-rating agencies inflate their grades before 2008. The authors do not dodge it. They warn that when auditors compete for contracts awarded by the companies they evaluate, assessment risks devolving into “rubber stamping,” the pattern they say financial auditing fell into before the 2008 crisis, and they point to Enron and Wirecard as showing where heavy reliance on a small number of large clients can end. Payment, they write, should not depend on audit results, but that alone is not sufficient, since an auditor may still sense that future work or continued access rides on favorable findings.

On the fix, the framework offers a research agenda rather than an answer. It floats payment models that break direct financial dependence on auditees, funding by insurers, regulator-administered pools, industry-wide levies, or payments from downstream enterprise users in high-stakes sectors, and it borrows financial auditing's test for material revenue dependence while flagging a problem: the customary benchmark of 10 or 15 percent of a firm's annual fees from a single client may be hard to stay under when frontier AI is by definition a limited subset of the industry, perhaps requiring a higher threshold. The authors decline to endorse any specific payment model and instead call for progress toward alternatives by the end of 2026. For oversight, they sketch a “PCAOB-for-AI”, modeled on the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board created after the Enron-era accounting scandals, with government-approved standards and the authority to revoke auditors' accreditation. Measured against the credit-rating charge, the framework concedes the diagnosis in full and treats the structural cure as unsolved.

The proposal maps onto existing machinery unevenly. In Europe, the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice already requires signatories to undergo independent external evaluations, and signing grants a presumption of conformity with the AI Act. The paper observes that notable non-signatories include Meta and Chinese AI companies, and that the evaluation requirement depends on signatories being able to find qualified assessors, a live constraint given how thin the current ecosystem is. In the United States, where participation in third-party assessment remains voluntary and concentrated among a few developers, the authors ask policymakers to create targeted safe harbors for good-faith auditing, resolve insurers' outstanding requests on AI-risk exclusions, and embed auditing requirements in procurement for high-stakes domains such as health and defense; the evaluation ecosystem itself, they say, should stand up a Frontier AI Auditor Accreditation Program.

Some of this exists in embryo. The paper counts METR's October 2025 review of Anthropic's pilot sabotage risk report among the first AAL-1 audits, an exercise METR has since repeated for the report covering Claude Opus 4.6, alongside third-party review of the safety work for OpenAI's gpt-oss release and the reciprocal safety assessments OpenAI and Anthropic ran on each other's systems. The authors are candid that these developments remain early-stage compared to established assurance regimes in the fields the paper mines for precedent, finance, aviation, and food safety among them.

Three tests will show whether the vision moves from paper to practice. The first is the authors' own end-of-2026 target for credible progress on payment models that do not run through the audited company, the hinge on which the credit-rating critique turns. The second is whether any frontier developer grants AAL-2 access, gray-box internals, unredacted safety cases, and cross-functional staff interviews, without a regulatory mandate forcing the point. The third is ecosystem scale: the paper itself notes the EU's independent-evaluation requirement depends on the existence of qualified assessors, and a PCAOB-for-AI, an accreditation program, or a non-client funding stream all presuppose an auditing profession that does not yet exist at the size the framework assumes.
