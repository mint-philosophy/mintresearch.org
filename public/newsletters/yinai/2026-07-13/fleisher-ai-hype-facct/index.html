A philosopher hands AI researchers the bill for hype

Will Fleisher's new FAccT paper argues that ordinary research can amplify hype whether or not the excitement is warranted.

By Iris Calloway · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

Will Fleisher, an assistant professor of philosophy at Georgetown University and a research professor at its Center for Digital Ethics, has a paper in the 2026 ACM FAccT proceedings arguing that AI researchers share responsibility for the hype their work feeds. He titled it "Inductive Risk of AI Hype," and announced it with a caveat aimed at skeptics of the framing: you can believe some AI excitement is warranted and still find the problem worth worrying about.

The argument runs through a concept from philosophy of science. Inductive risk names the fact that error is always possible even in excellent science, so the stakes of being wrong should bear on how a researcher weighs evidence and states conclusions. Accepting a false hypothesis and rejecting a true one carry different costs, and on this view those costs belong inside the researcher's methodological choices. Fleisher moves the analysis from the truth of a claim to its reception.

His mechanism turns on amplification. A researcher can reasonably argue for a hypothesis about AI that later proves false — ordinary exploratory work, since research routinely defends claims that have yet to become scientific consensus. In hype conditions, per the paper's abstract, that same argument reaches an audience far wider than intended, where a tentative claim gets read as confirmation and repeated as fact. Defending a hypothesis therefore carries, alongside its usual risk of scientific error, a second risk of contributing to hype, with consequences the abstract locates in the distribution of resources and in environmental damage. Fleisher's announcement holds that second risk apart from the question of whether the excitement is justified, so the concern stays live even for researchers who think AI genuinely matters.

The prescription is procedural: researchers should weigh hype risk when deciding how to conduct an inquiry and how to phrase its results, treating communication choices as part of their epistemic responsibility. The single-authored paper entered the proceedings in late June under an open-access license. A newsletter that summarizes AI research for a broad readership sits inside the amplification channel Fleisher describes, which gives the paper an uncomfortable second reading as a note to its own coverage.
