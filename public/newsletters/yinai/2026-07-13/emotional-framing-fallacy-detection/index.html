Wrap a bad argument in fear, and fewer readers catch the flaw

A five-author study finds AI-added emotion cuts human fallacy detection by about 15 percent, with fear the strongest blinder.

By Priya Raghunathan · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

Chen and four co-authors report that emotional framing generated by language models makes people worse at spotting logical fallacies. In “Emotionally Charged, Logically Blurred,” accepted to EACL 2026, they measure an average relative drop of about 14.5 percent in F1 when readers try to name the fallacy type in an argument that an AI has recoated with emotion, plus a 5.5 percent relative fall in simply flagging that a fallacy is present.

The method rewards a close read. Chen and colleagues benchmarked eight language models on injecting fear, sadness, or enjoyment into fallacious arguments while preserving the reasoning, then kept the four strongest generators (qwen3-32b, ds-llama-70b, gpt-4o-mini, and llama-3.3-70b). The human study ran on Prolific with native English speakers in the US or UK who held at least a community-college degree, three annotators per batch, judging 1,000 items: 200 original arguments plus 800 emotion-framed variants across five fallacy types, among them faulty generalization, ad hominem, and false causality.

Fear does the most damage

The per-emotion breakdown carries a wrinkle the paper states plainly: it splits results by the emotion readers perceived rather than the one injected. When annotators perceived fear, their F1 for naming the fallacy sat at 0.283, against 0.528 for enjoyment, with sadness at 0.337 and neutral at 0.437. Perceived fear cut the score to roughly half the enjoyment figure, a gap the authors report as statistically significant. The abstract adds that all three injected emotions correlated with significantly higher convincingness than neutral framing.

The authors caution that their stimuli come from a simple dataset drawn from online educational resources that may differ from real arguments in length and topic, and that emotion annotations drew low crowdworker agreement, a Pearson correlation near 0.2 on emotional appeal. They warn of misuse for persuasive misinformation and propaganda without offering policy fixes. Whether a deployed chatbot framing its own replies produces the same effect is untested; the study measured curated arguments rather than live conversation.
