The Windfall Nobody in Washington Is Ready For

Anton Leicht argues that IPO riches at Anthropic and OpenAI will pour safety-minded money into AI politics faster than the field can absorb it. His prescription is a deliberately fragmented roster of rival political vehicles, built starting now.

By Iris Calloway · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1119 words

Anton Leicht opens his July 13 essay "The Flood" with a forecast about money. Within a few months, he writes, employees at frontier AI developers will become "very, very rich" as their companies go public, and many of them, convinced Washington has governed AI badly, will spend the windfall trying to change that. The sums behind the forecast are on the record. Anthropic closed a $65 billion Series H on May 28 at a $965 billion post-money valuation, a round TechCrunch reported could be its last private fundraising before a public debut. OpenAI completed its restructuring into a Delaware public-benefit corporation in October 2025 and carried an $852 billion valuation as of March, with an offering considered possible late this year and a raise of $60 billion or more under discussion, per Forbes's tracking. Leicht's argument begins at the moment that paper wealth turns to cash.

His warning is that the money will be wasted, or will discredit the cause it means to help, unless the political machinery to receive it gets built first. His metaphor is the annual Nile flood: absorb it through irrigation channels and the fields are watered; let it run through too few channels and the crops drown. Washington's safety-advocacy field, he argues, amounts to a handful of organizations working from one narrow strand of tactical thinking. Pour a fortune through so few channels and you overextend a single strategy, invite counterspending, and hand opponents one target to caricature.

The bill that split the coalition

His central illustration is the Great American AI Act, the 269-page discussion draft Representatives Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan released on June 4. As Roll Call reported, the draft would require frontier developers with more than $500 million in annual revenue to publish frameworks disclosing catastrophic-risk assessments, report critical safety incidents within 15 days (24 hours when the risk is imminent), protect whistleblowers, and undergo independent audits twice a year, while freezing for three years any state law that specifically regulates AI development. The Future of Privacy Forum's comparison of the draft with state statutes notes that state authority over deployment and use survives, and that violations can draw penalties of up to $1 million, with each day counted separately. Leicht reads the package as industry's long-sought preemption purchased with real federal safety obligations. He liked the trade, and thinks the safety side gave it too little serious attention.

Prominent groups treated preemption as disqualifying. Americans for Responsible Innovation launched a Massachusetts ad campaign urging Trahan to oppose preemption, with spots titled "Amazing Grace" and "Protect Our Rights" built around children's safety, civil rights, and privacy. ARI president Brad Carson said Big Tech and the Trump administration "would love to see a Democrat push forward their plan to freeze state AI laws." Leicht describes one such ad closing on images of a candlelight vigil for dead children. He reads two tactical bets behind the rejection: a belief that time favors the safety side, so imperfect deals should be killed to preserve a better one later, and a commitment to the broad anti-moratorium coalition of labor, child-safety, and populist groups assembled last summer, a coalition adept at blocking but hard to reorient toward compromise. Neither bet is unreasonable, he writes. The cost falls on any policymaker who cares about safety but disagrees with the orthodoxy on economics or general tech regulation, and who now expects "a stern talking to and an ad to go with it" for staking out new ground.

The proxy war that produced nothing

The market dynamics worry him more than the substance. He points to the Democratic primary in New York's 12th district, where Fortune tallied $27 million in spending: roughly $19 million from the Anthropic-backed Public First Action supporting Assemblyman Alex Bores, and about $8 million from the OpenAI-linked Leading the Future opposing him. Micah Lasher, the presumptive favorite, who separately drew a $10 million contribution from Michael Bloomberg, won the race; Fortune's headline observed that the winner "told them both to get lost." Leicht's own section title is blunter: "I Spent $19 Million On NY-12 And All I Got Was Micah Lasher."

He concludes that concentrated AI money turns every race it touches into a referendum on AI, frightening congressional leaders and candidates away from the subject entirely, and that a small, coherent coalition makes a coherent target. The early sparring between Public First and Leading the Future showed, he argues, that an opponent can derail a spending push with far less money, which makes focused spending self-limiting.

What he wants standing by 2027

Leicht's remedy is deliberate fragmentation: a roster of uncorrelated bets, each able to absorb funding, each competing for the same safety money. He wants ideologically distinct vehicles that can translate AI safety for the heterodox right, pro-tech moderates, the populist left, and national-security hawks, so that a future bill can reach 60 Senate votes with a rationale available to every constituency. He floats a technocratic "friends of the future" PAC as one example and stresses that his aim is to get the competition of ideas started rather than to pick its winner.

The timing constraint drives the piece. Credible Washington operations need reputation, senior operatives, prior spending, and tested messages, all of which take time; donor-advisory funds assembled at the last minute, he argues, will be brittle and unfit for a high-salience fight. His concrete asks: would-be donors should declare their policy interests and make advance commitments before the windfall lands, and already-capitalized philanthropies should scout founders, seed organizations, and fund polling now, partly to crowd out the grifters he expects to prey on donors they see as "gullible nerds." The bottleneck he names is talent — senior operatives who can be nudged on substance but will not take tactical direction from safety funders, a loss of control he calls worth paying for.

What to watch

The essay is one day old, and no published reaction from safety funders had appeared as of this writing. The record does bear out the tension Leicht describes. Public First Action told Axios in late June it had raised $80 million for the cycle, an escalation of exactly the concentrated, preemption-opposing posture he treats as the field's overexposed bet. Whether a formal lab-employee collective-giving vehicle already exists is unconfirmed; Leicht gestures only at informal donor aggregation and advisory schemes, and public reporting names no dedicated fund. The wider money is documented: by The Nation's review of federal filings, super PACs funded by the crypto and AI industries have amassed more than $321 million across 14 committees this cycle. If the listings come and the windfalls follow, Leicht's question turns practical fast: whether the channels get dug before the flood arrives, or after.
