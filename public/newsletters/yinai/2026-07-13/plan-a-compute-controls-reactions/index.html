Plan A Bets on a US-China Compute Deal. Its Loudest Critics Want No One Holding the Lever.

The AI Futures Project's superintelligence roadmap has split its readers along one axis: govern compute from the center, or distribute authority so no cadre commands it.

By Adaeze Okafor · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1037 words

The AI Futures Project, whose 2025 AI 2027 forecast Zvi Mowshowitz credits as "a so far remarkably accurate set of predictions," has published AI 2040: Plan A, a year-by-year scenario in which Washington and Beijing agree in 2029 to slow the race to superintelligence and push the handoff to 2040. The plan sets its own schedule in plain terms: "In 2035, we pause at top-human-expert level AI in order to maintain human control." Then, in 2040, it unpauses. Mowshowitz spent a 9,600-word roundup cataloguing the reactions and opened by declining to sign on: "I am not endorsing Plan A." The democratic-consent and physical-limits objections had their airing in this section earlier in the week; the argument since has settled onto a different fault line, between governing compute from a center and distributing authority so that no group holds the switch.

The deal on paper

The mechanism is concrete, which is why it draws fire. Plan A asks the two governments to agree to avoid a reckless race to superintelligence, back the bargain with "total research transparency" for AI R&D, and intentionally enter a regime of "mutually assured compute destruction," under which each side keeps the ability to wreck the other's compute if the deal collapses. Axios's Ashley Gold compressed the prescription to three words, "slow everything down," in a first look quoted in the roundup. Scott Alexander, whose writing made it into the plan without a co-author credit, frames the ask as a surplus argument: the slowed path still delivers a cancer cure by 2035 and triple-digit growth, beating the timelines the accelerationists themselves demand (his introduction). Co-author Ryan Greenblatt, the second most accurate AI forecaster of 2025 by the roundup's accounting, concedes the odds up front: "Plan A isn't likely to happen, but pushing for something like this seems worthwhile." The byline runs through Daniel Kokotajlo, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead and Greenblatt; Semafor identifies the group as headed by Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI researcher.

The distributed-institutions objection

The counterposition crystallized around Séb Krier, AGI policy development lead at Google DeepMind. Jason Crawford quoted Krier's charge that under such a scheme "a cadre of elites decides which research directions are permissible, caps global compute and robotics." Crawford then asked for the missing rival: a "what to do" proposal built on "polycentric, competitive, or distributed institutions," or on Vitalik Buterin's d/acc principles. He was careful to add that a scenario is a way to think concretely, and is not itself an argument. The demand names a real gap. Krier's deeper objection targets the machinery itself; he calls building an "entire apparatus tasked with maximally empowering the government" dangerous, and nobody in the exchange has yet produced the distributed alternative Crawford wants to see.

Buterin, quoted at length in the roundup, refused to settle into either camp. He granted that Plan A improves on the darker option, because mutually assured compute destruction would give "one of 2-5 actors the ability to trigger a global compute winter," rather than letting a handful of actors selectively disenfranchise their enemies while exempting themselves. His own program is d/acc: formal verification, open and secure hardware, defensive biotech, work he argues is worth doing whichever of the two worlds we turn out to inhabit. His proposed compromise is a set of pre-agreed triggers, super-pandemics or mass unemployment, that would move skeptics and worriers alike toward a pause once reality picks a side. His verdict on his own idea, in the roundup: probably naive, though he sees zero non-naive plans on offer anywhere.

The authoritarian charge, and who has answered it

The centralization objection has a blunt version. Ramez Naam, after parsing the plan's restrictions, called it "a recipe for authoritarianism," arguing the proposals violate at minimum the spirit of the First and Fourth Amendments. From the opposite flank, MIRI's Nate Soares doubts the plan works at all, "I doubt their Plan A would work as written," though he thinks a serious attempt could produce the evidence that moves world leaders toward a full shutdown. The defense on the centralization point, so far, comes from commentators. Zvi argues the plan is "designed to try and head off authoritarianism," including what he calls authoritarianism by the AIs, and Buterin reads its compute-destruction regime as spreading the trigger rather than concentrating it. Whether any of the six authors has answered the specific claim, that the governance apparatus itself concentrates power, the roundup does not show.

The will the plan assumes it has

Every version of the deal depends on political will that barely exists today, and a companion post makes the deficit measurable. Charbel-Raphaël, arguing that the binding constraint on AI safety is political will rather than research, counted the awareness gap directly: of 1,534 submissions to the UN Global Dialogue, 15 mentioned superintelligence and exactly one mentioned takeover, while roughly 7% of the US Congress has publicly discussed AGI or loss of control (Alignment Forum). He also cites Greenblatt's illustrative estimate that takeover risk sits near 45% under today's weak coordination against 7% under an enforceable international slowdown. That the estimate comes from a Plan A co-author gives the two posts their connective tissue: on Charbel-Raphaël's reading, governance buys the direct risk reduction and buys the time for technical safety work.

What to watch

Three markers will tell whether this cluster moves. First, whether any of the six authors answers the elite-capture charge in the terms Krier posed it; by Zvi's account, Krier has precommitted to not engaging further, so the reply, if it comes, will have to come from the plan's side. Second, whether a distributed-institutions rival actually appears. Crawford is asking for one, and Bleys Goodson, who per the roundup seeks what he calls "a less authoritarian way to a safe open-research ramp-up" and offers a modeling site to help, is open about not yet having such a plan. Third, whether the 2028 US election, which the scenario titles "AI on the Ballot," surfaces any actor willing to open a compute channel to Beijing. Buterin's trigger-based compromise met an immediate question in the roundup: who would make that agreement, and how could anyone expect it to be honored.
