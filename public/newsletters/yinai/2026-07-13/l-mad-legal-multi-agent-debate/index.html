More lawyers help, more rounds hurt: a legal-debate test flags deliberation as a failure mode

Nguyen and colleagues find role-assigned debate lifts statutory-entailment accuracy by up to 8 percent, while extra discussion rounds push agents to reinforce each other's errors.

By Priya Raghunathan · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

Tan-Minh Nguyen and colleagues test multi-agent debate on one of law's cleaner problems, deciding whether a passage of statute entails a given hypothesis, and find that the shape of the conversation matters more than its length. Their Legal Multi-Agent Debate framework assigns distinct expert personas to separate agents, a presiding judge and two opposing counsel arguing for and against entailment, and improves on strong single-agent baselines by up to 8 percent on Japanese Civil Code entailment.

The full paper evaluates COLIEE Task 4 statutory-entailment items from three recent competition years, running Qwen3 backbones at three sizes alongside a Llama 3.1 8B baseline and aggregating verdicts through consensus thresholds (majority, supermajority, unanimity) or a ranked-choice vote. A separate ablation on the Qwen3-8B backbone scales two dimensions independently, from two to five agents and from one to five discussion rounds. Widening the population yields a modest upward trend in accuracy. Deepening the deliberation yields a gradual decline the authors call “over-deliberation drift”: instructed to critique earlier answers, agents generate unnecessary doubts, and in one worked example a correct first-round verdict on Civil Code Article 357 is overturned by a flawed second-round objection that pulls the whole group off the right answer.

The gains land unevenly across capability levels. The mid-tier Qwen3-30B-A3B averaged 80.86 percent alone and 88.46 percent under consensus debate, while the strongest single-agent setup, Qwen3-32B with self-consistency at 89.98 percent, matched or edged out its own debate variants. At the bottom of the range, debate hurt: Llama 3.1 8B, near 72 percent on its own, lost accuracy under the debate protocols, a result the authors attribute to a “competence threshold” below which weak agents validate each other's flawed premises instead of correcting them.

The authors present the findings as “practical boundaries and safety margins” for deploying collaborative agent systems in high-stakes legal reasoning, and the evidence covers one statutory-entailment benchmark rather than open-ended legal judgment. Within that scope the pattern held: extra agents improved accuracy, and extra rounds of argument eroded it.
