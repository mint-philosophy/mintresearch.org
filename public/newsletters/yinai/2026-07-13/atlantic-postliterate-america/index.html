A Postliterate Public, Asked to Govern AI It Cannot Read About

Rose Horowitch's Atlantic cover story says America has stopped reading deeply. Set beside the thin state of AI's public debate, that diagnosis becomes a governance problem: oversight assumes an electorate that can still parse a hard argument, and fewer can.

By Iris Calloway · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1048 words

A document that governs a technology is only as good as the public that can read it. That premise sits under nearly every proposal for AI oversight, from disclosure rules to safety audits to the expectation that citizens will hold the institutions building these systems to account. Rose Horowitch's August cover story for The Atlantic argues that the premise is quietly failing. Americans encounter more text than any generation before them and sustain attention on less of it, she writes, and the consequence reaches past literature into the machinery of self-government.

The numbers she assembles are not ambiguous. On an average day in 2004, 28 percent of Americans read for pleasure; by 2023 the figure had fallen to 16 percent, a drop that Jessica Bone and colleagues traced across 236,000 responses to the American Time Use Survey. That same study found the steepest declines among Black Americans, lower-income households, and rural residents, so the readers who remain skew toward the already advantaged. Fewer than half of adults read a book of any kind in 2022, and roughly a fifth of adults now account for four-fifths of the books read at all.

Horowitch's argument turns on comprehension rather than decoding. Nearly 30 percent of American adults cannot paraphrase or make inferences from a multipage text, up from under 20 percent in 2017. In a 2024 study she cites, English and English-education majors at two regional Kansas universities were asked to read the opening paragraphs of Dickens's Bleak House with the full internet at their disposal; only 5 percent came away with an accurate, detailed understanding of the passage, and at least a quarter took the figurative language literally, inferring that dinosaurs walked the streets of 19th-century London. Oversight depends on exactly the faculty in retreat here: holding a long, adversarial argument in mind and testing it against what you already know.

The tool that automates the thinking

Generative AI enters this account as accelerant and symptom at once. Horowitch treats writing as the process by which people work out what they think, a view she draws from the Georgetown computer scientist Cal Newport, who argues that composing forces thought into an orderly, linear sequence and exposes shoddy reasoning along the way. Automate the writing and you risk automating away the thinking it produces. Early evidence points that way. Michael Gerlich surveyed and interviewed 666 people and found frequent AI use negatively correlated with critical-thinking scores, a relationship his analysis traces through cognitive offloading, with the youngest participants showing the heaviest reliance on the tools and the lowest scores. A team led by Nataliya Kosmyna at MIT wired 54 essay writers to EEG across four months of sessions, splitting them among a chatbot, a search engine, and no tools at all; the chatbot group showed the weakest neural connectivity of the three and struggled to quote from essays they had just written. Neither study establishes lasting damage, since Gerlich's data are correlational and the MIT team tracked a few months of sessions. Both describe strain on the same capacity that governing a fast-moving technology asks of a citizen.

A second-order effect lands directly on regulation. Horowitch reports a threefold increase in books released on Amazon each month since ChatGPT launched in 2022, alongside a surge in scientific-journal submissions, much of the new text fluent and much of it derivative or wrong. A citizen trying to follow an AI policy fight now wades through machine-generated commentary that reads well whether or not it is true. Discernment grows more necessary at the same moment the reading habits that build it decline. That paradox sits at the center of her essay, and it is the part most relevant to anyone who expects public pressure to discipline the technology.

Where the reading crisis meets the governance gap

The stakes come into focus when Horowitch's diagnosis is set beside the actual state of AI's public debate. Writing on the Alignment Forum over the weekend, Charbel-Raphaël, who runs an AI-safety think tank and discloses that interest in the post, counted the submissions to the United Nations' Global Dialogue on AI: of 1,534, exactly one mentioned takeover risk, and fifteen each mentioned superintelligence or AGI. By his count, 40 current members of the US Congress, about 7 percent, have ever publicly discussed AGI or loss of control. A postliterate public and a policy conversation that barely engages the technology's hardest questions are the same shortfall seen from two ends. Oversight assumes readers who can follow a technical argument to an uncomfortable conclusion, and the supply of such readers is thinning as the arguments grow more consequential.

Horowitch is not alone in reading media shifts politically. She revives Joshua Meyrowitz's 1985 argument in No Sense of Place that electronic media teach voters to choose leaders for how they look and sound on screen over how they write and reason, and she names Donald Trump the first postliterate president, a communicator of epithets, self-contradiction, and memes fitted to an oral, screen-native culture. The NYU philosopher Kwame Anthony Appiah gives her the plainest statement of the stakes: if people lean on AI until they lose the capacity to develop their own views, "we'd stop being the kind of humans that we are." She handles the counterargument with care. Every new medium, from the novel to the newspaper to television, drew the same laments, and print-book sales and independent bookstores are growing right now. The optimists' data, she notes, describes a shrinking, self-selected minority.

Countermeasures exist, and some are measurable. Nearly two dozen states have banned phones during the school day; in the year after Texas's ban took effect, one Dallas school district checked out 200,000 more library books than the year before, a rise of nearly 25 percent, and some teachers are adding whole books back to their curricula. None of that touches the regulatory question Horowitch leaves open. If informed consent is the basis on which a democracy licenses a powerful technology, a public that increasingly cannot read the terms cedes the decision to whoever writes them. The texts themselves will survive, she writes; every word in Alexandria's library would fit on a single chip today. The ability and the desire to read them are draining away.
