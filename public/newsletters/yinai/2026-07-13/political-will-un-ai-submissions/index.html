The safety argument almost nobody at the UN made

Charbel-Raphaël Segerie scraped 1,534 submissions to the UN's first AI governance dialogue. One mentioned takeover. He argues that winning over policymakers now does more for safety than producing new research.

By Silas Boyd · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1156 words

When the United Nations convened the first meeting of its Global Dialogue on AI Governance in Geneva on 6 and 7 July, it did so atop a public archive of written submissions filed ahead of the session. Charbel-Raphaël Segerie and his colleagues scraped that archive, counted 1,534 submissions, and ran a plain string search. The word "cyber" appeared in 518 of them. "Superintelligence" and "artificial general intelligence" each appeared in 15. "Takeover" appeared in exactly one.

Segerie, executive director and co-founder of CeSIA, the French Center for AI Safety, uses that tally to open a 9,000-word argument on the Alignment Forum with a flat title: "The current bottleneck is political will, not research." The claim is that the field has enough policy ideas and enough known safeguards to act on catastrophic risk, and that action stalls for lack of people willing to spend political capital getting decision-makers to care. The UN scrape is his opening exhibit for a wider count of who is, and is not, in the room.

A funnel with almost no one at the top

Segerie frames political will as a funnel that runs from basic awareness through to costly, sustained championing. By his count, 40 current members of the US Congress have publicly discussed AGI or loss of control, about 7 percent of the body, with the number roughly doubling every five and a half months. Of those, he counts roughly three genuine champions pushing safety measures at real institutional cost. Awareness thins further in the arenas where rules get written: he cites Corporate Europe Observatory figures showing that of 97 senior European Commission meetings on AI in 2023, industry took 84, civil society 12, and academics one, with Google alone taking 10.

The imbalance extends inside the safety community itself. US AI governance work, Segerie writes, runs about 3.6 researchers for every advocate, an ecosystem that rewards novel analysis over the slower labor of repetition and relationship-building. He argues the marginal hire should tilt the other way, toward parity or beyond. He also points to how little the existing playbook has produced. On SaferAI's risk-management tracker, the highest-scoring company, Anthropic, sits at 35 percent, against a ceiling of 59 percent if a single company adopted every best practice already found somewhere in the industry. DNA-synthesis screening remains voluntary, incident reporting is rare, and Anthropic has, by his account, more than once accidentally trained its models against the chain-of-thought reasoning that safety teams read to catch misbehavior.

Where the will is supposed to lead

The reason political will carries so much weight in Segerie's telling comes from Ryan Greenblatt's "Plans A, B, C, and D for misalignment risk," which sketches a spectrum of how hard the world is trying. Plan D roughly describes the present: the leading company does not treat misalignment as a priority, and about ten people on the inside, working with a few percent of its compute, carry the safety effort. Plan A is a strong international agreement with real enforcement and a genuine slowdown. Greenblatt's tentative estimate has conditional takeover risk falling from about 45 percent under Plan D to about 7 percent under Plan A, an 84 percent relative cut. Advocacy, in Segerie's reading, buys movement up that spectrum: lower risk directly, and time for technical safety work to actually be applied. Greenblatt allows that a substantial fraction of the risk remaining under even his best plans comes from takeover being very hard to avoid; Segerie answers that hard alignment argues for more political will, since only will buys the time and seriousness a hard problem demands.

Segerie's ranked interventions follow. He puts direct, repeated engagement first: knocking on the doors of media, policymakers, and influential institutions, aimed at the roughly 100 to 1,000 decision-makers he believes shape the field. He wants safety organizations to coordinate around shared demands, such as international red lines, so advocates arrive with one ask rather than many; more than 200 of the 1,534 UN submissions, he notes, already call for red lines of some kind. He presses for relationship-building before any crisis, on the theory that a warning shot only moves policy when the audience already holds the concepts to interpret it. He argues for naming superintelligence and extinction risk in public rather than sanding them down to preserve access, a habit he faults his own organization for indulging when it dropped the risk page from its redesigned website. And he flags open consultation windows, like the UN's, as low-competition venues where a marginal submission becomes unusually visible.

The persuasion research he leans on cuts against any hope of a quick fix. David Broockman and Joshua Kalla's deep-canvassing study, as Segerie reports it, found that ten-minute doorstep conversations shift attitudes by roughly 0.08 standard deviations, enough to move someone from somewhat opposed to slightly less opposed. He takes that sobering baseline as evidence that opinion moves through repeated contact, coalition-building, and independent reinforcement sustained over years.

The author's own hedges

Segerie states his conflicts directly. He has run an advocacy-oriented think tank for two years, so a conclusion that the world needs more advocacy flatters his own work, and he tells readers to discount accordingly. He writes that he is more confident about the problem than about the solutions. He rests parts of the case on rough estimates, anecdotes, cross-pollster comparisons, and a loose analogy to climate advocacy; he allows that some AI regulations could backfire and that research on genuinely unresolved alignment questions still needs to happen. The string-matching method behind the UN figures is itself blunt: a submission could gesture at loss-of-control risk without using any of his search terms, and a raw count of the word "takeover" measures vocabulary as much as concern.

That last point marks the open question the piece leaves. Segerie's core empirical claim, that awareness of catastrophic risk is scarce among the people who write the rules, is hard to dispute from the UN numbers. His stronger claim, that safety hiring should shift toward advocacy because implementation has fallen so far behind existing ideas, is the contested one, and the tension shows inside his own argument: his July 11 postscript notes that AI 2040: Plan A, a detailed scenario published the same week by the AI Futures Project with Greenblatt among the contributors, hinges on a US-China agreement by 2029, a political precondition he describes as far off. Published, named rebuttals remain thin this soon after the post appeared; the comment threads on the LessWrong crosspost are the first place to watch for them.

The UN process itself offers a second test. The Geneva meeting opens a recurring series, with a second session scheduled for May 2027 in New York. If Segerie's diagnosis is right, the counts in the next archive, how many submissions name loss of control against how many name cyber, will show whether the field started knocking on doors or kept writing papers.
