Foreign Policy splits the AI question between arms race and borderless risk

Two new FP podcasts ask who is winning a $300 billion race, and whether any single government can govern a model that ignores borders.

By Iris Calloway · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

Foreign Policy's weekly podcast roundup set two readings of the AI contest beside each other. On Ones and Tooze, Adam Tooze takes the arms-race frame at face value, asking "Who is winning? And what does 'winning' even mean?" The episode page puts private investment in AI at nearly $300 billion for 2025 and likely to grow this year, names Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta among the companies spending, and asks what the race means for the economy and for humanity. The description stops at the question; it does not say who Tooze thinks is ahead.

The second episode inverts the frame. "No Borders," the opening episode of ProHuman: Governing the Global AI Revolution, a co-production of Foreign Policy and the Future of Life Institute, starts from the premise that frontier systems such as the fictional Mythos ignore national borders, so governance must be global. Future of Life CEO Anthony Aguirre explains why a model like Mythos threatens every country at once, and former Irish president Mary Robinson interviews Yoshua Bengio on why no single state can address the risks alone. The Elders, who announced the eight-part series on July 8, state the organizing claim plainly: the largest risks originate in a handful of jurisdictions while "the impact of AI will be felt across the globe."

Mythos did double duty this week. The same fictional episode appears in Charbel-Raphaël's Alignment Forum argument that "the current bottleneck is political will, not research," where reactions to Mythos illustrate how apparent warning shots get disputed, reframed, or confined to narrower worries such as cyber risk. ProHuman works that gap from the institutional side. The Elders' announcement lists later episodes pairing Stuart Russell on international red lines, David Song on safety standards, Sam Ramadori on technical cooperation and emergency response, Eugênio Garcia on benefit sharing, and Julia Morse on international institutions. One series asks who is ahead; the other argues the question cannot be answered one country at a time.
