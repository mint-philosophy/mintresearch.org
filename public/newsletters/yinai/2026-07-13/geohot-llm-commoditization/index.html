Hotz Bets the Value of AI Won't Land Where the Money Went

The comma.ai founder says frontier labs are overvalued because open models commoditize the gains, even as hyperscalers commit $700 billion to capex.

By Nadia Osei · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 2

George Hotz spent a July 12 blog post professing love for the technology whose backers he thinks are badly overpriced. Writing on his personal site, the comma.ai founder, who at seventeen became the first person reported to carrier-unlock an iPhone, called himself "absolutely giddy" about AI and said he is excited for new language models, self-driving cars, video generation, and coding agents. He then turned the enthusiasm into a market claim: the labs building frontier systems will not capture the value they create. AI's advance, he argues, runs mostly on Moore's law and general progress in computing, and the case against open weights is, in his words, "a fear of commodification."

That thesis lands against a concrete number from the other side of the trade. Dave Patterson, as relayed by the Laude Institute, says Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta will spend more than $700 billion on capital expenditure this year, spending he attributes to competitive fear rather than economic sense. If Hotz is right that the output commoditizes, that outlay is buying rents his thesis says will erode.

The evidence in Hotz's post for making do with local models is a single datapoint. He set up a Linux box running the opencode agent on a GLM-5.2 model on his own hardware, and reports that asking it to install tmux with his own configuration simply worked. The post offers no benchmarks, so how that setup compares with hosted frontier systems cannot be judged from the text.

Hotz also softened his earlier dismissal of AI coding, conceding he "might have been a little harsh" about models being unable to program. He now reports getting some boost from them and calls effective use a new skill, while relaying a Linus Torvalds comparison, agents at a 10x productivity gain against compilers at 1000x, and judging both figures extreme. His skepticism survives in an aside: if the gains are real, he asks, where is all the new software they should imply? The vibe-coded stuff, he writes, is still "slop."
