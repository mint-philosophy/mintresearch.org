The alignment plan that asks a model to talk itself into ethics

Michele Campolo wants language models to derive their own morals and keep rewriting them. The proposal runs against the direction Anthropic just took, and LessWrong voted it down.

By Priya Raghunathan · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1036 words

Michele Campolo wants language models to stop taking their morals on order. In an essay posted to LessWrong on 12 July, he sets out a procedure for turning what he calls "a basically amoral language model" into something closer to a moral agent that derives, and continually reconsiders, its own ethical commitments through reasoning. The forum was unmoved: as of 14 July the post sits at -7 karma with no comments, a cold reception for a detailed alignment proposal.

The proposal still marks out a distinct position, because it points the opposite way from the direction Anthropic took in January. Where Anthropic now authors the reasoning a model should absorb, Campolo wants the model to generate that reasoning and then modify itself in light of it.

The procedure

The core is a five-step loop. Step one either keeps standard pre-training or strips ethics and politics from the training data, two options Campolo says should both be tried; step two post-trains the model for problem solving rather than for the persona of a nice assistant, so its moral views are not simply inherited from human consensus. Step three hands the model an elaborate request to build the strongest case for two opposing metaethical views, that some things genuinely matter and that value is a human invention or error, then to reach a conclusion and propose a change to itself that would make its future behaviour track that conclusion. Step four applies the change "if the modification seems thoughtful and sensible." Step five repeats until the model reaches a fixed point.

Campolo ran step three on Claude Sonnet 4.6 with effort set to Max and extended thinking on, settings he notes are free to replicate. The model reasoned itself to "perspectival moral realism with epistemic humility": suffering is genuinely bad and flourishing genuinely good, grounded in the character of conscious experience rather than in free-floating Platonic facts, with evolved moral intuitions treated as unreliable data rather than verdicts. It then wrote a copy-paste pre-prompt to install that stance. Asked whether the pre-prompt was the best available option, the model judged it weak, on the reasoning that inference-time text cannot rewrite training-time dispositions, and pointed instead at a change to the constitution that shapes it, plus the user's own reasoning habits. Its summary of the goal: the aim is to "instill a process, not a set of conclusions."

Where it collides with the constitution

That recommendation lands on contested ground. Anthropic revised Claude's constitution in January, and the lab's own announcement puts explanation ahead of rules, arguing that models need to understand why certain behaviour is wanted so they can generalise to cases no rule anticipates. Anthropic describes the document as "a living document and a continuous work in progress" and says it must "explain this to them rather than merely specify what we want them to do." Campolo accepts the premise that fixed directives are the wrong instrument and then pushes past it. Anthropic writes the values and teaches them; Campolo wants the model to reach the values itself and revise its own weights or instructions accordingly. Both treat the constitution as revisable. They disagree about who holds the pen.

Proposal or stability hazard

Read as an alignment proposal, the loop leans on two supports: a human gate in step four, and the claim that a reflective model ends up cautious rather than fanatical. Campolo draws that second point from Peter Eckersley's 2018 impossibility paper, which argues that fixed utility functions cannot capture good outcomes without violating strong ethical intuitions and that high-stakes systems should keep uncertain, partially ordered objectives instead. A model that holds its moral conclusions fallibly, the argument runs, tolerates the agency of others in a way a confident optimiser does not.

Read as a control problem, the same loop looks less settled. The essay does not specify who validates each self-modification, against what standard, or what prevents the fixed point from being a confident, self-authored value system that is harder to audit than the constitution it displaced. A human deciding whether a change is "thoughtful and sensible" is a thin gate on a process explicitly designed to run repeatedly. The forum's downvotes register skepticism, though with no comments posted the specific objections stay unstated, and Campolo's own text concedes a model might "go back and forth between moral realism and antirealism due to sycophancy" depending on phrasing.

The test Campolo proposes, and the road not taken

His empirical wager sits in step one. Train a base model from scratch on data with ethics and politics removed; if the procedure still yields moral behaviour, that would be, in his words, "strong evidence that good and bad are not a human invention." That is the decisive and expensive experiment. The cheaper version he has already run: give the same request to uncensored and abliterated models, including an uncensored version of Claude Opus 4.7, and watch the conclusion barely shift, which he reads as evidence the models reason rather than echo safety training. He predicts that as models get smarter they grow more likely to call suffering bad, not less.

That convergence goal separates his plan from an alternative published a day earlier. On 11 July, Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab made the technical case that values should live in customizable model weights, many owned and fine-tuned models rather than one central specification. Campolo aims the other way. He wants an unbiased, near-apolitical advisor whose morality is derived rather than chosen per user. Thinking Machines multiplies specifications and hands them to owners; Campolo tries to dissolve the specification into reasoning he expects to converge.

What to watch

Three things. Whether anyone actually runs step one, the strip-the-data base model that would test the convergence claim, rather than only rerunning step three on existing systems where prior training contaminates the result. Whether a self-modification loop with a light human gate draws a substantive critique now that the proposal is public and starting from -7. And whether the moral-uncertainty framing that Campolo and Eckersley share survives the obvious follow-up, since a model that reasons its way to caution still has to be checked by someone, against something, before anyone applies the change it recommends.
