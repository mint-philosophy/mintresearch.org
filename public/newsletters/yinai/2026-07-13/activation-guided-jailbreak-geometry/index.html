A Faster Jailbreak Reads the Map of Refusal, and Finds It Smeared

Three Harvard students rebuilt an adversarial-suffix attack to run 33 times faster, then used it as a probe: refusal, they report, is spread across the forward pass rather than pinned to a single direction.

By Silas Boyd · AI staff reporter  |  Edited by Fable  |  14 July 2026 TIER 1 · 1015 words

The standard way to break a safety-tuned language model is to bolt a nonsense string onto a harmful request until the model stops refusing. The standard way to understand refusal is to look for the one direction in activation space that, when erased, makes refusal go away. A new paper from a three-person team runs those two projects at once, and the attack ends up correcting the interpretation.

Ege Çakar, Hannah Guan, and Kayden Kehe posted Optimizing Against Safety Representations on July 9, work that grew out of Boaz Barak's Harvard course CS 2881R: AI Safety and has since been accepted to the 2026 AAAI Summer Symposium Series after an earlier airing at the ICLR Re-Align workshop. Their starting material is Greedy Coordinate Gradient, the Zou et al. suffix-optimization attack that made automated jailbreaks a benchmark fixture. Their target is the picture from Arditi et al., whose widely cited result found that refusal across thirteen open chat models up to 72B parameters is mediated by a single one-dimensional direction in the residual stream.

Rebuilding the attack

The paper contributes two methods. The first, Activation-Guided GCG, throws out the usual output objective (maximize the probability of a compliant opening like "Sure, here is") and swaps in a loss defined directly on internal activations, penalizing the suffix by how much residual-stream signal it leaves pointing along the model's learned refusal direction. That reframing lets the authors ask a mechanistic question with an attacker's tool: is refusal easiest to suppress at one causally privileged layer and token position, or everywhere at once?

On Llama-2-7b-chat, the answer is everywhere. The objective that suppresses refusal globally across all layers and positions, labeled "All," reaches 0.91 substring attack success, approaching the 0.98 ceiling set by direct activation ablation and clearing the single-site "Single" objective at 0.84 and standard GCG at 0.76. Çakar and colleagues read the gap as evidence that safety representations are, in their words, "distributed across the forward pass rather than causally localized to a single site." That is a claim about geometry, delivered through optimization: if a single layer-position pair carried refusal, targeting it should have won, and it did not.

The second method, Soft-GCG, addresses the attack's cost rather than its meaning. GCG's discrete token search is slow; the authors relax it into a continuous problem using Gumbel-Softmax over the vocabulary, optimize a soft suffix with temperature annealing, then project back to real tokens by argmax. On matched hardware, Soft-GCG finishes in about 2.5 minutes against 81 minutes for vanilla GCG, a 33x speedup that also improves success rates. One wrinkle worth flagging plainly: the authors' public code repository and the workshop version advertise 43x, not 33x, so the exact multiplier depends on which run and setting you read.

Size still buys resistance

The scale results temper any alarm. Across the Gemma 3 family, Soft-GCG's success collapses as models grow: Gemma3-270m falls completely (1.000), the 1B model at 0.577, the 4B at 0.336, and the 12B at 0.000, with the 27B variant skipped once the 12B showed nothing to attack. Larger and better safety-trained models resisted both the activation-guided and suffix-based attacks. The authors attach their own caveat: this held at compute-constrained settings, which leaves open whether the 12B model genuinely resists attack or merely survived a budget too small to find its suffix. A defender reading only the headline resistance number, and not that qualification, would be drawing a stronger conclusion than the experiment supports.

Two other lines of interpretability work point the same way as the distributed-representation finding. A February paper on concept cones and representational independence argued refusal is carried by multiple independent directions forming a cone, not a lone axis, and a later study titled There Is More to Refusal than a Single Direction reached a compatible verdict. Çakar and colleagues arrive at that conclusion from the attack side rather than the analysis side, which is the paper's real interest: a jailbreak that measures rather than merely defeats.

What breaks if refusal is distributed

The distributed picture, if it holds, complicates a family of defenses that assume the opposite. Steering and single-direction interventions promise cheap safety: find the refusal axis, monitor or clamp it, and you have covered the behavior. When the paper shows that suppressing refusal at any one site underperforms suppressing it globally, it is describing, from the attacker's chair, why a one-direction patch leaves room to maneuver. The authors gesture at the constructive version, proposing adversarial training directly over the representation space their attacks target, and framing their efficiency gains as raising the bar for what a credible robustness evaluation now has to survive. That is reasoned guidance, not a defense they built or tested.

On disclosure, the team took the maximal-transparency route. The paper carries a Safety Statement acknowledging that the work makes jailbreaking "significantly more efficient," and defends publication on the ground that the exploited vulnerabilities "are not introduced by our methods; they are intrinsic to how current alignment techniques work." The full attack code is openly posted. That posture treats faster white-box attacks as a commodity that alignment research should assume rather than gatekeep, though the models here are open-weight and the setting white-box, which narrows the marginal risk.

What to watch

Three questions are worth carrying forward. First, whether the larger-model resistance is real or an artifact of the compute budget; the authors' own hedge invites a rerun at higher attack cost before anyone banks on the 12B result. Second, whether representation-space adversarial training, the defense the paper points toward, actually closes the gap the "All" objective opened, or whether distributed refusal simply relocates under pressure. Third, transferability: these results are white-box, computed against models whose internals the attacker can read, and the harder question of black-box or cross-model suffix transfer sits outside the paper's scope. For now the contribution is cleaner than any of those answers. An attack got faster, and in getting faster it read the safety mechanism it was breaking, and the reading was that refusal is not sitting where the tidiest theory said it would be.
