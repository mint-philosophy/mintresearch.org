AI access improves patent drafting and seniors’ later editing scores

A three-month trial compares assisted work with later editing. Senior lawyers performed better on a final task intended to be unaided; results for juniors were more varied.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 September 2026 874 words · ~4 min

AI access improved patent drafting, with larger estimated gains for juniors. Senior lawyers who had received AI access then performed better on an editing test intended to be unaided. David Autor of MIT and colleagues at Google report that divergence in Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise? Evidence from a Three-Month Field Experiment in Patent Drafting, released as an NBER working paper on September 7. Their experiment examines whether help with everyday production also develops the judgment needed to assess someone else's work.

The researchers enrolled 133 practicing lawyers at eleven US intellectual property firms and randomized access within firms and experience groups. About two-thirds received Google's experimental InFlow writing assistant and access to training tailored to patent work. InFlow could generate text, rewrite selected passages, discuss source documents and critique drafts against specified criteria. Controls used their usual legal tools without generative AI writing assistance. Lawyers could use InFlow in their ordinary work between assessments; the researchers tested them shortly after access began and again after three months.

The drafting exercises required lawyers to turn inventor notes and diagrams into patent language, adding narrower claims to a supplied main claim and explaining how the invention worked. Independent patent attorneys, unaware of treatment assignments, assessed legal strength and technical accuracy, including whether the wording preserved commercially useful protection. AI access improved scores at both assessments and reduced the incidence of poor drafts. Estimated gains were larger for juniors, although the sample could not establish the size of that seniority difference confidently. Most estimated time savings also accrued to juniors.

For the final assessment, 91 participants marked up a flawed AI-generated patent draft under instructions prohibiting AI use. The exercise required them to repair the document and explain its weaknesses. Seniors previously given AI access scored roughly half a standard deviation above senior controls. The researchers classified lawyers with at least seven years of experience as senior. Junior lawyers showed no detectable average improvement. Their scores spread out: fewer mediocre results, more poor results and an offsetting increase in good ones. That pattern is compatible with AI helping some juniors learn while impeding others; the study could not determine why individuals differed.

The authors investigated the editing differences through an exploratory NotebookLM analysis of the submissions. High-scoring work, predominantly from treated seniors, removed wording that needlessly restricted patent protection, restructured legally vulnerable sections and explained the reasons for changes. Some lower scorers concentrated on surface corrections or exhausted their time before reaching the claims. Juniors sometimes identified a legal defect without fixing it, but that behavior appeared in both treatment and control groups. The authors suggest that automated drafting may free experienced lawyers to concentrate on strategy while reducing juniors' practice at the underlying work. They did not experimentally test that mechanism.

Firms rejected the researchers' preferred baseline skills assessment, so the learning interpretation depends on randomization producing comparable starting abilities. Roughly a third of the enrolled lawyers did not complete the final task, although completion rates were similar across treatment and control groups. Researchers also could not directly enforce the AI prohibition. They screened submissions for possible AI use through model-assisted and manual review; excluding suspect submissions preserved a positive senior estimate but weakened the evidence for it (p = .06, compared with .02 before exclusions). Such screening cannot establish that every remaining submission was unaided.

InFlow gained new features during the study, including automated document review. Firm-level usage records show activity concentrated around the assessments, limiting what can be inferred about sustained daily practice. Google funded the experiment's direct costs, and recruitment depended on firms already doing patent work for the company. Separate surveys of ordinary client work were too imprecise to establish that the benchmark gains translated into faster completion of real patents.

Autor and colleagues connect their results to earlier experiments that separated assisted performance from later learning. In Hamsa Bastani and colleagues' 2025 PNAS paper, Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics, students given ordinary AI help performed worse on subsequent unaided exams. A version designed to supply hints, guided by teacher-provided solutions, largely avoided that harm, without producing a clear independent learning advantage. Their design varied the assistance students received, something the patent experiment did not do.

Earlier legal evidence was more encouraging. Nicholas Bednar and colleagues' Artificial Intelligence and Human Legal Reasoning, a Minnesota Legal Studies research paper also cited by Autor's team, found that law students who initially used AI to synthesize legal materials subsequently performed better when applying them without AI. The patent trial adds sustained access among practicing specialists to evidence dominated by students or professionals learning unfamiliar tasks.

Shawn Kohrman argues in his September 10 essay Apprenticeship Was Never Just About Skills that employers must preserve direct supervision and human correction as AI takes over routine work. In his September 14 Marginal Revolution commentary, Tyler Cowen argues that fixed-task experiments can miss productivity gains when people eventually move into different work. The trial leaves that longer-term adjustment unresolved. Autor and colleagues propose repeated unaided assessments and experiments that vary workflows and time spent on tasks. Those would help determine whether firms can preserve the practice juniors need while capturing the drafting improvements already observed.
