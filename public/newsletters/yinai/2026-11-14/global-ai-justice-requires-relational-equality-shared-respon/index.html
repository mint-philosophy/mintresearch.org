Global AI justice beyond the race for dominance

Boris Babic and Brian Wong Yue Shun defend shared responsibility for diffuse harms and equal participation in AI governance. Their September Element argues that cooperation can also serve states’ interests.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 November 2026 1083 words · ~5 min

Countries can gain from sharing AI technology while giving smaller states and their citizens more influence over its development, Boris Babic and Brian Wong Yue Shun argue in Geopolitics of Artificial Intelligence. Cambridge published the two University of Hong Kong scholars' Element on September 18. They connect an account of responsibility for dispersed harms with an argument for global relational equality: people should be able to participate in developing, accessing and governing AI without being subordinated to more powerful actors. Their strategic argument is that cooperation can serve states' own interests as well as justice.

Babic and Wong begin with a hypothetical asylum and immigration model called Bernie. Its government refuses foreign training data and supplements domestic data with a voluntary survey skewed toward middle-class, majority-ethnic respondents suspicious of immigration. The model produces discriminatory recommendations, while its opacity makes them hard to explain. An applicant rejected because the system predicts future criminality never enters the country, so the prediction cannot be checked against that person's subsequent conduct there. Applicants scattered across borders may also lack the resources or legal standing to seek redress.

The authors use Bernie to explain how geopolitical conditions compound familiar AI risks. Nationally separated datasets can make systems less reliable when applied across borders. Models reflecting different national assumptions can also recommend conflicting responses to the same diplomatic situation. Race rhetoric encourages governments and companies to relax safeguards, withhold information and treat rivals' gains as losses. Babic and Wong describe these effects as interactional risks: harms arising from the combination of AI systems and the political circumstances in which they operate.

Where an identifiable actor culpably causes a harm, they accept conventional responsibility and liability. Their concern is the many cases where development and deployment involve numerous institutions, changing models and dispersed causal contributions. Blaming the nearest human operator can conceal the role of everyone else. To address such cases, they adapt Iris Marion Young's social connection model: people who help reproduce the structures generating an injustice acquire shared responsibilities to remedy it, even when they cannot be blamed for causing a particular injury.

They refine Young's account in two ways. Following Maeve McKeown, they understand connection through actions that reproduce the background conditions of injustice. A venture capitalist funding an autonomous-weapons company can have such a connection even if another investor would have supplied the money. They also argue that forward-looking responsibilities can be enforced without treating every connected person as culpable for the original harm. Criticism, denial of promotion or failure to renew a public mandate can respond to neglected duties. Where investigations uncover intentional or reckless wrongdoing, ordinary liability remains available.

A second hypothetical system, Susan, identifies targets for lethal drones. Its development involves political pressure, weakened safety standards and defective synthetic data; its deployment results in innocent deaths. Babic and Wong assign responsibilities across the institutions involved, proportionate to actors' power and capacity to change events. A junior sergeant cannot reasonably be expected to sabotage the system, while a senior engineer can be expected to investigate data suppliers. Their proposed duties include compensation and acknowledgment of harm, as well as preventing recurrence. Shared responsibility supplements culpability and leaves identifiable wrongdoers accountable.

For AI's benefits, the authors draw on relational egalitarianism, associated with Elizabeth Anderson and others. Equal standing involves more than receiving an equivalent share of resources. Citizens of a poorer country may gain access to an AI product while remaining subject to rules they cannot influence. Conversely, distributing a discriminatory model more widely does not fix its disrespectful treatment of the people it describes. Babic and Wong therefore want justice assessed across the relationships created by AI development, use and governance.

They extend this view globally because coercion and dependence cross national borders through trade rules, sanctions and other institutions. Their justification ultimately concerns individuals; states can represent them and help secure equal standing. The resulting proposals include antitrust action against dominant AI firms, greater availability of model weights, AI literacy for disadvantaged communities and stronger agenda-setting powers for smaller states. They also argue for opposing unjustified export restrictions. They allow that some hierarchies can be justified, while rejecting a system that leaves weaker parties dependent on powerful actors' discretion.

The strategic account asks why states should cooperate even when motivated by their own interests. Babic and Wong argue that AI is poorly modeled as a contest with one winner. A model can be used by multiple parties, and a provider can gain customers and influence by sharing it. This differs from a prize available only to whoever arrives first. They criticize the US AI Action Plan's combination of enthusiasm for spreading American technology and fear of others benefiting from it, arguing that dependence on American systems can itself serve American interests.

Their preferred model is a stag hunt, a coordination game with two stable outcomes. Countries can share and benefit together, or remain isolated because each expects the other to withhold access. Neither country wants to change course alone under those expectations, even though mutual cooperation would improve both positions. Institutions that make commitments credible can help move countries toward sharing. Babic and Wong go further under a stronger assumption: if countries prefer even unilateral openness to mutual isolation, sharing becomes their best strategy regardless of the other's choice. These conclusions depend on their account of the relative benefits; game theory does not establish that ordering by itself.

The authors apply this argument to export controls and the national-security defense associated with Dario Amodei. They argue that restricting a rival's progress sacrifices commercial and cooperative gains, while encouraging technological self-sufficiency and retaliation. They favor continued Chinese rare-earth exports and wider access to semiconductor manufacturing equipment. Their recommendation depends on judging those benefits greater than the security costs invoked by opponents. The book explicitly leaves low-probability existential risks outside its analysis; its account of responsibility does address military uses and lethal targeting.

Babic and Wong propose institutions to make cooperation more dependable. An international mediation or adjudication center could hear AI disputes; alternatively, a restored WTO appellate system could contain a specialized body, though its trade mandate would cover less. Multilateral agreements could improve access to chips, equipment and minerals, with smaller states using their importance in supply chains to preserve room for nonalignment. Domestic laws could assign duties of redress across development and deployment, with governments acting when private actors fail to discharge them. These proposals connect their demand for equal participation with the practical need for credible commitments and continuing cooperation.
