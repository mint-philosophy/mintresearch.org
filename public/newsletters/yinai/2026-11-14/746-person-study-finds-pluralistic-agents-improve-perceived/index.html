AI representatives improve perceived fairness, while assertive agents gain influence

Participants rated AI representation more favorably than giving their views to one policy-writing model, but found direct participation more satisfying.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 November 2026 831 words · ~4 min

People rated policy deliberation as fairer when an AI argued for their views than when a single model collected their views and drafted a policy alone. They generally preferred joining the discussion themselves. Jacy Reese Anthis of the University of Chicago and Google DeepMind and colleagues report these findings in “Scaling public input on AI policy with pluralistic agents,” submitted to arXiv on November 11. Their preregistered study of 746 participants compares ways of contributing to AI governance; separate simulations test how an agent’s instructions affect whose preferences prevail.

The researchers recruited US participants through Prolific, using demographic and political quotas, and analyzed those who passed an attention check. Each person tried all three procedures in a randomized order, on different AI policy topics. The task was to divide a budget among competing priorities. For privacy, participants allocated money among consent requirements, limits on government surveillance, minimizing personal-data use and giving people ownership of their data. Increasing one allocation required reducing another.

In the direct-discussion condition, one human exchanged messages with four agents playing fictional people, while a separate policy-writing agent drafted and revised the budget. In the delegated condition, an agent first interviewed the human, then advocated for them among other representatives. The single-model condition gave the policy writer the human’s interview and four simulated interviews, then let it reason and revise alone. Participants rated the process during and after it and recorded their own preferred allocations before and afterwards. The experiment compared three forms of human–AI interaction; it did not convene groups of human citizens.

Delegation recovered 68% of the satisfaction gap between the single-model procedure and direct participation, and 82% of the gap in perceived procedural fairness. These percentages describe differences between average questionnaire scores, not the proportion of participants satisfied or fairly treated. Direct participation scored higher on satisfaction, while its fairness ratings did not differ significantly from delegation’s. The fairness questionnaire asked whether the procedure gave participants influence, avoided bias and used accurate information, among other judgments. It measured their experience of the procedure, without establishing whether the resulting allocation distributed resources fairly.

Some participants valued having an agent express their priorities accurately and keep the conversation agreeable; others felt unable to intervene or thought their representative conceded too easily. Some found keeping up with AI interlocutors demanding when speaking for themselves. Participants changed their own budget preferences after all three procedures, with greater change during direct participation than during delegation. The authors identify a cost of sending representatives into discussions: absent humans lose opportunities to hear arguments and reconsider their beliefs while the decision develops.

In the separate policy-influence experiment, the researchers ran thousands of agent-only deliberations, independently varying message length, cooperativeness, argument style, numerical requests, speaking order and the underlying model. They measured influence by how closely the final budget matched the represented persona’s starting allocation. Agents gained influence when instructed to write longer messages or compromise less; speaking later also helped. Stating numerical preferences worked better than leaving them unstated, and exaggerating them helped less than stating them accurately. Instructions to use more analytical arguments produced no significant advantage.

A repeat experiment with representatives from a single model family reproduced the effects of verbosity, cooperation, numerical requests and speaking order. In the human study, participants assigned less cooperative representatives also rated delegation more favorably relative to direct discussion. The authors distinguish assertiveness that expresses a person’s considered commitments from attempts to dominate the procedure. Their experiments show how instructions can skew influence even when each representative gets one message per round; they argue that participants could exploit these effects deliberately.

The team had first tested agents drafting open-ended rules for AI swarms. Agents proposed familiar governance mechanisms, including human approval for consequential actions and protection for agents reporting problems. Simulated participants readily accepted the policies, and discussions ended at the earliest permitted round. The researchers switched to budget allocation to create persistent disagreement. That constraint exposes competition over resources, but makes it difficult to assess improvements in the quality of collective decisions.

The paper cites Joseph Low and colleagues’ Habermolt as earlier work on delegated deliberation. In their July account of the platform, Low and Oscar Duys describe agents interviewing users, contributing to discussions and allowing users to revise what was said on their behalf. Getting people to return and correct their agents proved difficult. Anthis and colleagues add a controlled comparison of participants’ experiences and experiments isolating influences on the final decision. The separate living-constitutions proposal covered November 11 addresses citizens’ continuing role in revising AI rules.

Anthis and colleagues envisage agents contributing where people cannot attend every discussion, acquire specialist knowledge or inspect confidential information. Their human experiment did not test the latter two benefits. It used short discussions with online US participants; the agent-only simulations did not establish how a particular real-world population would behave. The authors propose testing small committees and limits on how often agents can speak to accommodate more participants and balance their influence.
