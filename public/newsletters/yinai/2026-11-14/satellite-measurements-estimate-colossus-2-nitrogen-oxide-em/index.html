Satellites track Colossus 2’s gas-turbine pollution

Estimated nitrogen-oxide emissions were roughly 19 times the rate expected from the planned permanent turbines with pollution controls.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 November 2026 605 words · ~3 min

Gas turbines powering Colossus 2 emitted nitrogen oxides at roughly 19 times the rate expected from the planned permanent fleet with pollution controls, Kevin D. Gauld and colleagues at MIT and Carbon Mapper estimate in “Quantifying AI data center nitrogen oxide (NOₓ) emissions from space.” First posted to arXiv on August 23 and revised October 19, the paper uses satellite observations to follow emissions from the Southaven, Mississippi, power plant supplying the nearby Memphis datacenter. Its comparison is between temporary turbines operating during the study and permanent equipment specified in a March construction permit.

The researchers’ analysis follows the plant from August 2025 through August 2026. They first detected its plume in December 2025; estimated emissions rose as more generating equipment arrived. After February, emissions averaged about 870 kilograms per hour, with a reported spread of 165 kilograms per hour across the two-week estimates. Pollution increased immediately downwind of the plant, while monitors at a neighboring power station recorded much smaller emissions. The researchers also checked wind changes and found that weaker ventilation could not explain the increase. These measurements give a concrete example of the local pollution concerns described in James O’Donnell’s reporting, covered November 9.

The March 11 permit issued to MZX Tech specifies 41 natural-gas turbines, each equipped with selective catalytic reduction and an oxidation catalyst. Its three equipment groups have different per-turbine emissions limits; adding the nitrogen-oxide limits gives approximately 47 kilograms per hour across the fleet. Those short-term limits apply on a three-hour rolling basis and exclude startup and shutdown, while separate annual limits include them. The paper describes continued reliance on temporary turbines while the permanent installation was delayed. The 19-fold comparison therefore measures the difference from the planned, controlled fleet; it does not establish a 19-fold breach of an applicable legal limit.

NASA’s TEMPO instrument measures sunlight scattered back from the atmosphere, using ultraviolet and visible wavelengths to identify pollutants. From geostationary orbit it can scan North America hourly, allowing repeated observations as winds and emissions change. The measurement used here is the amount of nitrogen dioxide in a vertical atmospheric column. Inferring a rate of nitrogen-oxide emissions requires accounting for how wind carries the plume and how its chemistry changes after combustion.

Gauld’s team aligned cloud-free images with the wind before averaging them, so a plume blowing in different directions would remain visible. They subtracted pollution observed before the turbines arrived to isolate the plant’s plume from the surrounding city. Their calculation combines the amount of nitrogen dioxide across the plume with wind speed, then estimates total nitrogen oxides. Comparisons with continuously monitored US power plants showed that the initial estimates were systematically low, so the team calibrated the Colossus results against those direct measurements. Emissions at the controlled-fleet rate would fall below this analysis’s detection capability.

Kang Sun and colleagues documented a related measurement problem in “Hourly Nitrogen Oxides Emissions Estimated From TEMPO and Comparison With Facility-Level Monitoring Data,” published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres in November 2025. They compared satellite estimates with smokestack monitors at 14 US power plants. Accounting for changes between successive hourly observations improved their estimates, but the satellite-derived rates remained systematically below the monitors’ readings. They identified difficulties in retrieving atmospheric nitrogen dioxide and converting it to total nitrogen oxides near large sources. Gauld and colleagues cite that work in explaining why their estimates require calibration.

The permit separately requires equipment-level testing and monitoring, including initial tests and continuous emissions monitoring. Those compliance measurements differ from the satellite study’s two-week averages. The study estimates emissions from the site; it does not measure residents’ exposure or resulting health effects.
