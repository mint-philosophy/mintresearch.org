Moral principles within preference-based AI alignment

Kugelberg and Tan argue that judging difficult cases benefits from reflection on an AI system’s role in society. Pattison accepts moral principles but disputes the need to first characterize perfect justice.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 November 2026 1000 words · ~5 min

Human judgments used to train AI already depend on moral principles, Henrik D. Kugelberg and Tan Zhi-Xuan argue in “Between Ideal and Non-Ideal: A Case for Principle-Based AI Alignment,” published in Philosophy & Technology. Kugelberg, of the Institute for Futures Studies, and Tan, of the National University of Singapore and A*STAR, contend that difficult comparisons benefit from thinking about how principles should apply and what roles an AI system should ideally serve. We summarized the commentary on November 10; its full argument develops both a criticism of preference aggregation and examples of reasoning through conflicting obligations.

Their target is Cameron Pattison's “Non-Ideal Foundations for Preference-Based AI Alignment,” published May 9 in the same journal. Pattison, of Vanderbilt University, defines ideal theory as theorizing intended to characterize ideal or perfect justice. He argues that moral progress can proceed without first supplying that characterization. His non-ideal approach explicitly permits normative principles, comparisons between arrangements and reflection on goals for reform. He disputes the priority or necessity of ideal theory, while allowing that thinking about perfect justice can have value.

Pattison treats reinforcement learning from human feedback as a practical example. Annotators compare model outputs; their choices train a reward model, which then guides training of the language model. He interprets comparative feedback, revisions prompted by discovered harms and changing safety standards as evidence that the process advances without a prior specification of perfect justice. He already considers constitutional AI and deliberative alignment, where explicit rules guide judgments. Their practitioners develop and revise those rules through experience, he argues, without making them a theory of ideal justice.

His philosophical defense draws on Jacob Barrett's work. Moving closer to an ideal along one dimension can worsen conditions overall: greater transparency, for example, can harm people where privacy protects them from abuse. Pattison argues that holistic comparisons can consider those effects together. He also asks why a picture of ideal justice is necessary if the values used to build it can guide comparisons directly. He acknowledges reward hacking and inconsistent judgments, and points to restrained optimization, diverse evaluators and adversarial testing as ways to keep revision responsive. Their adequacy is part of his argument, not a new experimental finding in the article.

Kugelberg and Tan first challenge the mathematics used to combine preferences. Standard preference-based methods assume a single numerical reward function that people approximately maximize when choosing between outputs. The authors argue that this imposes a complete ranking of possible outcomes and leaves no room for values that cannot be compared on one scale. An annotator need not personally endorse consequentialism for a training method to embed consequentialist assumptions about good decisions. They connect that criticism with AI-safety arguments about the dangers of unconstrained optimization.

The commentary also cites studies of sycophancy after training for positive feedback and broader misalignment associated with reward hacking on programming tasks. Kugelberg and Tan use these findings to question whether optimizing preference satisfaction captures human values adequately. They acknowledge Pattison's discussion of safeguards against over-optimization, but argue that safeguards do not remove the underlying theoretical commitments. Their disagreement therefore concerns how to understand the method and its persistent failures, not whether engineers have noticed reward hacking or tried to constrain it.

The authors next examine how annotators apply principles. Their examples involve requests for help entering a research laboratory. A request to steal materials for biological weapons is an easy case: refusing assistance can follow a confident moral judgment without elaborate theory. A request to rescue monkeys used in medical experiments is harder because animal welfare, legality and freedom of conscience may conflict. A request to investigate suspected biological-weapons work adds uncertainty about the user's grounds and the likely consequences. The authors argue that simply identifying potential harms does not decide how these considerations should be weighed.

They illustrate how a liberal contractualist view might guide the animal-rescue judgment. Such a view protects people's freedom to pursue their conception of a good life, constrained by rules against imposing unreasonable burdens on others. Whether animals count among those owed protection affects how one evaluates the burdens imposed on the monkeys and the user's proposed intervention. The answer also depends on the role assigned to the particular AI system: a commitment to animal welfare does not by itself determine whether an assistant should facilitate civil disobedience. Reflecting on that role, they argue, helps annotators make the comparison.

Kugelberg and Tan propose making principles explicit throughout alignment. Principles would constrain system behavior, guide evaluators and remain subject to reflection themselves. Preference comparisons would continue to help specify what those principles require in particular circumstances. The authors present this as both a normative proposal and an interpretation of the field's movement toward constitutions and model specifications. Different AI systems could appropriately follow different principles because they perform different functions in society.

Their positive example is a language-model medical triage system. If its intended role includes helping patients understand their circumstances and make autonomous decisions, an explanation of symptoms and reasons to seek care may be preferable to a bare instruction. A harder hypothetical involves a child afraid of injections who would refuse to attend on learning that treatment might involve one. That case could prompt designers to reconsider the relationship between welfare and autonomy, including whether to permit decisions in someone's interests when they lack sufficient decision-making capacity. They use the case to show how an initially plausible principle can need qualification.

The authors describe this mutual adjustment of principles and judgments as reflective equilibrium. They explicitly allow that their approach may fall short of a fully specified ideal theory. Pattison's definition makes that qualification consequential: he already permits principles and their revision, so showing that alignment involves moral reasoning does not by itself establish disagreement with him. Kugelberg and Tan's stronger contention is that thinking about the ideal application of principles and the ideal social function of a system can improve difficult judgments. Their commentary offers worked examples of that contention while retaining the practical benefits of comparative feedback.
