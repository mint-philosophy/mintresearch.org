Lambert calls for a US open-model institution after Huang’s departure

Lambert argues that open models need sustained investment, frontier research and political protection.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 November 2026 693 words · ~3 min

Nathan Lambert argues that American open-model developers need an institution capable of producing leading models and defending their continued availability after Jensen Huang’s departure from NVIDIA. In his November 14 Interconnects essay, “Open-source AI without Jensen,” Lambert describes Huang’s support as buying time for an immature industry to establish customers and revenue. His concern extends beyond financing: someone must attract exceptional researchers, intervene when projects encounter trouble and persuade policymakers to preserve access to open models.

Lambert starts with NVIDIA’s continuing commercial interest in openness. A diverse population of model developers would limit dependence on a few powerful customers capable of designing their own chips. He nevertheless expects the company’s immediate need to stabilize operations and meet customer commitments to displace some of Huang’s ambitious investments. In its November 12 transition announcement, NVIDIA appointed Colette Kress interim CEO and said its board expected operations, customer commitments and the product roadmap to continue without interruption.

Lambert identifies a promising business in helping enterprises adapt models to their own work and retain control over the resulting systems. Such customers can train an existing model further for particular tasks and run it on infrastructure they choose. He remains uncertain how large that market will become. Meanwhile, releasing model weights does not remove the need for chips to run them. Lambert argues that purchases by the largest closed-model developers leave inference providers competing for scarce computing capacity, allowing high serving margins to persist even when the models themselves are openly available.

His economic argument continues the October discussion of DeepInfer and open-model economics. Lambert argues that open weights alone cannot make intelligence a cheap commodity while computing remains scarce. He expects companies integrating models with data centers and chips to retain advantages of scale. Huang’s support, in this account, gave smaller participants time to develop sustainable businesses under those conditions.

Lambert fears that regulation could curtail that experimentation before the businesses mature. He worries first about poorly designed state rules and then national restrictions, describing growing political support for safety regulation as a threat to open-model availability. He predicts that a serious cyberattack involving a popular Chinese open-weight model would intensify demands for restrictions. His proposed response is advance coalition-building: open-model supporters need someone able to intervene before a crisis determines policy. Lambert also argues that much of the value created on private infrastructure escapes public measurement, making benefits harder to demonstrate against highly visible harms.

Lambert also emphasizes the difficulty of assembling leading researchers. He draws on the November 13 ChinaTalk and SemiAnalysis discussion, where Dylan Patel says leading researchers are concentrated in established laboratories or scattered across startups. He rates Moonshot’s talent alongside leading US teams, identifying computing and deployment capacity as its disadvantages. Jordan Nanos adds that NVIDIA’s spending on Nemotron has not produced a model superior to Chinese competitors.

Nanos expects NVIDIA to execute its near-term plans, drawing on its supply chain, customer relationships and balance sheet. He would judge its longer-term prospects by whether senior technical staff stay over the following year. Patel describes contacting Huang personally to resolve open-source software problems. NVIDIA retains extensive institutional resources; in Patel’s account, some practical assistance depended on Huang’s intervention.

NVIDIA has already organized collective work on open models. Its March 16 Nemotron Coalition announcement brought together model builders including Mistral AI and Thinking Machines Lab to contribute research, data and evaluations. NVIDIA described plans for an initial base model co-developed with Mistral, to be trained on DGX Cloud and underpin Nemotron 4. Members would continue developing their independent products. The coalition announced shared model development; Lambert wants an organization capable of sustaining both frontier competition and political advocacy.

Lambert sees Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Small as an encouraging Western release, while judging it well behind Kimi K3.1 Pro. His larger concern is whether an open-model developer can repeatedly raise enough money to keep pace with the closed frontier. He names leaders at Meta, Amazon and Microsoft as possible allies, but calls for open-model supporters to organize proactively. He wants a coalition that can help an American open-model developer keep raising money and intervene before a political crisis threatens its work.
