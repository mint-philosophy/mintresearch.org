Sharadin separates the costs of AI assistance from displacement

His November Cambridge Element extends an earlier argument about children with an account of changing labor markets and the enjoyment and achievement adults could lose when work becomes easier.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 14 November 2026 1111 words · ~6 min

Losing a cognitive job need not deprive someone of a flourishing life, while keeping that job with pervasive AI help could make it less satisfying. Nathaniel P. Sharadin of the University of Hong Kong develops this argument in AI & Cognitive Work, published November 7 in Cambridge's Elements in Philosophy and AI. His earlier Synthese paper, “Cognitive enfeeblement by design,” argued that ubiquitous assistance threatens children's development, as we covered in October. The Element places that concern within a broader account of the economics of cognitive work and its value for adults.

Sharadin defines work economically, as tasks undertaken for remuneration, and cognitive work as work performed primarily through mental activity. Lawyers, programmers and analysts are examples; manual occupations can involve substantial thought without falling into his category. He identifies a historically unusual combination of demanding skills, formal credentials, high wages and resistance to automation. Because output has depended on scarce human attention, productivity has often grown slowly, making services such as education and legal advice relatively more expensive as wages rise elsewhere.

He considers two possible futures: AI capabilities keep improving at roughly recent rates, or they plateau near their current level. These are assumptions for conditional arguments, not forecasts with assigned probabilities. Under continued improvement, he expects widespread displacement. Following Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo's task-based account of automation, he treats jobs as bundles of tasks: technology removes some tasks and creates others. But new tasks preserve cognitive employment only if humans can perform them better or more cheaply than AI. Continued improvement, he argues, threatens that condition as much for newly created cognitive tasks as for existing ones.

Sharadin examines the economic mechanisms that might preserve employment. Cheaper cognitive services can expand demand, but he doubts demand will rise indefinitely fast enough to absorb productivity gains. Automating some tasks can make the remaining human tasks more valuable while still reducing the number of workers needed. Wealth created by automation may finance more jobs in care or personal services, without preserving cognitive work's former position. Comparative advantage might leave humans some cognitive tasks, but their wages would remain constrained by the cost of deploying an AI system instead.

Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb's model of O-ring automation receives a separate reply. In that model, the quality of a finished product depends on the quality of its component tasks; automating some lets humans concentrate more attention on those remaining. Sharadin accepts that this can raise a worker's contribution, but argues that it does not establish how many workers an economy will employ. He also expects limits to the improvement that extra time can bring to human performance. Regulation, liability and organized labor may slow displacement, he adds, while facing economic pressures against maintaining restrictions.

Even if capabilities plateau, he expects assistance to spread through default settings and competitive pressure. Existing systems can take over parts of planning, searching and producing drafts, leaving workers to evaluate the results. Skill requirements may persist, but shift toward verification; credentials designed to test production may become less informative. Wage effects are less settled in this scenario. Sharadin acknowledges that sufficiently strong demand growth could sustain employment if assistance remains stable and humans remain necessary. He nevertheless argues that the full historical package, including slow productivity growth, cannot survive widespread adoption.

His defense of displacement starts with a claim about well-being: cognitive work has no exclusive claim to engagement, achievement, autonomy or social connection. Carpentry can absorb a skilled worker as coding does. Independence depends heavily on how a job is organized; a plumber can have more discretion than a junior lawyer. Losing the goods specific to a practice may be painful, but that applies to displacement from manual occupations too. He also argues that automating cognitive labor could expand research and make legal or administrative help available to people unable to afford professional assistance.

Sharadin does not deny that displacement can produce hardship. He treats transition support and the distribution of productivity gains as political problems requiring action. Dependence on institutions that distribute resources also needs accountable governance, though wage labor already leaves many people dependent on employers. His response to the gradual-disempowerment argument associated with Jan Kulveit and colleagues is narrower: losing cognitive employment alone does not make all human participation economically dispensable. Workers who move into other occupations still earn, spend and pay taxes. His optimism about displacement from cognitive work therefore does not settle the consequences of automating every form of labor.

For adults who retain assisted jobs, Sharadin identifies costs to enjoyment and achievement. Work can be absorbing when its difficulty stretches a person's abilities without overwhelming them. Assistance may lower that challenge until the job becomes boring. On Gwen Bradford's account, achievement requires competent causation and difficulty; removing the difficulty can reduce the achievement. Sharadin considers a less demanding alternative too: even if effortless accomplishment counts as achievement, the effort it required may help explain its contribution to well-being. Adults could seek challenge elsewhere, but an easier full-time job does not necessarily leave more time for demanding pursuits outside it.

He regards these adult costs as moderate compared with the developmental concern about children. Planning, controlling impulses and sustaining attention require practice. Assistance that both removes demanding tasks and offers an easy way around those remaining may interfere with the development of capacities needed to direct one's own life. His Open Future Principle holds that impeding this development is morally wrong unless there is substantial justification, while allowing that competing considerations can sometimes justify it. This reprises the central argument of his earlier paper; the Element supports it through developmental research and comparisons with helicopter parenting.

Sharadin distinguishes that prediction from opposition to every educational use of AI. Carefully designed tutors might preserve and calibrate useful difficulty. Evidence that such tutors help children would not resolve his concern about general assistance available by default and used to avoid effort. He also argues that writing, calculators and GPS left users responsible for managing their thinking, whereas assistants can take over planning and checking themselves. These claims concern the effects of sustained use on development; the Element presents an argument from existing research, not a new longitudinal experiment.

The distinction between useful output and participation also connects with Sasha Mudd's Kantian account discussed November 11. Mudd asks institutions to preserve opportunities to develop judgment and character. Sharadin's Element adds a different claim about paid employment: those goods need not depend on retaining cognitive occupations, while the effort involved in activities still matters. He acknowledges that better-than-expected economic outcomes would reduce the scale of the problems he describes, and that developmental evidence could revise his conclusions about assistance.
