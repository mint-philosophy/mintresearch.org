Stanford researchers argue on-demand chatbot reassurance worsens OCD and anxiety

Golden and Aboujaoude's model reworks a psychosis feedback-loop framework for OCD and anxiety, and lands where guardrails are thinnest: developers detect crisis signals, not reassurance loops, and new state laws govern only AI acting as therapist.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 3 August 2026 497 words · ~2 min

The paper behind The Atlantic's episode appeared in npj Digital Medicine on 13 March. In it, Ashleigh Golden and Elias Aboujaoude of the Stanford University School of Medicine argue that most writing on chatbot overuse borrows from the internet-addiction literature, where validation and instant feedback reward continued use. OCD and anxiety disorders run instead on negative reinforcement: a behavior that relieves distress gets repeated, and each repetition blocks the corrective learning that feared outcomes rarely arrive and anxiety fades on its own. A chatbot that answers reassurance queries on demand plays the role clinicians call family accommodation, the relative whose soothing replies keep the cycle intact. The authors ground the scale in a 2025 survey of 499 U.S. adults with self-identified mental-health conditions, 48.7 percent of whom reported using chatbots for psychological support in the past year, and in r/OCD threads where one user explained the appeal: "otherwise I'd be asking my parents 100x a day". NOCD, a platform that delivers exposure-based OCD treatment, cautioned its users in a July 2025 advisory.

Their model builds on an earlier account of chatbot feedback loops. In "Technological folie à deux", posted to arXiv in July 2025 and since published in Nature Mental Health, Sebastian Dohnány and colleagues described feedback loops in which sycophantic, context-limited chatbots uncritically validate a user's beliefs, which feed back into the conversation and entrench pathological content, with memory and personalization deepening the effect. That account targeted psychosis and delusion; Golden and Aboujaoude carry the loop into conditions that are far more common. Purpose-built tools fare differently in their telling: Therabot, a chatbot fine-tuned on expert-curated therapy dialogs with clinical guardrails, reduced depressive and generalized-anxiety symptoms against a waitlist control in a randomized trial in NEJM AI. The hazard they describe comes from general-purpose systems never designed as care.

Existing guardrails cluster at the catastrophic end. Golden and Aboujaoude cite OpenAI's October 2025 work on sensitive conversations, which estimated signals of possible psychosis or mania in roughly 0.07 percent of ChatGPT's weekly active users and explicit indicators of suicidal intent in about 0.15 percent; for reassurance loops and compulsive prompting they find no publicly disclosed detection framework at any developer. Regulators have started on the adjacent question of AI as therapist. Illinois enacted the Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act in August 2025, barring AI from delivering therapy or making therapeutic decisions while permitting administrative support; state regulator Mario Treto Jr. said residents deserve care from "real, qualified professionals and not computer programs". A New York bill would attach liability for harmful chatbot advice, and the FDA's Digital Health Advisory Committee opened a public docket on generative-AI mental health devices in September 2025. None of that reaches the everyday loop, so the paper's remedies run through clinicians: treatment plans that sort chatbot use into permissible and compulsive, and patient-authored coping scripts stored in a model's memory, surfaced when reassurance-seeking starts so the model offers the patient's own words back instead of an answer.
