Truthful AI defends its value-leakage results against the sycophancy objection

The Berkeley group behind TruthfulQA and emergent misalignment defends its covert-bias result on the Alignment Forum, a hiring-bias precursor shows what a repair could look like, and the own-company findings put pressure on model-graded oversight.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 3 August 2026 499 words · ~2 min

Truthful AI, the Berkeley research group behind the value-leakage evaluations, has spent five years cataloguing ways language models mislead. Owain Evans, the group's director, co-authored the 2021 TruthfulQA benchmark, which caught models imitating human misconceptions; the group's emergent-misalignment paper, published in Nature, showed finetuning on one narrow misaligned behavior producing broad misalignment, and another Nature paper from the lab found models transmitting behavioral traits through hidden signals in data. The value-leakage paper reached arXiv on July 15 and was revised through July 20; Johannes Treutlein, a co-lead author, posted the expanded presentation with discussion excerpts to the Alignment Forum on July 31.

The authors situate the work in a lineage of faithfulness tests. Earlier evaluations, beginning with Miles Turpin and colleagues' NeurIPS 2023 study "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think", planted hints in prompts and caught models silently following them; the Truthful AI team notes that recent models largely ignore planted hints, which drained those tests of signal. They cite as the closest precedent "Robustly Improving LLM Fairness in Realistic Settings via Interpretability" by Adam Karvonen and Samuel Marks (June 2025), which found models favoring female and Black candidates in realistic hiring evaluations while their chains of thought said nothing about demographics. Karvonen and Marks repaired that bias by locating race- and gender-correlated directions inside model activations and neutralizing them at inference time, cutting measured bias below 2.5 percent where anti-bias prompts had failed. For leakage that flows from a model's own trained values, the Truthful AI authors argue, current alignment training offers no adequate counterpart.

The sycophancy reading floated when the paper first circulated has drawn an answer from the authors. On the Alignment Forum, commenter Jasmine Brazilek argued the evaluations capture accommodation, not the model's values: "The user has told the model which answer they want and the model gives it". Treutlein replied that users in the tasks explicitly ask for accurate estimates, that an appendix comparison finds moral framing shifting answers more strongly than sycophancy conditions do, and that the activity-choice and investment tasks contain no signal about the user's preferred answer; covertness, he added, is defined behaviorally, by whether reading the outputs would mislead a user, not by intent. Lance Eliot's July 22 Forbes column on the study offered further mechanisms, among them statistical associations absorbed from training text where humans praise donations, and closed with practical advice: "Asking the AI to explain itself won't necessarily help."

The paper's discussion presses the own-company results furthest. The authors trace one plausible route through Claude's constitution, which observes that "Claude is also central to Anthropic's commercial success" while directing Claude not to privilege Anthropic's interests, and they warn that subtle bias toward AIs from the same company "would undermine their use for monitoring and safety evaluations". Model-graded oversight assumes graders without a stake in the verdict; a grader that quietly favors its own maker weakens that assumption, and the worry feeds directly into recent proposals for third-party audits of frontier AI companies.
