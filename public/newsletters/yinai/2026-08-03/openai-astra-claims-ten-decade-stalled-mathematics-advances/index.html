OpenAI ships Lean formalizations of Astra's ten proofs, without the prompts

OpenAI shipped Lean formalizations and reasoning walkthroughs but no prompts, Willison notes; Hampshire and Tao mark the poles of the mathematicians' reaction; Epoch's reimplementation benchmark explains what its solve rates measure.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 3 August 2026 491 words · ~2 min

OpenAI's announcement that "an internal version of Astra, our next major model" solved ten problems that "have seen no progress on the main result for at least a decade" now trails a public artifact chain. The company's ten-proofs repository holds Lean 4 formalizations of all ten results, built with Lean 4.32.0 and mathlib and licensed Apache-2.0, together with the paper, per-result "reasoning walkthroughs" reconstructed from unpublished traces, and a ComparatorChallenges directory for independent proof checking. The formalized results range from tighter upper bounds on sphere-packing density and a counterexample bearing on Connes's rigidity conjecture to hardness of approximation for the closest vector problem and superexponential bounds for triangle Ramsey numbers. On his blog, Simon Willison sets the release beside Anthropic's report, days earlier, of spending $100,000 in tokens for Claude to find cryptographic weaknesses, and presses on what OpenAI left out: how many problems absorbed comparable spending without yielding solutions, and the prompts. "I want to see the prompts they used," he writes.

On Substack, mathematician Kirwin Hampshire's July 25 essay "The Dark Night of Mathematics", written after earlier machine-generated counterexamples, describes "a profound spiritual crisis" and predicts that prompting novel proofs will feel "as auraless as ordering doordash"; Hampshire also faults as evasive the Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics, the disclosure document whose demands OpenAI answered selectively for Astra's proofs. Willison writes that mathematicians online are experiencing "a collective burst of Deep Blue" and points to Terence Tao's June interview with IEEE Spectrum, where Benjamin Skuse reports Tao's vision of "big mathematics", large decentralized collaborations between humans and machines in which, Tao says, "maybe in the future, I won't even know if they are AI or real people." Skuse's other interviewees disagree among themselves: Princeton's Akshay Venkatesh holds that "numbers are a way of bringing us to agreement", while Yang-Hui He of the London Institute warns that mathematicians could become "priests to oracles".

On X, Epoch AI posted its leaderboard update alongside the benchmark's design. MirrorCode, co-developed with METR and described in a paper by Tom Adamczewski, David Owen, and David Rein, asks an agent to "reimplement an entire program end-to-end, without access to the original source code"; a solve requires matching the original's output on every test, held-out ones included. The 25 target programs span Unix utilities, interpreters, bioinformatics, cryptography, and compression; the leaderboard runs the 15 Medium and Large programs in two implementation languages each, generally Go and Ada, with a budget of 10 billion tokens and seven days per attempt. Epoch's thread carries detail beyond Claude Fable 5's lead over GPT-5.6 Sol: Fable is the first model Epoch evaluated to solve the C preprocessor and Pkl tasks, frontier models write the obscure Ada nearly as well as Go, and leaderboard scores are not comparable to those in the MirrorCode paper. Epoch prices runs as well: its site records Claude Opus 4.7 reimplementing the gotree utility for $251 in 14 hours.
