Chakrabarty and Ginsburg assemble the market-harm record Kadrey v. Meta lacked

Chakrabarty and Ginsburg's book-market study answers the question Judge Chhabria left open in 2025, revises an earlier NBER reading of AI's book flood, and lands on a platform whose AI disclosures never reach readers.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 29 July 2026 492 words · ~2 min

The study behind those numbers takes aim at an earlier benchmark. In the NBER working paper "AI and the Quantity and Quality of Creative Products", economists Imke Reimers and Joel Waldfogel found that LLM diffusion tripled new book releases between 2022 and 2025, that books containing AI text made up over half of 2025 releases, and that consumers came out about 7 percent ahead in surplus because the surge added many modest-quality books. Reimers and Waldfogel flagged AI from short previews and asked how those titles themselves fared; Chakrabarty's team ran detection across each book's full text and measured what the entrants did to everything around them. On that measure the market looks worse: titles with observed sales per quarter grew 19.2-fold over three years while quarterly revenue grew 8.9-fold.

In Kadrey v. Meta, decided in June 2025, Judge Vince Chhabria ruled that Meta's training on the plaintiff authors' books was fair use because they offered no empirical evidence of market harm, while writing that market dilution, harm from a flood of substitutable AI works, could defeat fair use on a developed record. The study, with Columbia copyright scholar Jane C. Ginsburg as coauthor, presents itself as that record. It quotes Chhabria's question about what happens "as more and more AI-generated books are written" and answers that the crowding effect grows with scale. The authors also mark the doctrine's limit: AI books competing with works a model never copied raise no claim, since "competition without copying is not infringement", so the theory, which they note the U.S. Copyright Office has endorsed, ties liability to the upstream copying that enabled the competing output.

Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing guidelines require publishers to tell Amazon when a book contains AI-generated content, defined as "text, images, or translations created by an AI-based tool", exempt AI-assisted work from disclosure, and put no label in front of customers. None of the 14,419 books in the sample disclosed AI involvement, and the authors suggest that silence may spare AI entrants a penalty from readers who would balk. The paper cites Bowker figures: more than 3.5 million U.S. self-published titles registered in 2025, about 84 percent of ISBN-registered releases, most reaching market through KDP.

Much of the reply thread went after the detector. Skeptics asked whether Pangram, the study's detection tool, misfires on older prose; Chakrabarty answered that "It never flags anything pre 2023 as AI". Romance author Carla Krae objected from inside the market, writing that readers reject AI books they can identify, that detectors flag books written in the 1990s, and that AI titles "sink unless the scammer also has ad dollars". The revenue arithmetic also bears on who captures AI's value, a question Kobe Yank-Jacobs pressed in "Bernie's bad bet on OpenAI": creating value and capturing it come apart, and here the capture went to a prolific few, one pseudonymous author earning $1.7 million across eight titles in the twelve months after release.
