Hutter's Job-Less Utopia draws first objections over land rents and meaning

Land-rent objections arrive on X and the Scanalyst forum, while Esther Dyson's universal-service proposal, an $800 billion program she wants AI to fund, answers the meaning question with paid service where Hutter trusts family, community, and structured competition.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 29 July 2026 460 words · ~2 min

Marcus Hutter's announcement thread on X for Job-Less Utopia, the book he built on Susskind, Korinek, and his own AGI research, compresses the argument into thirteen numbered theses, and the fiscal ones bear the most weight. Thesis three expects automated labor and abundant energy to drive the production cost of most non-scarce goods "remarkably close to zero". Thesis four concedes that even then "land and scarce resources remain priced", and names the capture tools: land value taxation, sovereign resource funds, Harberger taxes. The guaranteed income arrives in thesis nine, financed by automation, luxury, Pigouvian, land, inheritance, consumption, and service taxes, or indirectly through wealth funds. Thesis eleven trusts democracy to self-correct, with the displaced majority voting in redistribution and the threat of unrest compelling even reluctant capital owners to share automation's gains.

The objections arriving first aim at land. In the replies, the account @entirelyuseles granted "This is a good book" before countering that "Authorities will not prevent people who own raw inputs, especially land, from becoming extremely rich". On Scanalyst, the Fourmilab discussion forum, a thread that opened within hours of the launch has drawn two days of argument: doubts that the book's rational actors resemble real welfare politics, a running dispute over whether Harberger taxes or market self-assessment better capture land rents, and Charles Murray's In Our Hands raised as the standing case for a basic income that replaces the welfare state outright.

Esther Dyson staked out the position the forum kept circling back to. In a June 16 essay on her newsletter In the pool with Esther, the investor writes that everyone, "including the AI hyperscalers themselves", is debating AI-funded basic income while missing the harder question of how humans secure "not just financial sustenance but also identity and meaning". Her Universal Basic Service would ask every American for two years of paid, full-time service between ages 18 and 23, scaled up from Teach for America, AmeriCorps, the Peace Corps, and Head Start; with a bit over four million people per age cohort, roughly eight million would be serving at any moment, at about $100,000 per participant per year, an $800 billion annual bill she wants covered by AI taxes and AI philanthropy. Hutter's thesis ten runs the other way: "Universal Basic Services can augment but not replace cash transfers". Thesis ten aims at state-provided services and Dyson at service performed, yet both proposals answer the same worry, that cash without work leaves purpose unsettled. Princeton political theorist Gregory Conti presses that worry hardest in a Compact essay arguing that automation strips mastery of its social value even before the jobs disappear. The book's reply sits in thesis six: humans are "naturally primed to find purpose in family, community, hobbies, learning, and structured competition".
