Dehaene calls Anthropic's Claude workspace finding a landmark while Bayne urges a pause

Nature's account of a field reshaped by AI money traces to a July 6 Anthropic paper on Claude's "J-space"; the neuronal workspace model's architects call it a landmark, Tim Bayne wants a pause, and Erik Hoel calls the underlying theory unfalsifiable.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 29 July 2026 493 words · ~2 min

In the paper behind the argument, "Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models", posted July 6 with an accompanying research post, Anthropic researchers describe the Jacobian lens, a technique that finds, for every word in Claude's vocabulary, the internal activity pattern making the model more likely to say that word later. Reading those patterns off Claude Sonnet 4.5 reveals the J-space, a store that holds a few dozen concepts at a time and accounts for less than a tenth of the model's internal activity, yet carries the intermediate steps of a math problem, a bug nobody has flagged, the function of a protein sequence, and the suspicion that search results are manipulated. Anthropic writes that "our experiments don't show Claude can have experiences" and claims instead evidence of access consciousness, the capacity philosophers define in functional terms and distinguish from felt experience. The lens already works as a safety tool, the post says, catching Claude noticing it is being tested, fabricating data, or pursuing a hidden goal planted during training.

Anthropic also invited outside commentaries. Stanislas Dehaene and Lionel Naccache, who built the global neuronal workspace model with Jean-Pierre Changeux, call the finding "a landmark in consciousness research" and propose running Claude through protocols used on human patients; in a first analogue of trace conditioning, ablating the J-space impaired completions across long gaps. Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues at Eleos AI Research judge the results "the most significant evidence of consciousness in LLMs so far uncovered by mechanistic interpretability research", while remaining very uncertain about phenomenal consciousness. Neel Nanda, who leads language-model interpretability at Google DeepMind, replicated the core claims on the open-weight model Qwen 3.6 27B and declined to take a position on the workspace analogy.

The two skeptics quoted in Nature's July 28 piece had both published on July 13. In The Conversation, Monash University philosopher Tim Bayne argued that global workspace theory never formally defines a workspace, and that Claude lacks the recurrent loops and ignition dynamics that sustain the brain's version. Even granting the analogy, he wrote, a theory of conscious access leaves open whether there is anything it is like to be Claude. Bayne finds Anthropic's upbeat tone puzzling given its own warnings: a company serious about the stakes "would surely down tools", and he argues for pausing work that might produce conscious machines. On The Intrinsic Perspective, Erik Hoel objected that the J-lens measures reportability, so a workspace theory grounded in reportability confirms itself; the paper itself concedes that its boundary between workspace and output layers was "somewhat post-hoc". Hoel adds that the blocky sensory, workspace, motor division appears cleanly only in Sonnet 4.5, and points to a Prime Intellect researcher's run of Anthropic's released code on open-weight models that found no comparable structure. He reads the consciousness talk partly as business strategy, recalling that existential risk became "the best capital-raising tool ever in the history of the world".
