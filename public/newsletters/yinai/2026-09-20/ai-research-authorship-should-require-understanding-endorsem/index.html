Daniel Litt separates sharing AI output from claiming mathematical authorship

His September 20 thread links authorship to understanding and endorsement. Replies address credit, collaborative papers and the difference between research data and work submitted to arXiv.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 September 2026 846 words · ~4 min

University of Toronto mathematician Daniel Litt argues in a September 20 X thread that anyone presenting a mathematical document as their own work should understand it and be able to defend it. He permits sharing AI-generated PDFs of any quality, provided people are honest about what they have done and accept that readers may judge them. Misrepresenting a document’s origins or one’s understanding of it, he writes, “shades into misconduct”. In a clarification, Litt allows someone to circulate an unread PDF if they say so. Putting one’s name on the document, however, ordinarily signals responsibility for its contents.

David Savitt agreed about misconduct but challenged whether such posting was acceptable: established academics may face little reputational cost while setting standards for everyone else. Litt answered that researchers could maintain a labelled section of their website containing AI answers they had not considered worth developing further. Levi Harris suggested a separate AI repository or an arXiv tag for papers whose authors disclaim responsibility or understanding. Litt rejected admitting those documents to arXiv; he takes an author’s signature to communicate endorsement and understanding.

Litt also separates permission to circulate a result from credit for discovering it. A reply from @Soledad7000 warned that posting an unexplained result could discourage anyone from understanding and properly writing it up, because the novelty credit had already been claimed. Litt responded that merely posting an automatically generated PDF should not earn that credit. Institutions should change incentives that reward such posting at the expense of developing knowledge. He expects hiring to adjust quickly, predicting that generating papers without deeper engagement will be an unsuccessful strategy by the next hiring season.

Collaborative research complicates responsibility for an entire document. Balázs Pozsgay noted that specialists working together cannot always understand every part equally well, a problem that predates AI. Litt said authors should identify the parts for which they are responsible. When Pozsgay explained that his field makes those divisions apparent at conferences but does not record them in papers, Litt called for changing that practice.

For AI laboratories, Litt endorsed a different form of release. Jason Rute proposed publishing collections of intermediate material, including proofs in the Lean formal language, unpolished AI write-ups and recorded reasoning, openly presented as research data for others to investigate. Rute compared this to releasing data from a physics experiment. Litt said he would prefer laboratories to handle their results that way.

Existing publication rules already connect authorship with accountability. arXiv’s moderation policy requires authors to disclose significant use of generative AI and holds each signatory responsible for the paper’s contents, including errors or plagiarism introduced by a model. Medical publishing’s ICMJE authorship criteria address divided expertise more explicitly: authors must know which colleagues are responsible for other parts and trust the integrity of their contributions, while agreeing to ensure that concerns about any part are investigated and resolved. Those criteria combine differentiated contributions with accountability for the whole work; they do not require every collaborator to possess identical expertise.

Litt had already distinguished producing mathematics from demonstrating expertise in his September 13 essay A beginning for mathematics, covered here on September 14. He welcomed interesting results however they were produced and proposed assessing understanding through oral defenses and sustained discussion. The current thread adds obligations attached to claiming authorship. In September, he also reposted Jason Abaluck’s argument for circulating results people do not yet understand. Abaluck allowed both reserving arXiv for understood work and awarding professional standing for contributions to understanding. Litt’s permission to share openly labelled output is compatible with those distinctions.

Litt’s concern about arXiv predates this exchange. In July 2025, he judged six of eight recent submissions mentioning the Hodge conjecture to be AI-generated nonsense containing invented references. His present proposal explains which responsibilities he thinks should accompany circulation of such material.

A separate dispute illustrates why disclosure and exposition raise distinct questions. Ali Jadbabaie, Amin Saberi and Suvrit Sra announced a September 19 preprint, Kadison–Singer partitions and Bilu–Linial graph signings in polynomial time, claiming algorithms for two discrepancy problems. Its AI-use statement discloses extensive work with Claude and OpenAI models. Aleksandar Nikolov criticized the paper’s writing, subsequently challenging its explanation of the main new idea and asking for a readable account of the proof. Jadbabaie acknowledged imperfect prose and invited technical corrections. Litt did not identify this paper as his target; the exchange concerns how authors explain work for which they have disclosed AI assistance.

Other proposals address the growing volume of material. David Craven reported that arXiv mathematics submissions rose from 4,040 in January to 6,307 in August, and questioned journals’ capacity to absorb the increase. Alexander Kasprzyk proposed metadata recording AI use to improve information about submissions. Recording which tools authors use is compatible with Litt’s demand that they endorse what they sign; his objection to Harris’s proposal concerned papers whose authors disclaim that responsibility. The September 11 Fields medallists’ declaration similarly called for time to explain new ideas and acknowledge prior work when announcing AI-assisted results.
