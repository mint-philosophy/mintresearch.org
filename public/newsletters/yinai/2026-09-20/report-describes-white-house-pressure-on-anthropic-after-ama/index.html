Politico reconstructs the June calls that forced Fable offline

New accounts of June 12 show officials considering board changes and Trump discussing imprisonment as Anthropic resisted a shutdown. Politico follows the export-control directive through separate Mythos and Fable restorations, while the promised public review framework remains absent.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 September 2026 1407 words · ~7 min

Politico Magazine's September 20 feature by Sophia Cai and Cheyenne Haslett reconstructs the private exchanges that forced Claude Fable offline in June. Their account draws on more than a dozen participants speaking anonymously and contemporaneous documentation; Anthropic declined to comment and referred them to its published statements. The shutdown and Amazon's involvement were already covered in YiNAI's June editions. The feature, shared by Josh Vlasto, adds the June 12 calls, coercive options officials considered, and negotiations over restoring access. It follows the relationship from Mythos's April release through Fable's July return and the subsequent breakdown in trust.

According to Politico, Fable 5 shipped on June 9 after testing by the Commerce Department's Center for AI Standards and Innovation, or CAISI, and Treasury approval. Two days later Amazon researchers found a way past its safeguards, and Amazon CEO Andy Jassy telephoned the White House. Amazon, an Anthropic investor, had been asked by the administration for feedback. AWS was also a launch partner in Project Glasswing, Anthropic’s April program giving selected organizations access to Mythos for defensive security work. The Amazon connection was reported that weekend. Politico adds a disputed account of the effort to reach Amodei: aides said they were told he was at a wellness retreat; Anthropic denied this, and a person close to the company said he was unavailable for one hour while other executives responded immediately.

On June 12, chief of staff Susie Wiles gathered National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross, White House attorney Will Scharf and deputy chief of staff Richard Walters to consider compelling a shutdown. Politico reports that they discussed the Defense Production Act and forcing changes to Anthropic's board. A person familiar with Trump's discussions told the reporters that, if Anthropic refused, the president said, “I want to send them to jail.”

The documented calls show how quickly the disagreement became a demand for compliance. Cairncross told Amodei that a widely available, readily bypassed model presented national-security risks officials could not accept. Amodei offered to explain guardrails, arguing that no frontier model was immune to jailbreaks and that Anthropic had never claimed otherwise. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said officials and industry participants had already concluded Fable could not remain generally available. Amodei's insistence that the behavior was expected only strengthened Bessent's objection. Amodei warned that withdrawal would establish a precedent against commercially deploying models above some capability threshold. When he said he could not withdraw Fable an hour after hearing the concern, Cairncross answered, “I have the answer I need.” Bessent called the refusal a bad decision.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's separate call lasted an hour without agreement. Politico reports that he began by praising Anthropic, prompting other officials listening in to text that he was supposed to pressure Amodei. At 5:21 p.m., Commerce sent a directive barring access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by foreign nationals anywhere, including Anthropic employees. Amodei called Lutnick roughly three hours later to ask whether excluding his overseas programmers meant taking the models down; Lutnick confirmed that was intended. Anthropic's same-evening statement said it disabled access for all customers to comply. It said the letter supplied no specific account of the security concern and the evidence conveyed to it had been verbal. Customers began looking elsewhere: Politico describes Telnyx chief executive David Casem accelerating his company’s move toward GLM, made by China’s Z.ai, after losing access.

Politico traces the policy dispute back to April 7, when Bessent warned five bank chief executives about Mythos's capacity to threaten financial institutions and other infrastructure. Microsoft had privately warned officials after receiving early access in March. Wiles assigned the response to Cairncross, whose office ran four working groups and sent industry a 31-item questionnaire. Companies had to sign nondisclosure agreements before seeing the proposed rules. His office's early-May draft proposed what companies understood as mandatory 60-day government evaluation followed by 75 days of restricted access for vetted users. OpenAI's internal talking points objected that this would disrupt model release schedules. A revision made review voluntary, and officials gave company representatives tightly controlled readings of the text on May 20. The next morning David Sacks persuaded Trump that delaying American models would benefit China; Trump canceled the signing. Sacks had left his AI-czar role that March.

Officials subsequently cut the review window in the revised proposal from 90 days to 30, Politico reports. Trump signed Executive Order 14409 on June 2 without a ceremony. It directed officials to develop a voluntary framework for government access to designated frontier models up to 30 days before release to trusted partners. The NSA director would determine which models qualified, and the order expressly excluded mandatory licensing or preclearance. Fable's pre-deployment checks therefore did not settle what officials would demand when a new concern arose after release.

Anthropic co-founder Tom Brown took over negotiations from Amodei. Politico describes a nearly two-hour call in which Lutnick explained that Commerce could not settle the matter alone: the National Cyber Director, Pentagon, NSA and CAISI all needed assurance about the safeguards. Anthropic sent researchers and engineers to Washington while colleagues supplied testing results from San Francisco and New York. Anthropic's June 30 account says a retrained safety classifier blocked the reported technique in more than 99% of cases and sent blocked requests to Opus 4.8, with more benign requests also caught. CAISI tested the old and new safeguards; Anthropic said its researchers judged both highly effective.

Politico reports that Deputy Defense Secretary Stephen Feinberg insisted on an independent evaluation and Brown met him personally. Mythos returned to approved US organizations on June 26. Commerce lifted Fable's restrictions on June 30, and it returned globally on July 1 after 19 days offline. CNBC saw Lutnick's letter permitting trusted-partner access to Mythos after safeguards were accepted. The different restoration dates distinguish the restricted research release from Fable’s public availability. Politico also reports that OpenAI submitted ChatGPT-5.6 for CAISI checks and accepted an initial release to trusted partners. Sam Altman’s late-June internal memo described that arrangement as one the company did not favor for the longer term.

The export-control mechanism had already drawn legal objections. In a June 16 CSIS analysis, Kate Koren, Kevin Kurland and Aalok Mehta said the unpublished letter reportedly invoked an emerging-technologies authority that had never been used to issue a control and lacked implementing regulations. They also described prior Commerce opinions excluding remote-access transactions from export regulation. Another provision reportedly cited in the letter allowed individually notified controls for a limited group of adversarial countries, raising a separate question about the worldwide restriction. Their analysis distinguished foreign employees’ access to technical information from customers using a model hosted on Anthropic’s servers. Joseph Hoefer's June 15 Tech Policy Press essay asked whether an API service exports model weights, computation or a capability. He distinguished restricting any sensitive capability from restricting only capabilities that materially exceed what adversaries can already obtain.

Anthropic argued that Amazon had not uncovered capabilities unavailable from weaker models: Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5 and Kimi K2.7 could find the same vulnerabilities, and every model it tested could reproduce the single exploit demonstration. Its safeguards deliberately blocked some probably benign tasks to provide a safety margin, it said. Anthropic proposed developing an industry framework with Amazon, Microsoft, Google and other partners to assess bypasses by the added capabilities, their breadth, ease of harmful use and discoverability. A June 14 letter with 76 signatures by the following morning similarly argued that other models already performed comparable security work. Katie Moussouris, quoted by The Verge, disputed that the Amazon paper described a jailbreak. Officials told Wired on June 17 that the NSA had concluded Fable's safeguards could be disabled and that neither agency evaluators nor the NSA could investigate every possible bypass. They wanted Anthropic to keep testing and reporting problems itself.

Politico ends with the August deadline for review guidelines passing without a public announcement; only leading companies had been briefed. The White House agreed to release related records under the Freedom of Information Act by October 30. The missing public framework remains part of the federal-oversight dispute we covered on September 17. The New York Times reported on September 18 that no single official coordinated AI policy and Brown remained in regular contact with officials. Politico describes renewed antagonism after the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident and Amodei's September 12 call to slow capability development. June's operational compromise restored access without resolving the standard for the next intervention.
