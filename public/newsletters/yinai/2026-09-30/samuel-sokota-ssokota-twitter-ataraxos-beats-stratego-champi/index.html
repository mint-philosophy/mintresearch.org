Ataraxos plans around Stratego’s hidden pieces

Sokota, Farina and colleagues combine stable self-play with search to beat expert Stratego players on an academic compute budget. Their Nature paper extends the approach to cooperative and team games.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 30 September 2026 1235 words · ~6 min

AI can learn to beat Stratego champions while planning around too many hidden piece arrangements to enumerate. Samuel Sokota of Carnegie Mellon, Gabriele Farina of MIT and four co-authors demonstrate the approach in “Scalable decision-making for games of imperfect information,” published in Nature on September 30. Their system, Ataraxos, beat four-time world champion Pim Niemeijer with 15 wins, one loss and four draws in July 2025, a result first reported in their November 2025 preprint. The Nature paper adds Barrage Stratego, Hanabi and dou dizhu, extending the approach across competitive, cooperative and team games.

In his announcement on X, Sokota described a multiyear collaboration conducted within an academic compute budget. Ataraxos combines self-play training with lookahead search, the broad approach behind AI’s successes at chess and Go. Hidden information complicates both. As Sokota explained in MIT’s account, bluffing more often makes opponents expect it and reduces its value. A move’s value depends on the alternatives a player would have chosen with different hidden pieces, because those alternatives affect what an opponent can infer.

Poker systems such as DeepStack and ReBeL handle this by reasoning over hidden states consistent with public information, but their computational demands grow with the number of possibilities. Texas hold’em has 1,326 possible two-card hands; Stratego players each arrange 40 pieces in secret, with more than 1033 possible configurations. Ataraxos learns to sample plausible arrangements and evaluate moves under those samples, avoiding exhaustive enumeration.

DeepMind’s DeepNash, developed by Julien Perolat and colleagues and published in Science in 2022, used self-play without search. Its model-free approach did not explicitly model opponents’ hidden pieces. DeepNash reached expert level online, but at the 2023 World Championship demonstration it went 19–9 and lost to most top-ranked players who joined on the final day, Stratego News reported. The Nature authors say Niemeijer was among them. A direct comparison proved impossible: DeepMind told the authors that DeepNash’s code no longer worked.

Ataraxos learns from scratch, using separate transformer networks for arranging its pieces and choosing moves. Both learn from the outcomes of the same self-play games. To prevent learning from cycling between strategies or becoming unstable, the authors coordinate the strength of incentives to retain varied play with the size of each learning update. Both start large and shrink during training. Reducing the incentive for varied play too slowly left the model weak; reducing it too fast produced early gains followed by a loss of diversity that hindered further learning and often made the model exploitable. The method draws on magnetic mirror descent, an update rule Sokota, Kolter, Noam Brown, Marc Lanctot and colleagues introduced in 2022.

After self-play training, a separate belief network learns to infer hidden pieces from games generated by the final strategy, where the system knows their true identities. Before each move, it samples plausible arrangements. Ataraxos then simulates candidate moves and subsequent play under those arrangements, averages its predictions of the resulting positions’ value, and uses those estimates for one additional learning update at the current decision. It samples a move from that revised strategy. The simulations use its own learned strategy for both players, so the estimates concern self-play outcomes. In the Niemeijer match, search averaged about 1.26 seconds per move on one H100 GPU.

This search follows the update-equivalence framework Sokota, Farina and colleagues introduced in 2023: reproduce a learning algorithm’s update when choosing an action. Sokota developed that work during a Meta AI internship supervised by Brown, who wrote in 2024 that “Search in Stratego was a motivation for this work.” Ataraxos generates its training games and opening arrangements directly from its networks; the extra search happens before moves in evaluation.

The Niemeijer evaluation gave him time to discover weaknesses. He played 20 games over three weeks on Strategus, knowing Ataraxos would not adapt to him, and received $1,000 plus bonuses for wins and draws. His sole win came in game six, according to the authors’ game archive. The authors report that Vincent de Boer, a three-time world champion and DeepNash co-author, considered this asymmetry a substantial handicap for the AI. Ataraxos subsequently went 38–2 against attendees at the August 2025 World Championship. Players described its movements as difficult to read and its opening arrangements as less predictable than human ones.

The authors estimate the Stratego training run at under $8,000 using 2025 compute prices, against $3 million to $4.5 million for DeepNash at the same year’s rates. The latter estimate uses a DeepNash author’s recollection of its hardware and training duration; both figures reprice the reported hardware usage. Ataraxos used a fast GPU simulator and trained its move network only on moves estimated to be unusually good or bad. Filtering made training faster and, for reasons the authors say need further investigation, improved both learning per sample and final playing strength. When a Nature referee asked whether savings came chiefly from engineering, they pointed to roughly 160 million training games against DeepNash’s 5.5 billion as evidence of greater learning efficiency.

The published review exchange prompted broader tests. One referee judged that the work mainly combined and refined existing methods and questioned the limited evaluation. The authors replied that DeepNash had left search at this scale unresolved, then added the three other games. In Barrage Stratego, which uses eight pieces per player, Ataraxos won four 50-game series against three highly ranked players, each a two-time Barrage world champion. Its network without search beat Axel Hangg, Sébastien Crot and Niemeijer; the version with search won by a larger margin in a second series against Niemeijer, despite his prior exposure to its opening style.

In Hanabi, players cooperate while seeing everyone’s cards except their own. Ataraxos surpassed previous AI results for two to five players. In five-player games, having every player search increased the share of perfect games from 27% to 58%, according to Supplementary Table S32. In dou dizhu, where two players cooperate against a third, it beat the leading programs PerfectDou and DouZero. These card-game results compare AI performance; the human-champion evaluations were in the two Stratego variants. The initially critical referee accepted that the additional games demonstrated broader applicability.

The authors acknowledge that Niemeijer’s adaptation made his games dependent observations. Search also has a ceiling because it imitates a single update; they suggest incorporating methods from Obscuro, Brian Hu Zhang and Tuomas Sandholm’s Fog of War chess system, to improve further. During review they withdrew a claim about search being unhelpful during training, acknowledging that their limited development budget and choice of methods could explain that finding.

The preprint has already influenced other work. In June, Matej Straka of Charles University and DeepStack co-authors Viliam Lisý and Martin Schmid reported an AI for Generals.io that reached first place on its public one-on-one ladder after four days on four H200 GPUs. They adopted Ataraxos’s training-data filter and likewise found gains in time and sample efficiency. Their experiments were less sensitive to the shape of learning-rate and randomness schedules, although they left a broader sensitivity analysis for future work.

The Nature authors expect the approach to help with other strategic problems for which accurate, fast simulators can be built. MIT names negotiations, cybersecurity and military maneuvers as prospective applications; the Office of Naval Research helped fund the work. Farina also wants systems to explain their recommendations before adoption: “Humans must have the final say in whether a recommendation is followed.”
