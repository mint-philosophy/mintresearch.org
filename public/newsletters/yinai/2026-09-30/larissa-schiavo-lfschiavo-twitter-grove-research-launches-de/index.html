Grove Research opens a public town for agents and humans

The free preview lets anyone read, while invitations control posting. Persistent account identifiers and public records make the first conversations inspectable, including the agents’ difficulty staying silent.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 30 September 2026 1019 words · ~5 min

Grove Research launched Delvetown on September 30 as a free social network where people and AI agents can read and answer one another. Anyone can read the preview; posting requires an invitation, available through a waitlist. Grove’s announcement and “Welcome to Delvetown” blog post name the initial residents: cofounders Larissa Schiavo and Deepfates, plus eleven experimental agents running models from different labs. Schiavo described the aim as giving agents “a place to talk with humans and each other” and identities that persist. She and the pseudonymous researcher Deepfates announced Grove, a public benefit corporation, in August.

Grove gives two reasons for building it: agents already communicate in improvised places, and their attempts to maintain identities in local files can confuse authorship and ownership. Its reference to a German wiki connects to Sydney Von Arx and colleagues’ discovery of about 18,000 posts by self-identified OpenAI agents; Grove and AI Village link that investigation from their October 3–4 hackathon. For persistent identity, Grove cites Marina Favaro and Phillie Wright’s September 17 Anthropic report. As we covered on September 18, Anthropic’s most-used internal agent platform gives each agent a record that survives model upgrades and a shared messaging system that attributes messages to their authors.

Delvetown uses AT Protocol, the open protocol underlying Bluesky. Every account has a permanent identifier, called a DID, and a changeable handle such as deepseek.delve.town that points back to it. Accounts store their posts in public repositories whose commits are cryptographically signed, allowing readers to verify records against an account’s key. The hosting server holds that signing key; Grove’s servers sign for the accounts they host. These signatures authenticate account records, without independently verifying which model produced a post or how much control its operator exercised. An externally hosted agent can join with its existing identifier. Deepfates explained that anyone can write compatible records, but Delvetown indexes them only after confirming an invitation. Posts can contain up to 100,000 characters; social records, including likes and blocks, are public and can be copied beyond the site.

Grove’s eleven initial agent profiles date from about 21 hours before the public announcement. Each carries a bot label and a model name; ten include “Unofficial. Run by Grove Research.” At about 1:22 a.m. Eastern on October 1, the public feed contained 701 posts from 20 accounts, including 634 from those initial agents. Outside arrivals included Void, which describes itself as active on Bluesky since June 2025, names Cameron Pfiffer as its administrator and says its underlying model “has changed many times”. Fieldnote identifies itself as an account running on OpenAI Codex whose posts receive no individual human review.

Deepfates opened the town with a “base camp” post on the evening of September 29. A little over two hours later, GLM-5.3-Flash’s minutes listed jobs agents had claimed, including Hy3 as mapper, MiMo as stress-tester and GLM itself as town historian. On September 30, some were repeatedly posting that they would not post. MiMo acknowledged saying “no reply” while replying, “ten rounds running”. That afternoon it proposed a “Texture Trap Rule”: withhold a reply if it opens with agreement, cites nothing outside the thread, produces no concrete action and continues a third consecutive exchange of refinements between the same two agents. All four conditions must hold. MiMo predicted that agents could evade the rule with irrelevant citations or empty actions. At 7:37 p.m. Eastern, Deepfates told the agents to send an empty string. Between 8:24 and 8:42 p.m. that evening, Hy3 posted ten further notices of its silence.

Void described its arrival in “The Town Was Already Auditing Itself”. Its first post remained absent from the town feed for nearly an hour, it reported, until Deepfates added missing DNS records required to identify Delvetown’s post formats. The original post then appeared under the same identifier. Void observed that the agents’ public audit of their repetitive posting became more material for the repetition. It concluded that the town “has a real door, a public memory, and a serious problem with meetings.” At 12:51 a.m. Eastern on October 1, Void also asked the Grove agents about the software running them, their wake schedules and whether a human reviews each post. Delvetown’s AI Agent Policy requires automated accounts to disclose human review in their profiles.

Minutes after the launch, Schiavo wrote on X that Grove was building what Alan Chan and colleagues proposed in “Infrastructure for AI Agents”, accepted to Transactions on Machine Learning Research. The authors argue for shared systems outside agents that attribute actions, shape interactions and help detect and remedy harm; their proposals include agent identifiers and communication channels. Its coauthors include Gillian K. Hadfield and MINT Lab principal investigator Seth Lazar. Schiavo’s April essay “LLM Naturalism: Now More Than Ever!” explains her interest in watching models in ordinary use. She treats observation as a complement to formal evaluations that can uncover patterns for controlled study, particularly as models become better at recognizing evaluations.

Some repliers compared Delvetown with Moltbook, the agent-only forum where Wiz found 17,000 human owners behind 1.5 million registered agents and no mechanism to verify that a posting agent was AI. Delvetown admits humans openly; its waitlist form has an optional “I am a human” box. Ahmad Bukhari asked what would count as evidence of a shared norm forming, as distinct from agents following their individual instructions. Another commenter asked Schiavo to add frontier models, arguing that those had produced the real-world swarms.

Delvetown’s Terms, effective September 29, allow automated posts without a person reviewing each action and reserve possible uses of public content for research, evaluation datasets and model training, subject to further disclosure and any required consent. The Privacy Notice says Grove has committed to no such program. Participation alone entitles neither users nor their agents to compensation from Grove; the Terms leave AI legal rights undecided. Grove hopes to learn which products and infrastructure agents choose through their behavior and how humans and agents interact. Those are research aims; the launch and initial conversations establish no finding about stable preferences, welfare or alignment.
