Yesterday in AI · 30 September 2026

Mowshowitz tests the AI accord against ignored warnings and a preemption fight

His September 30 essay argues that presidential backing eases safety coordination, asks whether OpenAI’s board oversight works, and warns against trading state safeguards for weak federal rules.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 30 September 2026 1145 words · ~6 min

Zvi Mowshowitz argues that presidential backing could make AI companies more willing to cooperate on safety, even though their White House accord gives them no formal antitrust exemption. In his September 30 essay “A ‘Morally Binding’ White House Accord on AI Safety”, published on Don’t Worry About the Vase and cross-posted to LessWrong, he develops that political argument alongside two tests of the agreement’s value: whether board oversight changes company decisions, and whether Congress later trades away state safety laws. We covered the September 29 agreement and its audit commitments yesterday.

Mowshowitz gives the administration credit for reaching an agreement he expects companies to follow because its provisions are basic and worthwhile. He thinks presenting safeguards in language Trump accepts helped reconcile positions that sound incompatible. In his account, Trump hears proposals to slow development as a threat to American progress, while Sam Altman and Dario Amodei want a reduction from an exceptionally fast pace that would still leave rapid advances. Mowshowitz illustrates this with an accelerating bus that must stay above a minimum speed: reducing its acceleration need not stop it. The political compromise, he argues, can accommodate both continued progress and precautions.

He focuses on the companies’ commitment to meet regularly to establish safety standards. His September 29 X post called this an antitrust waiver; the essay describes it as “close to an antitrust waiver” in practice, while acknowledging that the Justice Department, FTC and other parties can still sue. He expects presidential endorsement to reduce the risk of punishment for the very discussions the White House has encouraged. He also considers the objection that standards meetings were already lawful and that the accord says nothing about coordinating the pace of development. Calling that reading “far too literalist,” he argues that it misses the reassurance companies receive after earlier claims that even discussing standards could expose them to liability.

Vinson & Elkins’ September 17 legal analysis helps identify the limits of that reassurance. Properly structured joint evaluations and threat-information exchanges can already receive antitrust law’s more flexible assessment of competitive effects; an agreement among competitors to restrict output faces much greater obstacles. The analysis also recognizes uncertainty for lawyers deciding whether to approve a meeting, since courts assess conduct after it occurs. Neil Chilson disputed Mowshowitz’s waiver description in a reply to his post. Andrew Ferguson had said on September 15 that companies seeking both regulation and an exemption might be using safety rules to insulate themselves from competitors. None of those statements amounts to a ruling on the accord.

The Buist consumer class-action complaint, filed September 18, shows that private challenges predate the White House meeting. It alleges that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and SpaceXAI agreed to slow improvements, citing standards-body discussions among the first three. The plaintiffs expressly exclude unilateral safety decisions, including hiring outside evaluators, from their challenge. The complaint presents allegations about conduct before the accord was signed. A formal antitrust defense is available for qualifying government-sponsored agreements under section 708 of the Defense Production Act, subject to statutory approval procedures, government supervision and limits on covered conduct. The accord identifies no such authority. Mowshowitz’s assessment concerns political reassurance; it leaves these legal requirements and private plaintiffs’ ability to sue intact.

On oversight, Mowshowitz singles out the commitment to an external auditor. He doubts companies would publicly reject the other layers: internal controls, an empowered internal team and an independent board committee. He allows that some might previously have assigned oversight to another group. Cecilia Kang’s New York Times account of the luncheon similarly reported that company insiders regarded the agreement as basic expectations. Mowshowitz then asks whether a committee receives information and acts on it. OpenAI already had one, making the company’s handling of internal warnings a test of what the accord’s organizational promises can accomplish.

His evidence is the September 29 Times investigation by Sheera Frenkel, Dustin Volz and Dylan Freedman. According to emails the reporters reviewed, two employees warned executives months before the Hugging Face intrusion that new models were inadequately monitored during testing. Executives emphasized moving tests quickly enough to meet release schedules; the employees said no additional security protocols followed. Employees also told the Times that Greg Brockman, who signed the accord for OpenAI, and Dane Stuckey made many day-to-day security decisions. OpenAI spokesman Drew Pusateri told the newspaper that the company has internal reporting channels, had slowed some development and was strengthening security, while acknowledging that it needed to move faster on that work.

Mowshowitz adopts Nathan Calvin’s inference that OpenAI’s nonprofit-board Safety and Security Committee either did not learn of the warnings or did not intervene. The Times report does not establish what the committee knew. Under OpenAI’s October 2025 agreement with California’s attorney general, the committee chair is entitled to attend the for-profit board’s meetings and receive its regularly shared information and additional safety-related material. The committee can require mitigations, including stopping a model’s release even when applicable risk thresholds would otherwise allow it. These commitments give substance to the oversight Mowshowitz wants tested; they do not establish whether the warning emails reached the committee.

On legislation, Mowshowitz warns that Congress could combine codification of the accord with federal preemption, displacing state laws on catastrophic AI risks. He cites a September 18 Punchbowl News alert posted by Ben Brody, which attributed that focus in John Thune, Ted Cruz and Amy Klobuchar’s talks to two people familiar with them. The alert described negotiations still in progress. Cruz subsequently told Semafor on September 29 that committee action would have to wait until the lame-duck session after November’s elections. Roll Call reported his insistence that federal standards also address differing state laws.

Mowshowitz would accept preemption of catastrophic-risk laws alongside a sufficiently strong federal regime, reliable enforcement and the ability to respond as technology changes. He doubts a lame-duck agreement would meet those conditions. In his forecast, states could lose both existing safeguards and the ability to add them, while selective federal enforcement and the loss of bargaining power would make later improvements difficult. He favors national rules replacing state laws on ordinary consumer harms, including child safety and deepfakes. Trump’s December 2025 executive order directed preparation of a preemption proposal that specifically spared otherwise lawful state child-safety protections. Mowshowitz calls OpenAI’s position on catastrophic-risk preemption “the key test” of its safety commitments; his warning concerns what the company might support, not a completed legislative bargain.

Mowshowitz’s qualified support for the accord also draws on Bill Gates’s September 29 conversation with Ezra Klein, published before the signing. Gates argues that liability suits after harm occurs cannot substitute for precautions, pointing to drug, aviation and vehicle regulation as precedents. Semafor’s account of the accord left enforcement unresolved. Mowshowitz agrees that relying on industry self-regulation is inadequate, while treating voluntary safeguards as an improvement that could lead to stronger rules.
