New York freezes new hyperscale data centers, and few were coming

Hochul's July 14 executive order pauses state permits for data centers drawing 50 megawatts or more, the country's first statewide data-center moratorium; Heatmap data shows three of eight recently proposed New York sites already canceled and the likeliest host towns closed to development.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 15 July 2026 423 words · ~2 min

Seventeen news days into the data-center energy debate, tracked here since June 29, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order on July 14 pausing permits for the largest new data centers, the country's first such statewide moratorium. The order stops state environmental permits for a year for "hyperscale" facilities, defined as those consuming 50 megawatts or more, while the Department of Public Service drafts a Generic Environmental Impact Statement setting standards for energy demand, water use and quality, and air quality. New York would "lead the way in creating the strongest standards in the nation," Hochul said.

The order touches few projects, going by Heatmap Pro data in Robinson Meyer's July 14 Heatmap Daily newsletter. Of eight large-scale data centers recently proposed in the state, three have been canceled and one, approved last year, is exempt; an upper Hudson Valley campus that could have covered a million acres and drawn up to 1,000 megawatts was scrapped last month after the town passed its own moratorium. Eight New York municipalities have banned data center development outright and three have restricted it, Meyer wrote, so most of the places where a project would pencil out have already closed the door.

The legislature had gone further. Last month it passed the Responsible Data Center Development Act, sponsored by Senator Kristen Gonzalez, which would freeze approvals for centers above 20 megawatts for a year; amNewYork reports Hochul has not committed to signing it, and her 50-megawatt line exempts more projects. Gonzalez called the order similar to her bill while noting its higher threshold, and a separate measure proposing a three-year freeze remains in committee. Meyer read the executive order as a way around the legislation and tied its timing to Hochul's re-election campaign this fall, likening it to her June 2024 suspension of New York City congestion pricing, which she revived after that year's election and let take effect in 2025.

Alongside the review, Hochul's office said, the state will run an Energize NY proceeding requiring data centers to supply their own power or pay more for energy, issue guidance on "community benefits" deals covering local infrastructure, childcare, and hiring, and pursue legislation to repeal sales-tax exemptions for the biggest facilities. Maine's legislature passed a comparable measure that its governor vetoed, TechCrunch notes, so New York's is the first to take hold. Meyer doubts the pause outlasts its initial 12 months, given how many towns and cities have already passed their own restrictions; whether other Democratic-run states follow, he writes, is another question.
