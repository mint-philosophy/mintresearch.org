Albanese establishes an Office of AI and proposes mandatory national standards

The prime minister used a University of Sydney speech to create an Office of AI, effective immediately, and to propose mandatory standards that would make large data centres net generators for the power grid, fund their own water infrastructure, and give artists control over whether AI trains on Australian books, music, art or news.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 15 July 2026 491 words · ~2 min

In a speech at the University of Sydney on 15 July, “AI in Australia’s interests,” Prime Minister Anthony Albanese established an Office of AI inside his own Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, effective that day, to pull together a response he said had until now run “issue-by-issue, sector by sector.” The office will coordinate the design of a new set of Australian Standards for AI, working with Industry and Innovation Minister Tim Ayres and Andrew Charlton, the assistant minister for science, technology and the digital economy. The standards fold the data-centre expectations announced in March into one regulatory framework Albanese described as “clear, consistent and mandatory.” Australia is serious about attracting frontier AI investment, he said, because it wants AI to “support and create good jobs, not replace them.”

Albanese reserved the heaviest obligations for large data centres. The government will create a legal obligation for the next generation of large-scale centres to underwrite new power supply, to pay their full share of grid connection so no costs are passed on to homes or businesses, and to put “at least as much energy into our grid as they take out of it,” building new renewable generation and firming to become net-generators. The rules will also require centres to minimise their water use, maximise their energy efficiency, and pay for any additional water infrastructure required. Australia, he added, has more than enough room for new centres without them competing with new housing.

On copyright, Albanese said “not everything produced in Australia is up for grabs”: Australian writers, musicians, artists and journalists must retain ownership and control of their work, and the law will spell that out. No company should use Australian books, music, art or news to build or train AI without the artist’s control, he argued, including control of the price and value of their work. “An artist’s creative endeavour is their work and their property,” he said. The Attorney-General has been facilitating consultation on copyright and artist protections where AI training is involved.

Albanese presented the standards as an approach still to be agreed. He will seek agreement from premiers and chief ministers at a National Cabinet he is convening next month, and the government aims to bring legislation to Parliament in early 2027, consulting with industry and trading partners on a framework for faster decision-making, better supporting infrastructure and genuine community engagement. He pointed to work under way across the cabinet: the climate and energy minister with state and territory counterparts and energy market bodies, the Treasurer on AI in the productivity agenda, the employment minister with employers, workers and unions on the workplace, the education minister on AI in schools, the communications minister on a digital duty of care, and the deputy prime minister and home affairs minister with their agencies and Five Eyes partners on national security. He framed the package as a bid to boost sovereign capability, strengthen national security and build economic resilience.
