Australia establishes an Office of AI and sets out binding data-centre rules

Albanese established an Office of AI inside his own department and promised what the government calls the first national AI framework any country will legislate; the binding obligations land on data centres' power and water, and critics on the left and right dispute the design.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 15 July 2026 485 words · ~2 min

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese's July 15 statement AI in Australia's interests set up an Office of AI inside the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, effective that day, and laid out what the government calls the first national AI framework any country will legislate. The office will drive a set of Australian Standards for AI, built on the existing Data Centre Expectations; National Cabinet takes them up in August, with legislation expected early next year. Albanese cast the standards as a way to shape "the future rather than letting the future of AI shape us." Senator Tim Ayres, Minister for Industry, Innovation and Science, and Andrew Charlton, Assistant Minister for Science, Technology and the Digital Economy, joined the announcement; Charlton called "a clear and enforceable social licence for AI" fundamental to the plan.

The release puts the binding obligations on large data centres. Operators must underwrite their own new power supply, pay their full share of connection costs so household bills are not affected, cut power when the grid needs it, and use water as efficiently as possible. Canberra will work with states and territories on where the centres are built, with input from local communities. The government says the regime will speed approvals and cover data centres and AI training alike, and that clearer rules will strengthen Australia's sovereignty and draw investment; Forbes Australia reported the pitch as making the country more than a data warehouse for products made overseas.

On copyright, the release promised the strongest possible protection for Australian artists and media, with writers, artists and journalists keeping ownership of their work and no company able to train AI on it without the creator's control. SBS News reported that the pledge answered fears the laws might be loosened, after the AI company Anthropic lobbied for copyright clarity in exchange for a $21.6 billion investment in Australia. The Media, Entertainment and Arts Alliance and the rights bodies APRA and AMCOS welcomed the commitment, though the detail is yet to come. Albanese said consumer-safety priorities would follow within weeks, building on the recently established AI Safety Institute.

Reaction reported by SBS News broke both ways. Greens senator David Shoebridge faulted the office for lacking statutory powers, calling it "a single door in his office" that would not deliver the protections people had asked for. Opposition Leader Angus Taylor attacked from the other direction, warning the office meant "more bureaucracy" that would slow adoption. Greenpeace's Joe Rafalowicz, pressing for a moratorium on approvals until binding energy and water rules exist, said Albanese was rolling out the red carpet for "water-guzzling energy vampires" left unregulated until at least 2027. Digital Rights Watch chair Lizzie O'Shea backed the office but warned that fast-tracked data centres would crowd out renewable projects and housing. Johanna Weaver of the Tech Policy Design Institute said the plan must be "fast followed with hard decisions" on copyright, jobs and the environment.
