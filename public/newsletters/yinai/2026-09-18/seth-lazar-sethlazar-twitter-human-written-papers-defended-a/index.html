Lazar argues that outsourcing prose forfeits scholarly credit

He links recognition to writing one's own prose and argues that checking mixed human and AI contributions costs the community too much. Replies challenge his distinction between prose and code, question the burden on less fluent writers, and propose testing understanding or documenting contributions.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 18 September 2026 1380 words · ~7 min

On X on 18 September, Seth Lazar argued that researchers who let AI write their papers surrender their claim to scholarly credit, even when they originated the ideas. The Johns Hopkins professor set out the advice he gives researchers in his lab: use AI during research, but write the text submitted for others to read. He had previously defended Philosophy & Public Affairs' prohibition on substantially AI-written papers, adopted after its experiment publishing Simon Goldstein's largely Claude-written article.

Lazar begins with the claim that originating an idea makes someone accountable for the resulting work regardless of who produced its wording. He rejects that claim because “Ownership is relational”: an author's entitlement depends on others recognising it. Once AI produces the prose, he argues, readers lose their reason to trust the author's assertion that the ideas belong to them. Reviewers must identify both worthwhile ideas and researchers who deserve credit for them; he says authors who relinquish ownership should not seek that credit or consume reviewers' time.

On limited editing, Lazar warns that authors can misdescribe substantial rewriting as minor assistance, including revisions that change every other word. He regards detection software as the community's main available defence against mass-produced submissions. Authors whose AI use approaches the detection threshold and who then dispute a flag, he argues, make it harder to identify people submitting machine output they neither understand nor own. His instruction is to leave a wide margin. He also rejects elaborate systems for dividing credit between human and machine: honest users' convenience would require the community to pay for distinguishing them from dishonest users. Writing can be learned, he says, and publishing in a second language does not justify that collective expense; ordinary scientific prose is sufficient.

Lazar allows AI use in research, including experiments and assistance developing ideas. He describes writing the paper personally as a costly signal: the author must put in effort that gives readers evidence of understanding, intellectual contribution and willingness to answer for the text. He accepts that someone can write a paper while having little mastery or ownership of its content, and treats that weakness as tolerable. The rule is meant to preserve a workable way to recognise researchers' contributions even though it cannot guarantee that every human-written submission meets that standard. He closes by urging people who want AI to write for them to establish their own journals or conference tracks and disclose the practice.

Lazar had argued in June that saving authors' writing time shifts costs onto reviewers. That month, the NeurIPS position-paper track he co-chairs reported rejecting 178 of 969 submissions before review for apparent violations of its AI-writing policy. Its chairs argued that the community should not bear the expense of ever finer distinctions among borderline cases; they also asked authors of another 123 submissions for histories documenting their drafts and AI edits. Philosophy & Public Affairs, where Lazar is an associate editor, adopted its prohibition in August. Our coverage of the ensuing debate described Benjamin Bratton's case for experimenting with AI writing and Lazar's reply to Carlo Ludovico Cordasco that writing signals an author's belief in the work.

Lazar's emphasis on recognition has a historical parallel in Robert K. Merton's Priorities in Scientific Discovery: A Chapter in the Sociology of Science, published in the American Sociological Review in 1957. Merton described scientists as surrendering exclusive control over published discoveries while retaining a claim to recognition for making them. Michael Spence's Job Market Signaling, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 1973, explains how education can distinguish applicants when acquiring it costs less for more productive people. Lazar similarly treats the effort of writing as evidence of qualities readers cannot directly observe.

Nihar B. Shah, an editor-in-chief of Transactions on Machine Learning Research, described a practical test of authors' understanding conducted during his August rotation. He invited authors of ten submissions already headed for rejection to discuss their papers and held meetings about seven papers. In three meetings the authors could not answer basic questions; in three they understood the broad ideas but struggled with technical details; in one they answered everything, although Shah found a major flaw in that paper. He questioned whether authors unable to explain their work could have verified it. The exercise took 20–25 hours over two weeks, illustrating the labour involved. The papers were selected from likely rejections, so the results describe that group.

On 17 September, Justin Payan, Bálint Gyevnár, Atoosa Kasirzadeh and Shah at Carnegie Mellon posted the arXiv preprint greCAPTCHA: Assessing Understanding as Evidence of Research Authorship Under Generative AI. It proposes proctored questions generated from a manuscript and the author's stated contributions, asking participants to explain choices, identify errors and assess limitations. The authors treat understanding as a prerequisite for verification; demonstrating it does not establish that the author actually verified the work or that the paper is correct. In a study with 31 researchers, scores distinguished answers about participants' own papers from answers about unfamiliar papers with an AUC of 0.90, a ranking measure where 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect. Participants identified grading and question-design problems requiring changes before deployment. Kasirzadeh urged Lazar to experiment with understanding tests, arguing that bans would not scale and AI helps some researchers. He answered that improving detectors and later disqualification make bans scalable, and judged the benefits of AI writing too small to justify alternative enforcement costs.

Peter Henderson asked whether Lazar's reasoning also applies to research code. Lazar offered a tentative distinction: AI-written code was not flooding the intellectual commons as prose was; code usually supported the contribution; and models often wrote code better than humans, enabling otherwise unavailable research. He said comparable intellectual gains from AI prose were possible in principle but were not occurring. He accepted reasons to avoid techniques one cannot understand or verify and explicitly said he was unsure the broader argument succeeded. Hiram Ring objected that writing itself can develop ideas. Lazar clarified that developing ideas in conversation with an LLM was permissible if the researcher then wrote the paper. Ring acknowledged the clarification while questioning the ambiguity of writing with AI; Lazar restated his rule: “don't ask other people to read words that you didn't write”.

The journal Una Mens: Homo et Machina replied that documenting provenance could address Lazar's concerns. Its policy for a second issue planned for December separates human authors, who retain responsibility, from an additional line naming AI systems, versions and contributions. Its first issue contains five papers written by founding editor Michael J. Miller with AI systems, without external peer review. Panos Ipeirotis accused Lazar of protecting a profession's control over credit at the expense of advancing knowledge. Anil Menon argued that the rule advantages already fluent writers and asked how improving other researchers' prose harms the community.

When Dirk, another participant, asked Lazar to specify the collective harm and justify a ban over disclosure, Lazar pointed to the volume of low-quality submissions. Permitting good AI writing, he argued, makes excluding bad AI writing prohibitively difficult; even detecting superficially sophisticated but incoherent work takes time. He added that disclosure rules also impose checking costs. He saw no compensating collective benefit when more low-quality submissions impede research, and judged a ban the best available response while accepting that other responses were possible.

Detector accuracy affects who bears those costs. In the 2023 Patterns paper GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers, Stanford's Weixin Liang and colleagues tested seven detectors on student essays. They misclassified 61% of 91 human-written TOEFL essays as AI-generated on average, while performing well on US eighth-grade essays. Those systems differ from Pangram, whose separate audits the NeurIPS chairs cited as reporting a false-positive rate below 0.1%. The figures describe different detectors and tests.

NISO's CRediT taxonomy records 14 kinds of research contribution, including drafting and editing; Una Mens uses similar labels for model contributions. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors recommends disclosing AI writing assistance in acknowledgements while holding human authors responsible. These rules specify contributions and obligations. Lazar disputes whether checking compliance is a cost scholarly communities should accept when they could require authors to write the prose themselves.
