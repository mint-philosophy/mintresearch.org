Anthropic retires its Constitution-alignment roadmap goal

The October 19 update retains constitutional assessment and a 90-day publication commitment, but ends a routine data review and acknowledges less testing of alignment arguments than planned. Anthropic also reports 23 wins and one tie for automated attacks against biological-weapons classifiers.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 October 2026 751 words · ~4 min

Anthropic retired its “Upholding Claude’s Constitution” roadmap goal on October 19, after its October 1 target date. The company says it will reconsider which goals best support alignment and publish its thinking later. Its Frontier Safety Roadmap now has a retirement note under Alignment, with no replacement goal there. Anthropic continues to assess constitutional alignment and plans to keep the public Constitution synchronized with the version used in training.

The retired goal combined oversight of training material with assessments of model behavior. Anthropic had promised to review representative samples of production-relevant post-training data and rewards for inconsistencies with the Constitution. It also planned to incorporate interpretability into behavioral assessments and thoroughly test its alignment arguments, including by evaluating intentionally misaligned test models. Those assessments would cover significantly more capable public models and, within a reasonable period after deployment, internal models presenting notable additional risks. The company would report findings in system cards or Risk Reports.

Anthropic’s retirement update records different outcomes for those activities. It says constitutional alignment remains a core metric in automated assessments and is reported for every model assessed, while interpretability evidence appears in all recent system cards. The company tested its alignment arguments with deliberately misaligned models less extensively than originally intended. It also stopped routinely reviewing samples of post-training data and rewards because the process found nothing beyond existing checks; a recent repeat on sampled training data again found nothing. Anthropic explicitly retains the prospective plan to update the public Constitution within 90 days of relevant internal or external deployments of models trained on an updated version. That clock dates from deployment, following a July wording change from the original reference to internal updates.

Anthropic describes the roadmap as a public means of coordinating work across departments. Its overview allows goals to change as the company learns, while promising to strive against lowering ambitions simply because it cannot execute. The Constitution goal’s retirement follows an earlier decision in May to complete a data-retention analysis without adopting a successor dated goal; Anthropic said other internal processes would drive that work. The roadmap’s longer-term expectations still include systematic alignment audits by the time AI can automate or dramatically accelerate leading research teams’ work, which the company considers plausible as early as 2027.

In the same October 19 update, Anthropic marked its internal red-teaming goal complete as of October 6, ahead of the January 1, 2027 target. Its automated attack search was compared with human bug-bounty submissions collected between April 20 and September 11. The comparison used eight biological-weapons questions across three generations of production safety classifiers, giving 24 question-classifier pairs. Anthropic reports that automation beat the best human submission in 23 pairs and tied in the remaining pair, taking 24–48 hours against weeks or months of bounty time. These comparisons concern attacks on the specified biological-weapons safeguards.

Anthropic’s August Risk Report explains how the human bounty worked. Approved researchers signed nondisclosure agreements and tested a fixed set of eight harmful biological questions using accounts on which Anthropic could monitor and train on their attempts. The company assessed how informative the resulting answers were and offered up to $35,000 for a novel universal jailbreak. For the earlier period covered by that report, submissions represented about 600 self-reported hours, excluding researchers who submitted nothing. That total does not measure the full April–September comparison window. The August report also described internal testing of the biological classifiers as insufficiently systematic and resourced to establish that it had met the roadmap goal.

The original goal was to develop an internal method matching or exceeding the collective bug-bounty participants’ ability to find jailbreaks, assessed through the aggregate substance of the findings. Anthropic now judges its automated results to meet that criterion. The October update reports the per-question comparisons and promises a fuller blog post; it does not describe the automated method or its compute costs. METR’s October 20 review separately recommends sustained testing of monitors for harmful actions by models working inside Anthropic.

Anthropic also added two policy goals. A proposal due December 15 will address government support for embedded independent evaluators, including their funding, access and independence. It builds on the October 15 announcement giving six METR staff internal access comparable to Anthropic’s risk-assessment teams. A second goal, due January 15, concerns policies targeting the pace of AI development when automated research accelerates capability gains faster than safeguards or governance can keep up. For that goal, Anthropic commits to publishing either a proposal or an explanation of why it decided against one.
