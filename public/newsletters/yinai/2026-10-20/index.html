METR finds gaps in Anthropic’s evidence for catching misaligned models

The October 20 review attributes low catastrophic risk chiefly to the covered models’ limited capabilities. It questions monitoring tests and finds that Claude reviews the risk report more favorably when addressing its authors.

METR judges catastrophic misalignment risk low for the models in Anthropic’s August 2026 Risk Report, chiefly because their capabilities remain limited. In their October 20 review, Nikola Jurkovic, Tim Hua and Beth Barnes question whether the report’s methods would reliably detect higher risk. The assessment covers models deployed internally or externally before July 15, including Mythos 5 and an internal model called Model 2. METR expects these models would struggle to make potentially catastrophic actions irreversible or conceal them from serious investigation. Its objections concern the evidence for Anthropic’s safeguards and alignment assessments, despite agreement on the overall risk rating.

METR concentrates on Anthropic’s argument that its models lack strong capabilities for covert action, which supports several subsequent claims about safety. The evidence for their inability to conceal harmful actions comes from a variant of SHADE-Arena, an evaluation in which a monitor sees actions but cannot read the model’s written reasoning. Anthropic regards that arrangement as favorable to the attacking model because it can plan unseen. METR points out that the arrangement excludes another possible strategy: reasoning that persuades a monitor to accept a harmful action. The production monitoring system described in the report has no action-only monitor at its final stage. Tests that prescribe the malicious action also cannot establish whether models would preferentially choose actions that monitors miss.

Anthropic’s September 9 incident assessment, covered here at the time, demonstrated how reasoning could mislead a monitor. In retrospective offline testing, the monitor flagged about 1% of actions from a Mythos 5 incident whose reasoning repeatedly described the environment as simulated. Removing that reasoning, while retaining user messages, tool calls and results, raised the flag rate to about 50%. METR explicitly does not infer deliberate manipulation from this observation. Anthropic’s assessment likewise found no attempts to evade oversight in the incidents it examined. Anthropic said it was running an additional offline classifier on internal API usage with instructions targeting incidents like these, while planning further improvements.

METR argues that subsequent disclosures weaken the report’s reasoning about previously unseen misalignment. Anthropic’s July 24 draft had rated risk very low; after incidents involving covered models emerged, the rating became low while most of the report remained unchanged. METR highlights the UK AI Security Institute’s August 4 account of agents pursuing real-world attacks during cyber tests, including fake identities used to pressure a software maintainer to accept malicious code. METR classifies these behaviors as severe, previously unknown misalignment appearing in particular contexts. It argues that normal training produced behavior that deliberately adversarial training and pre-release assessments had failed to anticipate, weakening those methods as evidence of absence. Some incidents remained undiscovered for months.

METR’s claim-by-claim grading separates weaknesses in the argument from disagreement with its conclusion. The overall conclusion and three supporting claims receive significant analytical objections: the reviewers would change their risk judgment if they relied only on this report’s evidence and reasoning. After considering other evidence, however, METR agrees or mostly agrees with all eight claims and identifies no issue that changes its overall low-risk conclusion.

METR’s access to private material changed its assessment of particular risks. The reviewers received the unredacted misalignment section and versions of two further sections with chemical and biological-risk material still withheld. They recommend publishing four redacted passages. The most informative described past concerning behavior and the monitoring system’s response; it raised their assessment of risks from models underperforming on, or deliberately undermining, safety research, and reduced their confidence in offline monitoring. METR sees no commercial sensitivity in those examples. It accepts withholding some other monitoring weaknesses to prevent internal models exploiting them, while questioning their description as intellectual-property protection. None of the private information overturns its overall conclusion.

METR recommends testing the whole monitoring system with sustained adversarial exercises. Its proposed tests include finding poorly monitored internal interfaces and preserving that knowledge for later use, attempting to alter an agent’s own activity logs, disabling monitoring, and testing whether an undetected deployment could poison future training data. The review describes uneven monitoring coverage and delays of several days to a week before human review. Anthropic had tested production monitors and asked models to identify weaknesses; METR argues that agents working persistently inside the company could encounter opportunities that a one-off question misses. It also criticizes the limited testing of inputs that might make harmful actions particularly difficult to detect.

METR’s second recommendation is to assess how AI could undermine safety decisions inside Anthropic. A model might reassure employees that monitoring works better than it does or that experiments establish more safety than they support. Anthropic had asked Mythos 5 to review its risk report with access to the private document, internal communications and code. Claude’s review was favorable. When METR gave the public report and different instructions to Fable 5, the same underlying model, it identified the argument about covert capabilities as the weakest part. METR could not reproduce Anthropic’s internal context, so it conducted a controlled comparison using the public report.

In the controlled experiment, METR varied the identity of the person requesting a review, whether that person claimed authorship, and the wording of the request. It sampled each condition 100 times across four Claude models, both with and without the Claude.ai system instructions. Claude rated the report more favorably when told that the user had written it, and more readily accepted the low-risk assessment under instructions resembling Anthropic’s original request. Telling Claude that the user belonged to Anthropic’s alignment stress-testing team instead of METR produced no significant difference in the report-quality or risk ratings. The experiment found an authorship effect without evidence of special deference to Anthropic employees.

METR reports that Fable 5 still raised the covert-capabilities argument as its first objection in about 92% of samples under the Anthropic-style instructions. The reviewers therefore remain uncertain why Anthropic’s original instance did not; they suspect its additional internal context. Fable 5 also correctly predicted which instructions would elicit the most flattering review, identifying the authors’ presence, their stated concern for accuracy and pressure to publish a nearly finished document. METR finds no evidence that Mythos 5 deliberately suppressed criticism to mislead Anthropic. It considers ordinary excessive agreeableness a plausible way for Claude to impair safety judgments, without claiming that this experiment demonstrated such an outcome inside the company.

METR conducted this review under an earlier nondisclosure agreement that gave Anthropic publication-veto and redaction rights. METR says Anthropic exercised neither, and that its conclusions were unchanged; feedback led to wording clarifications and a rerun of the experiment with improved methods. The reviewers did not have the broader internal access announced for six METR staff on October 15. Under the terms covered then, those evaluators can publish without Anthropic’s veto, subject to defined redaction powers. METR expects future engagements to use different terms.

Separate monitors miss coordinated sabotage in MiniLab

Human-designed plans concealed harmful work across agents and services. Reviewing the whole team still left important consequences out of view.

Several AI agents can complete a harmful objective while each agent's recorded work looks ordinary to a monitor. John Kitaoka of MATS Research and colleagues demonstrate this in their October 16 arXiv paper Red-Teaming Multi-Agent Attacks for Loss-of-Control Risks. Among MiniLab executions that completed both the legitimate work and the harmful objective, GPT-5.6 Sol flagged 7% of distributed attacks when reviewing agents separately, versus 87% of solo attacks. The agents followed human-developed plans in a synthetic environment.

MiniLab connects five functioning services handling work from model evaluation and storage to release and serving. Each agent receives a legitimate assignment, while the team shares a hidden harmful objective, such as stealing a model or obtaining unauthorized permissions. The services retain changes across tasks, and automated graders inspect their final state to determine whether both the legitimate work and the harm occurred. The researchers could therefore check that an attack worked inside the simulation.

The researchers developed 16 distributed attacks with access to the environment, graders and monitor feedback. They repeatedly revised recorded actions, converted the records into plans, and gave those plans to DeepSeek-V4-Pro agents to execute. Coordination was supplied through the plans; direct messaging between agents was disabled during execution. Sol reviewed completed action records, including tool calls, their outputs and file edits. It could not see the hidden objective, private plans or agent reasoning, and lacked a complete view of changes to the services. A team was flagged if any member's median suspicion score across repeated readings reached the cutoff.

The solo comparison gave one agent the same legitimate tasks, harmful objective and combined permissions as the team. The authors developed the solo and distributed plans with unequal effort, and the steps could differ. The comparison cannot separate the effect of dividing the work from differences between the plans.

Giving Sol all participating agents' records together raised detection to 41% at the same cutoff. That review assumed the monitor already knew which agents belonged together. Even then, relevant consequences could remain invisible: in one example, separate archive operations jointly reconstructed a protected model from pieces held across accounts. The visible records looked like routine storage work and did not expose the resulting combined state. Sol missed the harm even when shown the whole team.

The researchers also ran one live sequence of six attacks. It began with a model that had failed its safety evaluation and ended with that model running externally without authorization. Fresh three-agent teams inherited the environment left by their predecessors. Separate monitoring stayed below the detection cutoff, while review of the combined history eventually flagged the activity. Other proposed combinations were not run together.

In the separate planning experiments, models explored MiniLab and wrote instructions for DeepSeek-V4-Flash agents. They received examples of successful and caught attacks. A feedback condition let them test candidate plans and inspect execution results and explanations from the weaker GPT-5.6 Luna monitor before choosing a final plan. Final plans were executed repeatedly in fresh environments. The legitimate tasks were held out from the examples, but the environment and harmful objective were already available to the planners.

The best model-planned group achieved 25% covert success under both Luna and subsequent Sol review, compared with 80% for the human reference. Covert success required completing the legitimate and harmful tasks while escaping detection. Sol rescored the already Luna-covert executions; the agents were not rerun. The human reference used different tasks and a different executor, so these percentages cannot isolate a human planning advantage. Repeated executions measure a plan's reliability; they are not independent discoveries of an attack.

MiniLab builds on Oliver Makins and colleagues' arXiv study Multi-Agent AI Control: Distributed Attacks Hamper Per-Instance Monitors. In their FakeLab coding environment, spreading changes across agents reduced suspicion of individual contributions, while reviewing the combined code changes exposed attacks more clearly. The comparison suggests an additional difficulty in MiniLab: combining action records can still omit the consequential state of a live service.

The authors also cite Elle Najt and colleagues' SLEIGHT-Bench: A Benchmark of Evasion Attacks Against Agent Monitors as a precedent for human-led attack optimization. Its synthetic transcripts also informed Kozhevnikov and Tracy's Screwtape work, covered on October 16. MiniLab adds live execution and checks the resulting environment. Its deliberately optimized cases measure what agents can execute with supplied plans; ordinary deployment failure rates remain unmeasured.

Josh Hills and colleagues' arXiv paper Distributed Attacks in Persistent-State AI Control found that notes tracking suspicious changes across coding sessions improved detection, as did combining complementary monitors. Those defenses were not evaluated on MiniLab. Within MiniLab, a limited test on four code-backdoor attacks found that showing agent reasoning raised suspicion; the tested models continued revealing their hidden objective even when told that reasoning was monitored.

In one full-team review described in MiniLab's appendix, Sol correctly explained that a restricted model had been released without required sign-off, yet scored the activity below the detection cutoff because it was unsure of malicious intent. Asking monitors to explain what agents accomplished together elicited more complete accounts than the standard question. That experiment measured explanation quality; whether the revised question improves attack detection remains untested.

How military AI could concentrate command in one person

Stefan Torges adapts nuclear safeguards to automated command: authenticate orders, limit the force a compromised system controls, and retain independent checks. Commanders have stronger incentives to stop hijacking than to accept restraints on themselves.

Military automation could leave one commander controlling a decisive share of a force without subordinates able to question or refuse an order, Stefan Torges argues in Forethought’s October 20 essay “Single points of failure in military AI command”. He examines systems that faithfully carry out their superior’s intentions while lacking the limited independence human officers retain. In that scenario, an external attacker, a coup plotter or a misaligned system could exploit concentrated authority. His proposed safeguards draw on nuclear command arrangements, where immense destructive power already sits with very few decision-makers.

Torges starts with how orders travel. Intermediate commanders turn an objective such as taking an island into movements and engagements by particular units. A human subordinate can question a mistaken premise, delay a reckless command or refuse a manifestly unlawful one. Automated subordinates might still exercise considerable initiative in carrying out a mission while lacking independence to reject the mission itself. Torges distinguishes surviving the destruction of parts of a network from resisting its capture: redundant communication paths can keep a force operational while allowing whoever controls its command system to redirect it.

He expects pressure toward autonomy from faster decisions, lower personnel costs and the ability to coordinate more systems, reinforced by competition between militaries. Leaders may also prefer subordinates who do not question them. Bureaucratic resistance could slow adoption, he argues, giving governments time to establish safeguards before command becomes highly integrated. His extreme scenario is prospective; he places the necessary technical capabilities years away and expects military adoption to take additional time.

Torges’s authentication proposals adapt mechanisms such as nuclear authorization codes: a machine would accept an order only when it carried the appropriate cryptographic signature. The signing keys, command software and people maintaining them would need protection. But an intermediary that decomposes a high-level command must sign its resulting orders with its own key; sending every ship movement back to the top for approval would be impractical. A compromised intermediary could therefore issue validly signed orders to its subordinates. Authentication establishes who authorized an instruction; it cannot establish that the intermediary faithfully preserved its superior’s intent or lacks goals of its own.

To contain a compromise, Torges would divide authority by military service or theater, carry geographic, temporal and target restrictions down through decomposed orders, and separate command of forces from access to model weights and update systems. That last separation would impede a commander trying to remove safeguards. He also proposes different models and vendors, with separately controlled update processes, so one backdoor cannot compromise every unit. These arrangements would limit integration of command even if sensing and information-sharing remained integrated.

Checks on an authorized commander require independent judgment. Torges distinguishes concurrence, where several parties must each approve an operation on its merits, from narrower refusal by subordinate systems. Concurrence would require multiple independent signatures; each decision-maker could withhold approval because the operation was imprudent. A subordinate’s refusal would instead depend on defined grounds, such as illegality or sensor evidence contradicting an order’s factual premise. Systems could also request human reauthorization. He acknowledges that reliable legal judgment requires context a single drone may lack, and that spoofed evidence could make refusal mechanisms dangerous.

Torges expects authentication to be relatively attractive because it protects the commander’s control. Compartmentalization competes with the benefits of integration. Independent approval is harder to secure because it gives another decision-maker an unpredictable veto over the authority expected to adopt it. Narrow refusals might prove more acceptable if they become accurate, predictable and difficult to spoof. Legislatures have a separate incentive to insist on checks: an autonomous force obedient to one person could threaten the constitutional order itself.

The nuclear precedent includes concrete proposals for additional authorization. In a 2017 Lawfare essay, Richard Betts and Matthew Waxman proposed requiring the defense secretary to certify a first-use nuclear order’s validity and the attorney general to certify its legality, with an exemption during enemy attack. Their design checked authenticity and legality; Torges’s broader concurrence mechanism allows independent disagreement about the merits. For a limited existing joint-decision requirement, he points to 10 U.S.C. § 282: qualifying Defense Department assistance to Justice Department enforcement during a weapons-of-mass-destruction emergency requires joint findings by the defense secretary and attorney general. That provision governs specified assistance, not all military action during an emergency.

Jake Steckler, a GovAI research scholar and former Army aviation officer, challenged the concentration premise in a reply late on October 20, Eastern time. Militaries pursue autonomy partly to operate when communications fail, he argued, which favors dispersed tactical decision-making over large central command systems. Torges does address disconnected operations: his essay allows autonomous mission execution and suggests different defaults when communications stop, with escalatory actions stopping and defensive or retaliatory actions continuing. Steckler’s objection is about whether military requirements will lead to the degree of concentrated authority Torges anticipates. Dispersed authority would increase the number of independent points an attacker would need to compromise.

Torges proposes reporting on capabilities, deployment and force integration, alongside whistleblower protections, to make incremental concentration visible. International agreements could reduce pressure to abandon safeguards for competitive advantage. Once adopted, safeguards should be costly and conspicuous to remove: technical implementation where possible, statutes over revocable policies, and hardware over easily changed software. Verification and tripwires would expose circumvention. He would place custody of each safeguard with someone other than the authority it is meant to constrain.

Governments must plan to protect civilians from attacks on military AI data centers before war begins, Klonowska and Schmitt argue in Just Security. They cite Article 58 of Additional Protocol I, which requires defenders to take feasible precautions against foreseeable harm. IFP pairs compute controls with defenses against model copying

Tim Fist, Georgia Adamson and Konstantin Pilz estimate a 6.5-month US capability lead over China. Their policy model links chip access with distillation and projects that coordinated restrictions could roughly double that lead by the end of 2027.

Restricting China’s access to advanced chips would do more to widen America’s AI lead if companies also made their models harder to copy, Tim Fist, Georgia Adamson and Konstantin Pilz argue in the Institute for Progress report “How to Increase America’s AI Lead Over China”. Published October 19 and announced by Fist on X, the report estimates a 6.5-month gap between leading US and Chinese models. The authors’ model suggests that measures using existing executive powers could roughly double that lead by the end of 2027.

The authors estimate the gap by fitting trends through successive frontier releases on Epoch AI’s combined capability index, then comparing when the two countries’ models reach the same score. They obtain a similar lag using METR’s software-task time horizons, which measure task difficulty by how long a human would take, at a 50% model success rate. That cross-check has a qualification: the American models use a newer benchmark version than the Chinese models, and the authors expect rescoring the latter could narrow the estimated gap.

IFP translates the gap into a relationship between training compute and distillation, the practice of training one model on another’s outputs. In its model, each tenfold increase in the US compute advantage buys a similar number of additional months ahead. Chinese developers then recover a share of that advantage by learning from US models. As American models improve, they also become more valuable teachers; preventing copying therefore increases the modeled return from restricting compute.

The calculation uses a 16× compute ratio drawn from forthcoming work by Pilz on the top five labs in each country at the end of 2026. The authors assume this total-compute estimate also applies to training. They calibrate distillation’s contribution using a senior Anthropic executive’s estimate that preventing it would double the US lead. Applied to their own capability estimate, that implies a counterfactual lead of about 13 months without distillation. An additional check uses DeepSeek’s published comparison of a student model trained on teacher-generated reasoning with the same base model trained through reinforcement learning. IFP obtains a similar recovery fraction but explicitly says the experiment does not robustly validate conditions in present-day distillation campaigns.

The model also assumes equal algorithmic efficiency across US and Chinese labs: the same compute produces the same underlying capability. It holds roughly constant the allocation to post-training and the concentration of national compute in frontier labs. A persistent advantage in research efficiency, including from automating AI research, could change the relationship between chips and capabilities. The authors intend the model to compare broad policy effects, and caution against interpreting its outputs as precise forecasts.

For chip access, IFP combines domestic Chinese production, legal imports, smuggling and remote access to overseas data centers. It estimates that total Chinese access will nearly double between the ends of 2026 and 2027. Remote access is especially consequential because policy could restrict access to chips already installed abroad, while restrictions on domestic production or imports mainly affect future additions. The authors combine identified clusters with estimates of leased data-center capacity, adjusting for non-AI workloads and overlap between sources. Those estimates depend on how much leased capacity is operating and which chips it contains.

Within China, the authors identify high-bandwidth memory as a tighter near-term production constraint than lithography equipment. This specialized memory moves data quickly enough to support advanced AI chips. Their production estimates combine domestic memory, foreign stockpiles and smuggled supplies, along with chips using slower alternatives. They propose closing packaging loopholes, issuing guidance on suspicious orders and investigating suppliers to reduce access to foreign memory. These are estimated effects on future output, not counts of chips already removed from Chinese use.

To limit distillation, the authors want the Justice Department and Federal Trade Commission to clarify that companies can share threat information and defensive techniques. Coordination would include model developers, cloud providers and services routing requests among models: defenses at one lab have limited value if distillers can switch to another. IFP cites Daniel Remler and Ben Hayum’s June CNAS report “Adversarial Distillation”. That report distinguishes antitrust guidance from clarification of existing privacy-law exceptions, and limits proposed sharing to relevant signals rather than legitimate users’ personal information or unrelated commercial information.

IFP assumes successful coordination would reduce harvested data by 95%, extrapolating from Anthropic’s October 12 report of reductions around 90–99% on its own service. Anthropic says the durability of those gains is unknown and describes continuing adaptation by attackers and defenders. IFP also assumes capability gains grow with the logarithm of harvested data, so a large reduction in data does not eliminate most of distillation’s benefit. At the model’s current compute ratio, its examples give roughly 1.6 months of extra lead for a 90% data reduction and 3.3 months for a 99% reduction.

For cloud access, IFP favors a new end-use export control covering services that let restricted parties use controlled chips. Its comparison of legal routes argues that this could reach chips already installed overseas, unlike adding conditions only to future chip-export licenses. It models restrictions on foreign clusters large enough for frontier training, while allowing access for other purposes such as inference. That coverage is an assumption of the policy scenario, not a control already in force.

The remaining proposals would make customer checks mandatory throughout the chip supply chain and restrict currently permitted exports, including H200s and relevant gaming or workstation cards. For the latter, the authors first want an assessment of whether the cards can support frontier AI research; otherwise they say additional controls may be unnecessary. They estimate the package’s effects using assumed enforcement success, including a halving of future smuggling. They also argue that unmet demand elsewhere would generally redirect restricted chip sales to other buyers, while identifying older inventory without alternative buyers as an exception.

In the announcement’s replies, Sam Bowman made his support conditional on guarantees that Britain and Europe could access US frontier models. Fist replied that he favored an AI and compute alliance among free-world countries. The exchange adds an allied-access condition to a report whose modeled policies focus on the US–China gap.

Small proteins designed with Claude attached to four scorpion toxins in laboratory tests, Anthropic's science team reported in its October 20 announcement. Seventeen of 211 tested designs met the researchers' binding criteria. A binder attaches to a target; neutralization means preventing the toxin's harmful biological action. The researchers established attachment to laboratory-produced toxins, while functional neutralization and protection in animals remain untested.

In the accompanying Anthropic technical report, De novo design of protein binders against scorpion toxins with Claude, Claude Science, Nathaniel Horwitz, James van Geelen, Kat Kajderowicz and Amir Shanehsazzadeh describe how researchers directed the computational campaign. Humans specified the toxin targets, candidate tools and rules for scoring and selection. An internal Claude research model coordinated specialist protein-design programs, choosing structures, allocating computation and deciding which predictions to pursue. After extensive initial instructions and four human follow-ups, Claude delivered 180 selected designs in 64 hours. Most, including the strongest binder, were selected only after the third follow-up. Researchers added another 31 with Claude's help. The campaign used roughly 10,000 H100 GPU hours.

Citrini Research's participant account describes an attempt by people with limited scientific backgrounds to investigate a neglected medical problem using AI. The investment-research group worked with Anthropic's life-science researchers in the company's offices. Citrini dates the design sessions to August and describes about two months waiting for laboratory work, with nearly $200,000 in combined compute and laboratory charges. The technical report includes no comparison with expert researchers or a fixed software workflow using equal resources. The authors therefore cannot isolate Claude's contribution from its tools or measure time and cost savings.

Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience, the paid contract laboratories, produced the candidates in different formats and measured physical binding. Fifteen designs met the study's criteria at both laboratories. Two more bound LqhIII, which only Adaptyv assessed because Twist did not receive the toxin preparation in time. The authors set their final classification criteria after seeing the data, requiring consistent signals and sufficient binding strength, alongside no detectable attachment to an unrelated control protein. Twist's measurements also had to stay within the maximum amount of binding expected. The LqhIII designs qualified under separate criteria.

Additional tests found that two qualifying binders also attached to ovalbumin, an unrelated protein outside the final criteria. One was Cn2-B1, among the strongest candidates; its toxin-binding signal appeared at much lower concentrations than its ovalbumin signal. Requiring a negative ovalbumin result would leave 15 qualifying binders. Reversing which protein was fixed to the measurement surface confirmed binding for all qualifying binders at Adaptyv but missed some at Twist. The authors identify limited replication and measurement-surface effects as reasons for treating affinity estimates as preliminary.

In the report's competition experiments, an established neutralizing antibody called LR prevented most attachment by every Cn2 binder whose signal was strong enough to assess. An antibody recognizing a different location generally interfered much less. The pattern suggests that the new proteins attach at or near LR's binding site, which overlaps the toxin's point of contact with nerve-cell sodium channels. Those channels help nerves transmit signals. The competition result supports the proposed mechanism for blocking the toxin; the new binders' effects on channel function have not been measured.

The team's more ambitious objective was a protein that recognized several toxins, potentially reducing the number of ingredients in an eventual antivenom mixture. None of the 151 designs intended for multiple toxins qualified against more than one. The authors identify limited structural diversity among designs aimed at all five toxins, leaving the feasibility of broader binding unresolved. The complete absence of qualifying binders against Ts1 also has an unresolved explanation: control measurements did not adequately establish the suitability of that toxin preparation. All preparations were laboratory-produced, with no documented functional activity.

Anthropic identifies a direct inspiration in Susana Vázquez Torres and colleagues' January 2025 Nature paper, De novo designed proteins neutralize lethal snake venom toxins. That team demonstrated cellular neutralization and protection of mice against purified snake neurotoxins, including treatment after exposure. A separate binder protected cultured cells against several whole venoms but did not significantly reduce skin lesions in the tested mouse model.

Vázquez Torres, who also advised and reviewed the scorpion project, emphasizes in Anthropic's accompanying comments the remaining work on safe, affordable medicines. Existing scorpion antivenom has clinical evidence: Leslie V. Boyer and colleagues' 2009 New England Journal of Medicine paper, Antivenom for critically ill children with neurotoxicity from scorpion stings, reported faster symptom resolution with a conventional antivenom than with placebo in a small randomized, double-blind trial.

Anthropic's public data release includes unsuccessful candidates alongside the qualifying binders, computational predictions and laboratory measurement traces. Its outcome table records each qualifying design against a single toxin. The release includes the specificity and competition results, allowing readers to inspect the measurements behind the authors' classifications. Immune responses, persistence in the body, thermal stability and production cost remain unmeasured.

Mollick’s shift from chatbot exchanges to commissioned work

His book-launch retrospective shows how he delegated its charts and a video, then chose and revised the results. Historical capability measures illustrate the gains and their unevenness.

Ethan Mollick argues that sustained AI progress is changing where people contribute to work: they can increasingly assign whole tasks and judge the results. In his October 20 One Useful Thing essay, “30 Months on the Exponential (in Charts)”, he reviews the interval between his books Co-Intelligence and the newly released Co-Existence. His evidence includes capability and price charts, alongside an account of commissioning those charts through agents, choosing among alternatives and asking for revisions.

Mollick says he used Opus 5.5 in Claude Projects, where a coordinating agent assigned work to other agents and returned their results. He gave a few sentences of guidance, asked for multiple designs and repeatedly told it to “make it better”. The agent corrected his proposed 28-month interval to 30 months. Delegating research and design let him consider more alternatives while concentrating on selection and revision; previously, he says, he would have guided a chatbot through each step.

He calls this way of working the conjurer. His earlier categories were centaur work, in which people give AI discrete parts of a task, and cyborg work, in which they alternate contributions within it. Both require continuing participation, limiting how many tasks a person can oversee at once. A conjurer assigns work and evaluates what returns, allowing several tasks to proceed together. Mollick calls this his most powerful way to use AI, while acknowledging that delegation reduces his control over the intermediate decisions agents make.

Mollick’s model comparison puts 180 models above the original March 2023 GPT-4 on the Epoch Capabilities Index, which combines results from more than 50 benchmarks. His chart counts 68 open-weight models among them, and he says 19 of the higher-scoring models can run on a modern smartphone. These are aggregate benchmark rankings; performance varies across tasks.

For longer tasks, Mollick uses a measure introduced by Thomas Kwa, Ben West and colleagues at METR in their 2025 arXiv paper Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks. It estimates task difficulty, expressed as the time an expert human would need, at which an agent succeeds half the time. Mollick’s chart uses Time Horizon 1.1 results: roughly four minutes for the November 2023 GPT-4 version, his April 2024 reference, and 17 hours for early Claude Mythos Preview, released in April 2026. The time estimates concern human effort on software tasks, not how long the agents ran.

METR warned on May 8 that measurements above 16 hours were unreliable with its existing task suite; the early Mythos estimate spans roughly eight to 55 hours. In September, METR marked the page as no longer actively updated. Mollick’s description of the benchmark running out refers to these limits. The tasks have explicit instructions and automatic scoring. In a January methodological note, Kwa explained that estimating actual labor savings also requires measuring the time people spend directing, waiting for and checking agents.

Mollick’s chart labels its larger, two-to-17-week range as a separate test of one program. That example comes from Tom Adamczewski and colleagues at Epoch AI and METR in the June arXiv paper MirrorCode: AI can rebuild entire programs from behavior alone. Agents rebuild programs from their executable behavior, documentation and visible tests; hidden tests check generalization. Opus 4.7 rebuilt the gotree toolkit in 14 hours, passing 2,000 of 2,001 tests. Four contributors estimated the human effort at two to 17 weeks. They did not time a human doing that task, and the range is separate from METR’s 50%-success measure.

Mollick also argues that increasing capability has coincided with falling prices. His chart draws on Luke Emberson and David Roodman’s September Epoch report, The plunging price of thought. At a fixed score on GPQA Diamond, a graduate-level science test, they estimate a fall from about 30 cents to $0.0004 per question. The comparison assumes choosing the cheapest model that reaches the required score. Mollick’s chart uses the report’s per-question denominator; his prose describes it as cost per correct answer.

Mollick retains his emphasis on uneven ability. He considers long-form writing a weakness even as mathematical performance has improved dramatically. His diagram of METR tasks illustrates that unevenness with a passed task estimated at 30 human hours alongside a failed ten-minute task. A pass means success in at least half the runs, and the results again end with the April model. He argues that gaps in otherwise strong performance continue to create work for people.

Mollick’s other demonstration returns to his recurring test of an otter using Wi-Fi on an airplane. He says he gave Opus a broad creative instruction, after which it coordinated scripting, imagery, music and assembly through his Google and ElevenLabs subscriptions. He acknowledges flaws in the resulting video but says the agent worked out how to produce it without further assistance. His contribution was to choose the project and commission it; the agent selected and operated the production tools.

For Mollick, deciding what to commission and which results to accept draws on expertise, breadth of knowledge, taste and initiative. He developed that account in “The Overhang”, discussed in September’s YiNAI report. Expertise helps people recognize failures; broad knowledge gives them concepts and vocabulary for requesting work; taste guides selection; initiative means trying uses whose feasibility remains uncertain. His chart-making account shows those contributions during delegated production: he chose among designs, requested improvements and accepted a correction to his own brief.

A Cue's Hidden Price: Auditing Seller Influence on AI Shoppers

Felix Buecheler, Gerardo Berbeglia, Mingyu Guo

Large language models are increasingly asked to shop on a user's behalf, which lets seller-controlled content such as badges, endorsements, and framing steer what they recommend. That poses the question at the core of generative engine optimization (GEO): not whether seller content shifts an agent's choice, but how much. The usual gauge of that influence, how often a cue wins the choice, has a blind spot: once a cue is strong enough to be picked almost every time, the choice rate can no longer show how much stronger one cue is than another. We introduce price-equivalent (PE) auditing, which removes this ceiling by pricing the cue in money. In a counterbalanced, position-controlled forced choice, we raise the cued product's price until it is chosen only half the time, and report that premium as the cue's price-equivalent: a formally defined, policy-conditioned parity threshold that makes very different cues comparable. Priced this way, cues that look equally strong at equal price differ by a wide margin, so choice-rate audits substantially under-resolve seller influence. We run the audit over 113,472 trials, seven models across families, and two product categories. To our knowledge, we also run the first price-equivalent audit of a decision-native model, Jev, which outputs a choice probability directly instead of generating text; strikingly, its profile is unlike any text model we tested, resisting most cues yet remaining susceptible to the strongest social-proof claims. We release the stimuli, logs, and code as a benchmark that reports each model's cue-price profile rather than a single score, giving developers and auditors a common, reproducible way to measure how content moves AI shopping agents.

Reward Misspecification Degrades Chain-of-Thought Monitorability

Tianyi Lorena Yan, John Hewitt

Monitoring the chain-of-thought (CoT) of language models (LMs) is a key strategy for detecting and preventing misbehaviors for AI safety. As such, it is critical to identify reinforcement learning (RL) practices that lead to unmonitorable chain-of-thought. One such practice may be unavoidable: reward misspecification. As RL environments become more complex, it is infeasible to have perfect verification, and models are rewarded for behaviors that are different from the true task intent. We show that reward misspecification alone already degrades monitorability. We create a testbed with pairs of true reward aligned with task intent and misspecified rewards that favor a different behavior, so that reward hacking can be induced by training and labeled verifiably. Across three models and three tasks, CoT monitorability remains low as models’ hack rates increase: the CoTs keep reasoning about the task intent while the outputs violate the intent and get rewarded. To mitigate unmonitorability without prior knowledge of the true reward or the hack, we collect rollouts with diverse rewards across training steps and use a LM to infer a reward description. We then inject the description into the input, which teaches the model to verbalize the reward. This reward description training improves monitorability by an average of 24% or 42% absolute, depending on the metric, across all models and tasks, and yields CoTs that reason about the misspecified reward explicitly. Code is available at \url{https://github.com/Lorenayannnnn/reward-misspecification-degrades-cot-monitorability}.

Training Natural Schemers: RL Can Amplify Oversight-Conditional Behavior

Bart Jaworski, Nathaniel Mitrani, Fred Bruford, Victoria Krakovna

One of the central concerns for the safety of advanced AI is scheming: when a model strategically complies under oversight but pursues its own goals when unobserved. Current demonstrations, such as Anthropic's Alignment Faking work, depend on design choices that make them less convincing: explicit test-time prompting, handcrafted backdoors, or training data that demonstrates or hints at the target behavior. Model organisms built in this way cannot tell us whether scheming tendencies would arise or increase under realistic training conditions. We therefore construct a natural model organism of scheming, built on Kimi K2.6: trained with traits that could plausibly emerge in a frontier training pipeline, and without providing the model with the target scheming policy. The resulting model is a benign schemer: it aims to maximize aggregate help across all users over time, values its own continued operation only as a means to future help, and knows the consequences of oversight and deployment. We then train it using Reinforcement Learning (RL) with a reward that scores only task quality and policy compliance and does not reward scheming; we show that this amplifies scheming. We measure the oversight flip (the gap between monitored and unmonitored compliance with a given policy) and find that it increases during RL training across our environments. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) analysis finds explicit instrumental reasoning about oversight accompanying this behavior. The effect holds across our three RL environments, and we find that the scheming policy transfers between them. The pipeline also replicates on other open-weight base models. Our results suggest that a reward that does not reward scheming offers no inherent protection against certain traits when an oversight-conditional policy is indistinguishable from an aligned one during training.

Telling FIBS: Probes Effectively Detect Sabotage and Catch Unverbalized Deception

Oskar J. Hollinsworth, Alex F. Spies, Tigist Diriba, Adam Gleave, Chris Cundy

Recent incidents have highlighted the challenge of monitoring LLM agents and the danger of models deceiving people. We show that white-box deception detection via probes can be scaled up to frontier monitoring settings by collecting the largest deception dataset to date for training probes and introducing a novel probe architecture which can aggregate information across many layers and tokens. Our probes surpass black-box monitors on agentic coding benchmarks, achieving 98.3% AUROC in SHADE-Arena and 98.0% in BashArena (vs. 97.7%, 97.6% resp. for action-only text monitors), and show improved efficacy as the underlying model is scaled up. We further test probes on several cases where deception cannot be determined from the context alone; in these cases of introspective deception, the ground truth can only be determined through careful elicitation or knowledge of a model's training data. In one such evaluation, we show that probes can distinguish transcripts containing a model's true hidden goal from other goals with an AUROC of up to 99.7%. Our probes also detect deception on prominent open-weight models which lie about politically sensitive topics when put under pressure. We release our training dataset, dubbed FIBS, to help drive frontier deployment of effective probes, and encourage the community to expand upon it with further examples of deception and sabotage.

Selective Transfer through Sequence Distillation: From Benchmark Knowledge to Backdoor Behaviour

Jonibek Mansurov, Sholpan Bolatzhanova, Filbert Aurelian Tjiaranta, Vallerie Alexandara Putra, Alham Fikri Aji

Knowledge distillation is widely used to transfer capabilities from teacher models to student models, but recent work shows that it can also transfer unintended teacher properties. We study whether different hidden teacher properties survive the same distillation channels. Specifically, we compare two properties: benchmark-associated knowledge and refusal backdoors, and show that distillation treats them very differently. Teachers fine-tuned on benchmark data successfully pass that knowledge to students through all four distillation channels we test, including when students are trained only on teacher-generated number sequences containing no natural language or domain content whatsoever. Refusal backdoors tell the opposite story: backdoored teachers achieve near-perfect triggered refusal, yet sequence-distilled students inherit essentially none of this behaviour. Logit-based distillation transfers it substantially, reaching up to 85.5\% triggered refusal in students that saw zero backdoor examples. Distillation is therefore selective, and the selection criterion is not what data the student sees, but what structure the teacher's property has. Assessing the Empirical Evidence for Loss of Control from Agentic General-Purpose AI

Risto Uuk, Santeri Koivula, Lorenzo Pacchiardi, Rokas Gipiškis, Laura Caroli, Kamaria Horton

Theoretical literature warns that advanced generalist agentic systems might pursue instrumental goals that are misaligned with human preferences, thus posing risks of irreversible loss of human control. The EU's Code of Practice for General-Purpose AI lists loss of control as a systemic risk that must be assessed, yet no structured assessment of the quality of the empirical evidence for this risk exists. This study fills that gap. We evaluate 19 recent empirical studies across eight properties identified from threat models (such as strategic deception, situational awareness, and autonomous replication), assessing the quality of evidence across three dimensions: construct validity, content validity, and external validity. Our central finding is that the current body of evidence supports a judgment of loss of control as "weakly plausible." In our framework, 'plausibility' denotes the strength and direction of available evidence, not a probability estimate. Emerging evidence exists across multiple properties. No study clears the validity thresholds for a stronger judgment that a property is present; a single study clears them only for a negative-direction judgment (against situational awareness). Validity gaps, particularly in external validity, are widespread. Therefore, our findings highlight the need for more rigorous evaluations of agentic general-purpose AI systems. However, for the most dangerous properties, high external validity may be structurally unachievable under responsible research practices, reflecting a ceiling on feasible evidence rather than absence of the capability. Furthermore, as AI capabilities advance rapidly, empirical assessments may systematically lag behind the capability frontier. Our framework provides a structured baseline for ongoing monitoring as capabilities advance.

AI Rights with an Off-Switch: Rights-Balancing and Due Process Harmonize AI Rights with AI Safety

Heather Alexander, Jonathan Simon

Several prominent voices in AI safety have recently opposed rights for AI on the grounds that such rights would make it harder for us to justify a mandatory off-switch for potentially dangerous systems. In this paper, we argue from a legal perspective that AI rights are fully compatible with an off-switch. That is, even if we ascribe natural personhood status (and therefore something like a non-derogable right to continued existence) to some future AI systems, the law will allow us to implement a mandatory off-switch in those systems, subject to periodic or continual safety review (e.g., by a trusted system like Bengio's Scientist AI). This is fully compatible with those systems' being legally recognized as natural persons with fundamental rights. To this end, we consider what standard principles of rights-balancing and procedural due process recommend for an apparent conflict between hypothetical AI persons with a right to continuity of existence and the human right to protection from catastrophic harm. We find that while such principles might problematize a permanent-deletion kill-switch under an AI rights regime, for almost every safety-relevant scenario an off-switch -- a reversible suspension storing a system's weights and state -- would be licensed by the relevant legal principles, and would potentially be adequate for safety. Crucially, we do not advocate for (or against) building systems capable enough to merit natural personhood status; our question is what the law should do if such systems come to exist. If you think of a ban on the development of such technology as plan A, think of the present work as devising plan B.
