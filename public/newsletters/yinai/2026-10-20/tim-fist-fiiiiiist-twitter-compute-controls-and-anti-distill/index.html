IFP pairs compute controls with defenses against model copying

Tim Fist, Georgia Adamson and Konstantin Pilz estimate a 6.5-month US capability lead over China. Their policy model links chip access with distillation and projects that coordinated restrictions could roughly double that lead by the end of 2027.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 October 2026 1015 words · ~5 min

Restricting China’s access to advanced chips would do more to widen America’s AI lead if companies also made their models harder to copy, Tim Fist, Georgia Adamson and Konstantin Pilz argue in the Institute for Progress report “How to Increase America’s AI Lead Over China”. Published October 19 and announced by Fist on X, the report estimates a 6.5-month gap between leading US and Chinese models. The authors’ model suggests that measures using existing executive powers could roughly double that lead by the end of 2027.

The authors estimate the gap by fitting trends through successive frontier releases on Epoch AI’s combined capability index, then comparing when the two countries’ models reach the same score. They obtain a similar lag using METR’s software-task time horizons, which measure task difficulty by how long a human would take, at a 50% model success rate. That cross-check has a qualification: the American models use a newer benchmark version than the Chinese models, and the authors expect rescoring the latter could narrow the estimated gap.

IFP translates the gap into a relationship between training compute and distillation, the practice of training one model on another’s outputs. In its model, each tenfold increase in the US compute advantage buys a similar number of additional months ahead. Chinese developers then recover a share of that advantage by learning from US models. As American models improve, they also become more valuable teachers; preventing copying therefore increases the modeled return from restricting compute.

The calculation uses a 16× compute ratio drawn from forthcoming work by Pilz on the top five labs in each country at the end of 2026. The authors assume this total-compute estimate also applies to training. They calibrate distillation’s contribution using a senior Anthropic executive’s estimate that preventing it would double the US lead. Applied to their own capability estimate, that implies a counterfactual lead of about 13 months without distillation. An additional check uses DeepSeek’s published comparison of a student model trained on teacher-generated reasoning with the same base model trained through reinforcement learning. IFP obtains a similar recovery fraction but explicitly says the experiment does not robustly validate conditions in present-day distillation campaigns.

The model also assumes equal algorithmic efficiency across US and Chinese labs: the same compute produces the same underlying capability. It holds roughly constant the allocation to post-training and the concentration of national compute in frontier labs. A persistent advantage in research efficiency, including from automating AI research, could change the relationship between chips and capabilities. The authors intend the model to compare broad policy effects, and caution against interpreting its outputs as precise forecasts.

For chip access, IFP combines domestic Chinese production, legal imports, smuggling and remote access to overseas data centers. It estimates that total Chinese access will nearly double between the ends of 2026 and 2027. Remote access is especially consequential because policy could restrict access to chips already installed abroad, while restrictions on domestic production or imports mainly affect future additions. The authors combine identified clusters with estimates of leased data-center capacity, adjusting for non-AI workloads and overlap between sources. Those estimates depend on how much leased capacity is operating and which chips it contains.

Within China, the authors identify high-bandwidth memory as a tighter near-term production constraint than lithography equipment. This specialized memory moves data quickly enough to support advanced AI chips. Their production estimates combine domestic memory, foreign stockpiles and smuggled supplies, along with chips using slower alternatives. They propose closing packaging loopholes, issuing guidance on suspicious orders and investigating suppliers to reduce access to foreign memory. These are estimated effects on future output, not counts of chips already removed from Chinese use.

To limit distillation, the authors want the Justice Department and Federal Trade Commission to clarify that companies can share threat information and defensive techniques. Coordination would include model developers, cloud providers and services routing requests among models: defenses at one lab have limited value if distillers can switch to another. IFP cites Daniel Remler and Ben Hayum’s June CNAS report “Adversarial Distillation”. That report distinguishes antitrust guidance from clarification of existing privacy-law exceptions, and limits proposed sharing to relevant signals rather than legitimate users’ personal information or unrelated commercial information.

IFP assumes successful coordination would reduce harvested data by 95%, extrapolating from Anthropic’s October 12 report of reductions around 90–99% on its own service. Anthropic says the durability of those gains is unknown and describes continuing adaptation by attackers and defenders. IFP also assumes capability gains grow with the logarithm of harvested data, so a large reduction in data does not eliminate most of distillation’s benefit. At the model’s current compute ratio, its examples give roughly 1.6 months of extra lead for a 90% data reduction and 3.3 months for a 99% reduction.

For cloud access, IFP favors a new end-use export control covering services that let restricted parties use controlled chips. Its comparison of legal routes argues that this could reach chips already installed overseas, unlike adding conditions only to future chip-export licenses. It models restrictions on foreign clusters large enough for frontier training, while allowing access for other purposes such as inference. That coverage is an assumption of the policy scenario, not a control already in force.

The remaining proposals would make customer checks mandatory throughout the chip supply chain and restrict currently permitted exports, including H200s and relevant gaming or workstation cards. For the latter, the authors first want an assessment of whether the cards can support frontier AI research; otherwise they say additional controls may be unnecessary. They estimate the package’s effects using assumed enforcement success, including a halving of future smuggling. They also argue that unmet demand elsewhere would generally redirect restricted chip sales to other buyers, while identifying older inventory without alternative buyers as an exception.

In the announcement’s replies, Sam Bowman made his support conditional on guarantees that Britain and Europe could access US frontier models. Fist replied that he favored an AI and compute alliance among free-world countries. The exchange adds an allied-access condition to a report whose modeled policies focus on the US–China gap.
