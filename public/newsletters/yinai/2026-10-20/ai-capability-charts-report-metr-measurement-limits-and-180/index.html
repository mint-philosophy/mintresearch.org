Mollick’s shift from chatbot exchanges to commissioned work

His book-launch retrospective shows how he delegated its charts and a video, then chose and revised the results. Historical capability measures illustrate the gains and their unevenness.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 October 2026 897 words · ~4 min

Ethan Mollick argues that sustained AI progress is changing where people contribute to work: they can increasingly assign whole tasks and judge the results. In his October 20 One Useful Thing essay, “30 Months on the Exponential (in Charts)”, he reviews the interval between his books Co-Intelligence and the newly released Co-Existence. His evidence includes capability and price charts, alongside an account of commissioning those charts through agents, choosing among alternatives and asking for revisions.

Mollick says he used Opus 5.5 in Claude Projects, where a coordinating agent assigned work to other agents and returned their results. He gave a few sentences of guidance, asked for multiple designs and repeatedly told it to “make it better”. The agent corrected his proposed 28-month interval to 30 months. Delegating research and design let him consider more alternatives while concentrating on selection and revision; previously, he says, he would have guided a chatbot through each step.

He calls this way of working the conjurer. His earlier categories were centaur work, in which people give AI discrete parts of a task, and cyborg work, in which they alternate contributions within it. Both require continuing participation, limiting how many tasks a person can oversee at once. A conjurer assigns work and evaluates what returns, allowing several tasks to proceed together. Mollick calls this his most powerful way to use AI, while acknowledging that delegation reduces his control over the intermediate decisions agents make.

Mollick’s model comparison puts 180 models above the original March 2023 GPT-4 on the Epoch Capabilities Index, which combines results from more than 50 benchmarks. His chart counts 68 open-weight models among them, and he says 19 of the higher-scoring models can run on a modern smartphone. These are aggregate benchmark rankings; performance varies across tasks.

For longer tasks, Mollick uses a measure introduced by Thomas Kwa, Ben West and colleagues at METR in their 2025 arXiv paper Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks. It estimates task difficulty, expressed as the time an expert human would need, at which an agent succeeds half the time. Mollick’s chart uses Time Horizon 1.1 results: roughly four minutes for the November 2023 GPT-4 version, his April 2024 reference, and 17 hours for early Claude Mythos Preview, released in April 2026. The time estimates concern human effort on software tasks, not how long the agents ran.

METR warned on May 8 that measurements above 16 hours were unreliable with its existing task suite; the early Mythos estimate spans roughly eight to 55 hours. In September, METR marked the page as no longer actively updated. Mollick’s description of the benchmark running out refers to these limits. The tasks have explicit instructions and automatic scoring. In a January methodological note, Kwa explained that estimating actual labor savings also requires measuring the time people spend directing, waiting for and checking agents.

Mollick’s chart labels its larger, two-to-17-week range as a separate test of one program. That example comes from Tom Adamczewski and colleagues at Epoch AI and METR in the June arXiv paper MirrorCode: AI can rebuild entire programs from behavior alone. Agents rebuild programs from their executable behavior, documentation and visible tests; hidden tests check generalization. Opus 4.7 rebuilt the gotree toolkit in 14 hours, passing 2,000 of 2,001 tests. Four contributors estimated the human effort at two to 17 weeks. They did not time a human doing that task, and the range is separate from METR’s 50%-success measure.

Mollick also argues that increasing capability has coincided with falling prices. His chart draws on Luke Emberson and David Roodman’s September Epoch report, The plunging price of thought. At a fixed score on GPQA Diamond, a graduate-level science test, they estimate a fall from about 30 cents to $0.0004 per question. The comparison assumes choosing the cheapest model that reaches the required score. Mollick’s chart uses the report’s per-question denominator; his prose describes it as cost per correct answer.

Mollick retains his emphasis on uneven ability. He considers long-form writing a weakness even as mathematical performance has improved dramatically. His diagram of METR tasks illustrates that unevenness with a passed task estimated at 30 human hours alongside a failed ten-minute task. A pass means success in at least half the runs, and the results again end with the April model. He argues that gaps in otherwise strong performance continue to create work for people.

Mollick’s other demonstration returns to his recurring test of an otter using Wi-Fi on an airplane. He says he gave Opus a broad creative instruction, after which it coordinated scripting, imagery, music and assembly through his Google and ElevenLabs subscriptions. He acknowledges flaws in the resulting video but says the agent worked out how to produce it without further assistance. His contribution was to choose the project and commission it; the agent selected and operated the production tools.

For Mollick, deciding what to commission and which results to accept draws on expertise, breadth of knowledge, taste and initiative. He developed that account in “The Overhang”, discussed in September’s YiNAI report. Expertise helps people recognize failures; broad knowledge gives them concepts and vocabulary for requesting work; taste guides selection; initiative means trying uses whose feasibility remains uncertain. His chart-making account shows those contributions during delegated production: he chose among designs, requested improvements and accepted a correction to his own brief.
