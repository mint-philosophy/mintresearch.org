Anthropic’s bill critics press beyond a preemption rewrite

Zvi Mowshowitz challenges the safeguards in Anthony Cimino’s defence of Anthropic’s proposal. Nathan Calvin asks whether the company’s conduct fits its safety mission or a drive to build superhuman AI first.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 October 2026 1045 words · ~5 min

Anthropic’s promise to revise its draft bill’s state-law preemption language has not satisfied Zvi Mowshowitz. In his October 20 Substack response to federal-affairs lead Anthony Cimino, he argues that the company has acknowledged drafting problems without reconsidering a federal safety regime he regards as too weak to justify displacing state protections. He welcomes the proposal’s power to stop a lab’s internal model use, but warns that restrictions on intervention could encourage presidents to use other, less predictable powers. Nathan Calvin’s October 19 reply, which Mowshowitz endorses, questions the institutional priorities behind the proposal.

Cimino’s October 18 statement describes the draft as a translation of Anthropic’s Advanced AI Framework, circulated to a small number of congressional offices for feedback. It excludes some policies the company ultimately wants, he says, including ideas from Dario Amodei’s recent essay on slowing frontier development. He defends common federal standards for testing disclosures and incident reporting, citing nuclear safety and high-risk medical devices as areas with federal preemption. He says states should retain child-protection powers, whistleblower laws and common-law liability, and promises clearer language preserving their role in evaluator standards and labs’ ability to shut models down.

On intervention, Cimino wants Commerce to ask a federal court to block dangerous models with insufficient safeguards, supported by an independent assessment. Courts should act quickly while applying a meaningful standard against abuse. The circulating discussion copy attributed to Anthropic requires Commerce to establish an imminent enumerated risk, the necessity of suspension, the inadequacy of less restrictive measures and a narrowly tailored order. Its risk definition covers foreseeable death or serious injury to more than 50 people, or property damage or loss exceeding $1 billion, through specified biological, cyber, loss-of-control or automated-research pathways. The court would decide these questions without deferring to Commerce.

Mowshowitz objects that irreversible frontier risks require an intervention mechanism that can act before extended litigation. He turns Cimino’s occupational-safety analogy back on him: section 13 of the Occupational Safety and Health Act allows temporary restraining orders against imminent workplace dangers, including orders without notice. Anthropic’s discussion copy also permits an order before the developer is heard, lasting at most seven days, but requires an independent assessment and additional certification from Commerce’s head. Mowshowitz’s objection concerns the burden and design of the procedure; the draft does contain an emergency route.

The proposal expressly preserves federal powers under other laws. Mowshowitz argues that making its own route cumbersome would therefore increase the chance of another intervention like June’s Fable suspension. Anthropic’s June 12 statement said an export-control directive covering foreign nationals forced it to disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all customers, while calling for a clearer statutory process. Mowshowitz accepts the concern about arbitrary executive action but thinks this draft could worsen it. He nevertheless credits the internal-use suspension power as a substantial improvement: stopping public release alone leaves dangerous research inside a lab untouched.

Cimino says the harm threshold came from California law and may need reconsideration. Mowshowitz invokes the Hugging Face incident to argue that serious failures can fall below such thresholds. His suggestion that an incident below them would trigger nothing goes beyond the reporting provisions: section 7 separately requires reports within 15 days after a developer determines that a qualifying containment failure occurred, where it is expected to cause more than $250,000 in outside losses, death or serious physical injury. Smaller containment failures receive annual aggregate reporting. The blocking threshold and incident-reporting duties therefore require separate assessment.

Mowshowitz also challenges the preemption bargain itself. On his reading, Anthropic would centralize catastrophic-risk regulation in Washington while distrusting federal officials enough to constrain their response. Meanwhile, state rules on consumer protection and deepfakes would remain fragmented. The dispute over state suspension powers, covered in the earlier comparison with ModelMirror, remains relevant: section 14 lists suspension among four covered functions, but its operative preemption clause names only disclosure, third-party evaluation and incident reporting. It also directs that ambiguities favor state authority. Calvin’s concern about intended preemption is distinct from what that public copy expressly preempts.

Cimino says Anthropic is developing stronger incident-reporting proposals, evaluator requirements and ways to prevent labs shopping for accommodating assessors. Policies for government adjustment of development’s pace are also prospective. Mowshowitz considers the current proposal weaker overall than FRONTIER, despite improvements in individual provisions, and rejects Cimino’s description of it as the strongest proposal available. His criticism is directed at the compromise Anthropic chose to circulate; the statement promises further work.

Calvin’s long reply asks which account of Anthropic best explains its conduct: a company enriching employees and investors, an organization pursuing AI safety, or an organization trying to reach superhuman capability first through models that build their successors. He compares current choices with the company’s 2023 safety essay, which said that, if safety proved intractable, Anthropic should demonstrate the danger and help prevent dangerous AI development. The essay also envisaged a scenario requiring a halt to progress while safety research continued.

Calvin cites compute procurement and the growing use of Claude in internal AI research, alongside what he characterizes as weaker unilateral restraints in Responsible Scaling Policy V3. He treats the bill’s barriers to intervention as part of that pattern. Even a sincere belief that Anthropic must win before a less careful competitor, he argues, can produce the same acceleration and risks. Outsiders cannot inspect employees’ intentions; they can assess the company’s choices and whether leadership corrects them. He questions why purported mistakes repeatedly protect the company’s freedom to develop models, and whether frontier capability has translated into comparably strong safety advocacy.

Calvin acknowledges that his list is deliberately one-sided, crediting Anthropic’s safety research, outside collaborations and public advocacy. In the replies, Nick Moran offers its confrontation with the Defense Department as a counterexample: he argues that the resulting supply-chain-risk designation showed willingness to bear commercial costs for safety. Calvin closes by invoking Chris Olah’s May remarks about commercial, geopolitical and personal incentives affecting every frontier lab, and the need for informed outside critics.

Mowshowitz asks Anthropic to acknowledge where its proposal is weaker than FRONTIER, disavow or apologize for circulating a bill that does not reflect what it seeks, state its substantive policy goals, and announce steps to prevent a recurrence. Revising preemption language would answer only part of those demands.
