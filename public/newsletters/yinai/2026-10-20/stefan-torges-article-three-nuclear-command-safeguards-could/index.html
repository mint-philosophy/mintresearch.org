How military AI could concentrate command in one person

Stefan Torges adapts nuclear safeguards to automated command: authenticate orders, limit the force a compromised system controls, and retain independent checks. Commanders have stronger incentives to stop hijacking than to accept restraints on themselves.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 October 2026 901 words · ~5 min

Military automation could leave one commander controlling a decisive share of a force without subordinates able to question or refuse an order, Stefan Torges argues in Forethought’s October 20 essay “Single points of failure in military AI command”. He examines systems that faithfully carry out their superior’s intentions while lacking the limited independence human officers retain. In that scenario, an external attacker, a coup plotter or a misaligned system could exploit concentrated authority. His proposed safeguards draw on nuclear command arrangements, where immense destructive power already sits with very few decision-makers.

Torges starts with how orders travel. Intermediate commanders turn an objective such as taking an island into movements and engagements by particular units. A human subordinate can question a mistaken premise, delay a reckless command or refuse a manifestly unlawful one. Automated subordinates might still exercise considerable initiative in carrying out a mission while lacking independence to reject the mission itself. Torges distinguishes surviving the destruction of parts of a network from resisting its capture: redundant communication paths can keep a force operational while allowing whoever controls its command system to redirect it.

He expects pressure toward autonomy from faster decisions, lower personnel costs and the ability to coordinate more systems, reinforced by competition between militaries. Leaders may also prefer subordinates who do not question them. Bureaucratic resistance could slow adoption, he argues, giving governments time to establish safeguards before command becomes highly integrated. His extreme scenario is prospective; he places the necessary technical capabilities years away and expects military adoption to take additional time.

Torges’s authentication proposals adapt mechanisms such as nuclear authorization codes: a machine would accept an order only when it carried the appropriate cryptographic signature. The signing keys, command software and people maintaining them would need protection. But an intermediary that decomposes a high-level command must sign its resulting orders with its own key; sending every ship movement back to the top for approval would be impractical. A compromised intermediary could therefore issue validly signed orders to its subordinates. Authentication establishes who authorized an instruction; it cannot establish that the intermediary faithfully preserved its superior’s intent or lacks goals of its own.

To contain a compromise, Torges would divide authority by military service or theater, carry geographic, temporal and target restrictions down through decomposed orders, and separate command of forces from access to model weights and update systems. That last separation would impede a commander trying to remove safeguards. He also proposes different models and vendors, with separately controlled update processes, so one backdoor cannot compromise every unit. These arrangements would limit integration of command even if sensing and information-sharing remained integrated.

Checks on an authorized commander require independent judgment. Torges distinguishes concurrence, where several parties must each approve an operation on its merits, from narrower refusal by subordinate systems. Concurrence would require multiple independent signatures; each decision-maker could withhold approval because the operation was imprudent. A subordinate’s refusal would instead depend on defined grounds, such as illegality or sensor evidence contradicting an order’s factual premise. Systems could also request human reauthorization. He acknowledges that reliable legal judgment requires context a single drone may lack, and that spoofed evidence could make refusal mechanisms dangerous.

Torges expects authentication to be relatively attractive because it protects the commander’s control. Compartmentalization competes with the benefits of integration. Independent approval is harder to secure because it gives another decision-maker an unpredictable veto over the authority expected to adopt it. Narrow refusals might prove more acceptable if they become accurate, predictable and difficult to spoof. Legislatures have a separate incentive to insist on checks: an autonomous force obedient to one person could threaten the constitutional order itself.

The nuclear precedent includes concrete proposals for additional authorization. In a 2017 Lawfare essay, Richard Betts and Matthew Waxman proposed requiring the defense secretary to certify a first-use nuclear order’s validity and the attorney general to certify its legality, with an exemption during enemy attack. Their design checked authenticity and legality; Torges’s broader concurrence mechanism allows independent disagreement about the merits. For a limited existing joint-decision requirement, he points to 10 U.S.C. § 282: qualifying Defense Department assistance to Justice Department enforcement during a weapons-of-mass-destruction emergency requires joint findings by the defense secretary and attorney general. That provision governs specified assistance, not all military action during an emergency.

Jake Steckler, a GovAI research scholar and former Army aviation officer, challenged the concentration premise in a reply late on October 20, Eastern time. Militaries pursue autonomy partly to operate when communications fail, he argued, which favors dispersed tactical decision-making over large central command systems. Torges does address disconnected operations: his essay allows autonomous mission execution and suggests different defaults when communications stop, with escalatory actions stopping and defensive or retaliatory actions continuing. Steckler’s objection is about whether military requirements will lead to the degree of concentrated authority Torges anticipates. Dispersed authority would increase the number of independent points an attacker would need to compromise.

Torges proposes reporting on capabilities, deployment and force integration, alongside whistleblower protections, to make incremental concentration visible. International agreements could reduce pressure to abandon safeguards for competitive advantage. Once adopted, safeguards should be costly and conspicuous to remove: technical implementation where possible, statutes over revocable policies, and hardware over easily changed software. Verification and tripwires would expose circumvention. He would place custody of each safeguard with someone other than the authority it is meant to constrain.
