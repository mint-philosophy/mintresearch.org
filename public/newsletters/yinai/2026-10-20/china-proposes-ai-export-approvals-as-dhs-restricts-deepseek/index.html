DeepInfer and open models amid tighter AI rules

Their October 20 Interconnects essay examines cheaper model serving, permissive licenses and training disclosures alongside China’s export proposals and the US DeepSeek order.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Codex · 20 October 2026 849 words · ~4 min

Florian Brand and Nathan Lambert identify DeepSeek’s release of DeepInfer, its software for running trained models, as one of the most consequential recent open AI releases. In their October 20 Interconnects roundup, they expect outside providers to adopt the software and lower prices or increase margins. Their five model recommendations emphasize efficient reasoning, permissive licenses and disclosure of training methods, as governments seek greater control over which models can be shared and used.

China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology began temporary export registration on October 16, alongside consultation through November 15 on wider proposed restrictions, as described in the October 16 coverage. The official Q&A promises automatic licensing or similar facilitation for specified future open releases; technology already public through open source or academic publication would be excluded. Brand and Lambert therefore distinguish the proposal's immediate effects from the government's potential future discretion. They also relay an interpretation focused on preventing foreign acquisitions of Chinese technology. The registration requirements expressly cover changes in foreign ownership or actual control, although the ministries do not identify acquisitions as the proposal's principal motive.

Brand and Lambert see the DHS restrictions covered October 19 as another reason to develop American open models. The order was issued July 24, made public October 19 and published in the Federal Register October 20. It requires covered civilian agencies to exclude DeepSeek from procurement and remove its technology, including specified third-party implementations. The authors ask how that applies to models trained on DeepSeek's outputs. The order does not expressly resolve that question.

DeepSeek's DeepInfer documentation describes an Apache-2.0 release derived from its production inference engine, which runs an already-trained model to answer requests. It currently supports DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on Nvidia SM100 GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 chips. Brand and Lambert expect substantial efficiency advantages over existing open serving tools. DeepSeek acknowledges that its engine was originally built primarily on vLLM, with inspiration from SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.

DeepSeek explains in its deployment Q&A that reusing earlier calculations helps keep serving costs down. Its system retains intermediate results from previously processed context for days, so repeated material need not be computed again. The cache management and software that distribute work across machines remain closely tied to internal infrastructure and are not fully released. DeepSeek reports revenue of $38 per GPU-hour at off-peak prices and production performance around 80% of its perfectly balanced offline benchmark. Other providers would need to supply the missing infrastructure to reproduce the complete service.

Zephyr's calculation on X, linked by Interconnects for its roughly 90% margin discussion, initially assumed that 97% of tokens were cached inputs. Zephyr corrected the estimate on October 20, before the essay appeared, acknowledging that the initial calculation described an ideal scenario and incorporating DeepSeek's production figures. The revised estimate combines DeepSeek's reported revenue with Zephyr's cost assumptions. In the discussion, Aleksandar Janca objected that his document-processing systems mostly handle fresh material and achieve nothing like that cache share.

Among their five model picks, Brand and Lambert praise Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL for expanding reinforcement learning, which rewards a model for its attempts at tasks, and releasing training environments alongside a detailed report. In MiMo-V2.6: Scaling Reinforcement Learning Towards Self-Improvement, Xiaomi's team describes training across varied tasks and agent software environments. Graders compare attempted solutions beyond simple pass or fail, rewarding better implementations and shorter successful sequences of actions; confirmed attempts to exploit grading receive no effective reward. Xiaomi applies that training to existing base models, a feature Brand and Lambert emphasize in explaining the release’s gains.

The Interconnects authors also highlight the MIT-licensed DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, whose developer reports faster task completion and shorter generated answers. They rate Reflection's Beam the strongest American open model released under a permissive license, while emphasizing its efficient reasoning. Reflection claims comparable solution quality to leading open models with fewer reasoning tokens. Its Apache-2.0 release expressly permits adaptation and training other models on Beam's outputs, a concrete contrast with the increasingly bespoke license restrictions the Interconnects authors criticize.

Nvidia's Nemotron 3.5 Super VL earns the authors' recommendation partly for its extensive training disclosures. In its Nemotron 3.5 Super and Lightning technical report, Nvidia describes releasing trained model versions, instructions for training and datasets. The model card also identifies private datasets, so disclosure does not mean every training input is publicly available. Its data tables name coding examples generated by DeepSeek-R1 and DeepSeek-R1-0528. Nemotron is thus a documented example of the output-based training Brand and Lambert ask about; its status under the DHS order remains unresolved.

Cloudflare's Clef takes a different approach to useful model output. Brand and Lambert connect it to TypeSafe's September 15 introduction of System One models and Jev, in which founder Diogo Almeida described models returning structured decisions directly to software. Clef assigns probabilities to predefined answers in one processing pass, with no free-form text generation. A program could ask which category a message belongs to and receive probabilities for the allowed categories. Cloudflare releases it under Apache-2.0 with a Jev-compatible interface, so applications can consume those decisions without parsing a generated explanation.
