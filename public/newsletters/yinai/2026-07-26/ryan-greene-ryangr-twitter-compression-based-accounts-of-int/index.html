The century of theory behind Greene's compression essay

Solomonoff's 1960s induction theory, the Chinchilla and HKUST compression results Greene builds on, a concurrent coding scheme posted twelve days earlier, and a reply thread that folded reinforcement learning into the bit accounting.

The Minty Newsroom · Edited by Fable · 26 July 2026 498 words · ~2 min

Ray Solomonoff was born in Cleveland on July 25, 1926, one hundred years to the day before Ryan Greene posted “Compression and Intelligence” in his honor. Solomonoff presented algorithmic probability at a Caltech conference in 1960 and worked it out in “A Formal Theory of Inductive Inference” (Information and Control, 1964): weigh every program that could have generated the data observed so far, penalize each by its length, and let the shortest programs dominate the prediction of what comes next. Andrey Kolmogorov reached similar ideas independently in 1965, and the complexity measure took his name; the induction theory kept Solomonoff’s, since prediction was his goal. He was among the ten invitees to the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, one of three who stayed the whole summer.

Greene’s empirical case that language models approximate this picture rests on two papers. In “Language Modeling Is Compression” (2023), Grégoire Delétang, Marcus Hutter, Joel Veness and colleagues showed that Chinchilla 70B, trained only on text, compresses ImageNet patches to 43.4 percent of their raw size against PNG’s 58.5, and LibriSpeech audio to 16.4 percent against FLAC’s 30.3. In “Compression Represents Intelligence Linearly” (COLM 2024), Yuzhen Huang and colleagues at HKUST measured 31 open-weight models on 12 benchmarks and found average scores track compression of fresh text almost linearly. Greene reads their Qwen outlier, a series that beat its compressive weight on math benchmarks, as a warning that benchmark scores can be bought with proximate training data while broad-corpus loss resists gaming; held-out Witness tests just found the same gap in Opus 5’s ARC-AGI-3 gains. His related-works survey traces the thesis inside frontier labs to Jack Rae’s 2023 “Compression for AGI” talk and Ilya Sutskever’s account of SGD as a compressor.

The essay closes on data efficiency, arguing further gains must come from learners that extract more from each token, and leans on Dwarkesh Patel’s June 19 essay “The Data Black Hole at the Center of AI”: humans absorb roughly 200 million tokens of language by adulthood while frontier models train on tens of trillions, and teenagers learn to drive in about 20 hours against the billions of driving hours behind Waymo and Tesla. On July 13, Shikai Qiu, Marc Finzi, Yujia Zheng, Kun Zhang and Andrew Gordon Wilson posted “Requential Coding”, concurrent work Greene cites, in which a teacher selects training samples from the student’s own distribution and the code records only the selections, yielding descriptions they report as orders of magnitude shorter than prequential ones.

In the replies, Lunjun Zhang asked how reinforcement learning fits a compression account of intelligence. Greene answered that in theory Hutter’s AIXI extends Solomonoff induction to action, “the ideal agent is an expectimax-wrapper of the ideal predictor”, while in practice RL may only surface solutions the pretrained weights already contain. Nathan Delisle added that the KL penalty in RLHF works as “a budget on how many bits RL can change about the base model”, and that verifiable-reward RL feeds the model one bit per rollout.
